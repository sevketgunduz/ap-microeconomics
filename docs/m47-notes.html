The headline. Module 47 finishes Topic 2.3, and it finishes it well. The benchmark of 1 (MKT-3.E.3), the determinants (MKT-3.E.4), the effect of a price change on total revenue (MKT-3.E.5) and the variation of elasticity along a linear demand curve (MKT-3.E.2) are all here, with a figure for each. This is the best-aligned module in the elasticity run. Two gaps are vocabulary, and both run the same way: the framework's word is missing, not the idea. MKT-3.E.5 says "total revenue or total expenditure", and total expenditure occurs 0 times in this module and twice in all 927 pages — both in a macroeconomic context 360 pages earlier. The CED's own sample free-response question asks whether consumer spending would rise (Exam Information p. 133), which is the buyer's name for the same number. And MKT-3.E.2's "slope is not elasticity" is never stated: the phrase occurs 0 times in the book, and Krugman's own review question asks for the slope of a perfectly inelastic demand curve. One thing runs the other way, and it matters more. Perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic occur 0 times in the Micro CED — and 33 times across the nine released College Board papers (18 and 15). The framework omits them; the exam leans on them. Krugman opens the module with both and draws them. Teach them as examinable and do not be misled by their absence from the knowledge statements.

The essential knowledge statements this module delivers, verbatim:

MKT-3.E.2 (CED p. 49) — "… Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve, meaning slope is not elasticity."
MKT-3.E.3 (CED p. 50) — "Ranges of values of elasticity of demand are described as elastic or inelastic with the separating benchmark being a magnitude of 1, where the change in the price and the change in the quantity demanded are proportional. a. When the magnitude of the value of elasticity is greater than 1, the demand is described as being elastic with respect to that price in the range of the given change. b. When the magnitude of the value of elasticity is less than 1, the demand is described as being inelastic with respect to that price in the range of the given change. c. When the magnitude of the value of elasticity is equal to 1, the demand is described as being unit elastic with respect to that price in the range of the given change."
MKT-3.E.4 (CED p. 50) — "The price elasticity of demand depends on certain factors such as the availability of substitutes."
MKT-3.E.5 (CED p. 50) — "The impact of a given price change on total revenue or total expenditure will depend on whether demand is elastic, inelastic, or unit elastic."

Read MKT-3.E.3 closely with the class. Twice it says "with respect to that price in the range of the given change". The framework builds the next idea — that elasticity varies along the curve — into the definition itself.

Krugman's four objectives (p. 466):

  1. The difference between elastic and inelastic demand
  2. The relationship between elasticity and total revenue
  3. Changes in the price elasticity of demand along a demand curve
  4. The factors that determine price elasticity of demand

Objective 1 is MKT-3.E.3; objective 2 is MKT-3.E.5; objective 3 is MKT-3.E.2; objective 4 is MKT-3.E.4. The match is one-to-one, which is unusual in this course and worth saying so.

The margin definitions, in Krugman's own words

Perfectly inelastic demand (p. 466) — "Demand is perfectly inelastic when the quantity demanded does not respond at all to changes in the price. When demand is perfectly inelastic, the demand curve is a vertical line."
Perfectly elastic demand (p. 467) — "Demand is perfectly elastic when any price increase will cause the quantity demanded to drop to zero. When demand is perfectly elastic, the demand curve is a horizontal line."
Elastic, inelastic, unit-elastic (p. 467) — "Demand is elastic if the price elasticity of demand is greater than 1, inelastic if the price elasticity of demand is less than 1, and unit-elastic if the price elasticity of demand is exactly 1."
Total revenue (p. 468) — "Total revenue is the total value of sales of a good or service. It is equal to the price multiplied by the quantity sold."

The total-expenditure gap is one sentence to close and worth closing properly. Total revenue and total expenditure are the same money counted from opposite ends of the transaction: price × quantity is simultaneously what sellers receive and what buyers spend. Students who learn the seller's version only will read the CED's own sample question — about consumer spending on potatoes — as a different problem.

The slope gap is harder and more interesting. Krugman's Figure 47.4 uses a straight line whose slope never changes while its elasticity runs from infinite to zero. That figure is the proof; the module simply never says so. Worse, his own MCQ 3 and FRQ 1 ask for the slope of a perfectly inelastic demand curve and his rubric answers "infinite or undefined" — a legitimate question about a vertical line, but one that invites the confusion MKT-3.E.2 exists to prevent. Say the sentence out loud, write it on the board, and use Figure 47.4 as the evidence.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Slope is not elasticity

The headline correction of this module. A straight-line demand curve has one slope and every elasticity: infinite at the top, 1 at the midpoint, zero at the bottom. Figure 47.4 is the proof — the same line, total revenue rising then falling.

A second, quieter version of the same error: students compare two demand curves on one diagram and conclude the flatter one is "more elastic". Over a shared price range that is usually true, but it is not what elasticity means, and it stops being true as soon as the curves are drawn on different scales.

A perfectly inelastic demand curve has an undefined slope, not a zero elasticity and a zero slope

Krugman's MCQ 3 puts exactly this trap in option (a). A vertical line has elasticity 0 and slope undefined (or infinite). A horizontal line has elasticity infinite and slope 0. The two numbers move in opposite directions, which is the cleanest demonstration available that they are different quantities.

Elasticity is a property of a price range, not of a good

MKT-3.E.3 says it three times — "with respect to that price in the range of the given change". When an economist says the price elasticity of demand for coffee is 0.25, they mean at the current price. Students who write "coffee is inelastic" as a permanent fact will mis-answer any question that moves the price a long way.

Total revenue and total expenditure are the same number

Price × quantity. The seller calls it revenue; the buyer calls it spending; the framework names both. If a question asks what happens to consumer spending when a price rises, it is asking the MKT-3.E.5 question and the answer is decided by the elasticity.

"Inelastic" does not mean "no response"

It means the response is proportionally smaller than the price change. Only perfectly inelastic means no response at all. A student who treats 0.5 as "quantity doesn't change" will get the revenue direction right for the wrong reason, and will get Module 48's supply questions wrong.

Raising the price does not raise revenue "because the price is higher"

That is the price effect alone. There is always a quantity effect pulling the other way, and the elasticity is what says which wins. Insist on both halves in any revenue answer — it is how MKT-3.E.5 is marked.

The four determinants are not equally official

The framework names one: "certain factors such as the availability of substitutes" (MKT-3.E.4). Luxury, necessity and share of income occur 0 times in the Micro CED, and time appears only in other contexts. Teach all four — they are the standard list and released items use them — but if a question asks what the framework says determines elasticity, substitutes is the safe answer.

6. What this module does not give you for Unit 2

  • Any elasticity other than the price elasticity of demand. Supply, income and cross-price are

Module 48 and Topics 2.4 and 2.5.

  • The algebra of point elasticity. The course does not need it, and Krugman does not give it. The

midpoint method of Module 46 is as far as the arithmetic goes.

  • Tax incidence. The relative-elasticity argument that decides who pays a tax uses this module's

extremes but belongs to Topic 2.8 and Module 50.

  • The firm's use of all this. Why a monopolist never operates on the inelastic part of its demand

curve is Unit 4. It is the single best application of MKT-3.E.5, and it is 150 pages away.
