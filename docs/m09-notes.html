The headline. Module 9 is the better-aligned half of Topic 2.8. It is where Krugman first defines and draws a deadweight loss (Figure 9.2, p. 90; margin definition p. 92), the idea POL-1.A.5 makes examinable and Module 8 never names. What it still leaves out is the same word Module 8 leaves out — allocative occurs 0 times in the whole book — and the surplus accounting: consumer surplus and producer surplus occur 0 times in pp. 88–93, so the book never says who loses the $16 million of quota rent and the $2 million of deadweight loss. Two cautions run the other way. Krugman's four labels for the diagram — demand price, supply price, wedge, quota rent — occur 0 times each in the CED as labels for this idea, and 0 times on every released paper in the project. And the CED's one exclusion about quotas — "The graphing of quotas is beyond the scope of the course and the AP Exam" — sits under Topic 2.9's trade quotas, not here. Teach the diagram; do not over-drill the vocabulary.

The enduring understanding, verbatim (CED p. 55):

POL-1 — "Government policies influence consumer and producer behavior and therefore affect market outcomes."

Krugman's three objectives (p. 88):

  1. The meaning of quantity controls, another way government intervenes in markets
  2. How quantity controls create problems and can make a market inefficient
  3. Who benefits and who loses from quantity controls, and why they are used despite their well-known

problems

Objective 1 is POL-1.A.1 and LO POL-1.A(a); objective 2 is POL-1.A.4 and POL-1.A.5; objective 3 is LO POL-1.A(b) — answered in the book only as "who gets the quota rent", which is why the surplus split has to be supplied.

Verdict on alignment. Good. Six pages deliver the definition, the binding condition, the diagram, the deadweight loss and its cause, all on one exactly linear schedule. The gaps are the word allocative and the consumer/producer split — both short to supply, both done in the guide. The module's own vocabulary outruns the framework's: keep demand price, supply price and quota rent as working tools for reading the diagram, and treat wedge and the medallion market as background.

Learning objective POL-1.A (CED p. 55):

a. Define forms of government price and quantity intervention. b. Explain (using graphs where appropriate) how government policies alter consumer and producer behaviors that influence incentives and therefore affect outcomes. c. Calculate (using data from a graph or table where appropriate) changes in market outcomes resulting from government policies.
POL-1.A.1 — "Some government policies, such as price floors, price ceilings, and other forms of price and quantity regulation, affect incentives and outcomes in all market structures." (CED p. 55)
POL-1.A.4 — "Government intervention in a market producing the efficient quantity through taxes, subsidies, price controls, or quantity controls can only decrease allocative efficiency." (CED p. 55)
POL-1.A.5 — "Deadweight loss represents the losses to buyers and sellers as a result of government intervention in an efficient market." (CED p. 55)

Topic 2.8 never uses the word quota. Its statements say "quantity regulation" (A.1), "quantity intervention" (LO a) and "quantity controls" (A.4). All ten of the Micro CED's quota hits are in Topic 2.9 and its appendix entries (pp. 56, 159, 167), about trade quotas.

The exclusion, and what it covers

Topic 2.9 carries this, under POL-1.B.3 (CED p. 56, repeated on Appendix pp. 159 and 167):

POL-1.B.3 — "Quotas can be used to alter quantities produced and therefore affect price, consumer surplus, and total economic surplus." Exclusion: "The graphing of quotas is beyond the scope of the course and the AP Exam, but understanding how quotas affect quantities produced is within the scope."

Read it for what it is attached to: a trade quota in Topic 2.9, where the graph would be a world-price diagram. Topic 2.8 has no exclusion, its LO (b) asks for graphs of "quantity intervention", and POL-1.A.4 names quantity controls. So Figure 9.2 is in scope as a way to explain and calculate. But the sentence says "quotas", not "import quotas", and the released papers agree with a cautious reading: no paper in the project draws a quota. Teach the diagram; expect quota questions in words and numbers, and keep the drawing practice for price controls.

The same pattern shows up in POL-1.B.3's own wording, which is the guide's entry 9 in substance: a quota affects price, consumer surplus and total economic surplus. Krugman's Module 9 says nothing about consumer surplus.

2024 Q23 is the one released item about quotas, and it tests the quota rent without naming it: "A tariff will generate government revenue, while a quota will not" is the key (B, per ap_micro_2024_mcq_solutions.md). The reason is Module 9's: under a quota the gap between what buyers pay and what sellers need goes to whoever holds the licenses, not to the government — unless the licenses are sold. It is a Topic 2.9 item, and the test's question 18 asks the same thing of the taxi market.

The surplus split is short to supply and worth it. On the taxi numbers, riders lose $9 million of consumer surplus: $8 million becomes quota rent and $1 million is lost. Drivers lose the same $9 million: $8 million to the license holders and $1 million lost. So the quota rent of $16 million is a transfer from both sides, and the deadweight loss of $2 million is the part nobody gets. That one paragraph turns Krugman's "a loss that is experienced by both disappointed would-be riders and frustrated would-be drivers" (p. 92) into POL-1.A.5's "losses to buyers and sellers".

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Riders pay the demand price, not the supply price

Students find the quota line, see two points on it, and pick the lower one. Buyers are on the demand curve: the price they pay is the demand price of the quota quantity, $6, which is above equilibrium. Sellers are on the supply curve and receive $4, below it. A quota raises the price to buyers — like a floor, and unlike a ceiling — while sellers end up worse off, unlike a floor.

A quota creates neither a shortage nor a surplus at the price buyers pay

At $6 riders want exactly 8 million rides, the number allowed. The market clears at that price; that is what the rise in the fare does. Krugman's MCQ 4 option III (a surplus) is the floor answer carried across. His own p. 93 talks about "the shortage of taxis" in New York — that is the real city, where fares are also regulated, not his model, in which the fare rises to the demand price.

Quota rent is not deadweight loss

The rent is the rectangle — $2 × 8 million = $16 million — and someone receives it: the license holders. The deadweight loss is the triangle — $2 million — and nobody receives it. Students who learned "the inefficiency is the shaded area" shade both and call it all loss.

The rent goes to the license holder, and to the government only if it sells the licenses

New York issued medallions for $10 in the 1930s; the rent has gone to their owners ever since. A government that auctioned the licenses would collect the rent instead — which changes who gets the $16 million, not the fare, the quantity or the deadweight loss. This is the logic of 2024 Q23, where a tariff raises revenue and a quota does not.

"Using your own license is free" — it is not

Krugman's Sunil (p. 91): a medallion holder who drives his own cab earns $4 for driving and $2 he could have earned by renting the medallion out. The rent is an opportunity cost even when no money changes hands — Module 1's idea, met again.

Teach as a pair, never singly.

  • Demand price / supply price — what buyers pay against what sellers need, at the same quantity
  • Binding / non-binding — below the equilibrium quantity against above it
  • Quota rent / deadweight loss — a rectangle someone receives against a triangle nobody does
  • Quota / price control — fix the quantity and let the price adjust, against the reverse
  • License holder / driver — the same person can be both, and still earns the rent separately
