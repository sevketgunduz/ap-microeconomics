The Enduring Understanding is where the two courses part company, quoted verbatim (CED p. 36):

MKT-1 — "Most resources are scarce, and in most cases the use of resources involves constraints and trade-offs."

Compare the Macro framing of the identical five essential-knowledge statements, which sit under MOD-1 there: "The production possibilities curve (PPC) model is used to demonstrate the full employment level of output and to illustrate changes in full employment." Same diagram, same five EKs, two different reasons for teaching it. In Macro the PPC is a model of capacity and employment, which is why it returns there as long-run aggregate supply. In Micro it is a model of scarcity, trade-offs and allocation — and it returns here as the constraint behind every production and consumption decision in the rest of the course.

That is not a cosmetic difference. It decides what a point inside the curve is called, and it decides whether the module's last concept is examinable.

The learning objective MKT-1.C has three verbs, and part (c) is the one that gets skipped:

a. Define (using graphs as appropriate) the production possibilities curve (PPC) and related terms. b. Explain (using graphs as appropriate) how the production possibilities curve (PPC) illustrates opportunity costs, trade-offs, inefficiency, efficiency, and economic growth or contraction under various conditions. c. Calculate (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) opportunity cost.

Three things to notice in that wording.

MKT-1.C.2 says underutilized resources, and it is the only word the CED offers. It does not say unemployment, and unemployment occurs 0 times in the entire Micro CED against 103 in the Macro one. Krugman's p. 18 passage — which is excellent economics — uses the macro vocabulary because the same chapter serves both courses. Translate it, do not delete it.

MKT-1.C.3 names all three shapes, including decreasing. The framework is explicit and the book stops at two. See section 3.

MKT-1.C(c) allows tables as well as curves. Every calculation in the module is read off a graph. A table is arithmetically easier and is at least as likely on the exam. Module 4 (book p. 25) supplies tables; write one of your own for this module rather than waiting.

Note the CED's own spelling: the Micro framework writes "trade-offs" hyphenated in MKT-1.C.1, where the Macro framework writes "tradeoffs". Immaterial to marking; worth knowing if you grep the extract.

3. Required elements this module does not contain

Two of the five essential knowledge statements are not fully served by the text, and both gaps are verified by full-text search over all 927 pages.

MKT-1.C.3 — decreasing opportunity cost. The phrase "decreasing opportunity cost" appears 0 times in the book. No bowed-inward curve appears in the module, in its figures, or in its AP Review. Krugman argues on p. 20 that increasing costs are the realistic case and stops there. MKT-1.C.3 names all three. Supply the third — a figure and one sentence is enough, and the single test that covers all three cases is moving right along the curve, does it get steeper, stay straight, or get flatter?

MKT-1.C.2 — contraction. The word "contraction" appears 0 times in Module 3. The idea gets exactly one sentence on p. 21 — "If an economy's PPC shifts inward, the economy has become smaller" — and no figure, against a full section plus Figure 3.3 for growth. MKT-1.C.2 names growth or contraction in the same breath, and part (b) of the learning objective repeats the pair. Supply an inward-shift figure and one worked case.

A third gap that is not a missing concept but a missing format. LO MKT-1.C(c) permits data "from PPCs or tables as appropriate", and the module contains no production-possibilities table at all. Students who have only ever read a slope off a graph will stall on a table. One five-row table fixes it.

One thing to mark up rather than down. Krugman's efficient in allocation (p. 18) is background in the Macro course and examinable here (PRD-3.A.5, Topic 3.7). Note also that the word allocative occurs 0 times in the book — Krugman only ever writes "efficient in allocation", so students who search the index for the exam's vocabulary will not find it. Give them both phrasings.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

The Macro reading is reversed here

If you or your students have come from AP Macroeconomics, the instruction there was: the curve represents full-employment output, and a point inside it may be called unemployment, because MOD-1 says so and Krugman's p. 18 passage is the framework's own reasoning.

In AP Microeconomics:

  • EU MKT-1 frames the curve by scarcity and trade-offs, not by employment.
  • MKT-1.C.2 gives the examinable term: underutilized resources.
  • Unemployment occurs 0 times in the Micro CED, and recession is likewise absent.

Say it out loud, in one sentence: "Inside the curve means resources are sitting idle. In this course we call that underutilized resources, and the test is that you could get more of one good without giving up any of the other."

That last clause matters more than the vocabulary. The arithmetic test — a free gain is available — is what an exam question actually turns on, and it is the same in both courses.

And one correction that reverses the other way

Allocative efficiency is examinable here. The Macro notes mark it background. PRD-3.A.5 and PRD-3.A.9 make it Topic 3.7 content in this course. Teach it in this module as "efficient in production is about the quantity; efficient in allocation is about the mix", and say that the apparatus for deciding which mix — marginal benefit against marginal cost — arrives in Unit 3.

But do not let students claim it from a PPC. The diagram contains no information about what people want, so a labelled point on a curve can never be shown to be allocatively efficient. On a PPC question, efficient means on the curve.

Still worth correcting

"Bowed outward" is not "shifted outward." Bowed is a shape — the pattern of opportunity costs. Shifted is a movement — the economy's capacity changed. Both phrases contain the word outward and they are unrelated. This is the most common wording error in the module, and the shift-against-movement distinction it protects is the single most-tested idea in Unit 2.

The slope is the opportunity cost, and it needs units. Krugman states it once, almost in passing (p. 19): the slope of a straight-line PPC equals the opportunity cost of the horizontal good in terms of the vertical good. Insist on "one fish costs ¾ of a coconut" rather than a bare 0.75, and always compute the reciprocal too. MKT-1.C(c) is a calculate verb and unit-free answers lose marks.

A point outside is not impossible forever. It is unattainable now. Growth is precisely the event that makes it attainable — and in Topic 1.4, trade is the other one. MKT-2.B.1 has consumption going "beyond the PPC" through specialisation, which sounds like a contradiction to a student who learned "outside is impossible" as a slogan rather than as a statement about production.

Two further edges run outside the module: opportunity cost (Module 1) before anything in this one, and efficient in production before efficient in allocation, whose home is Topic 3.7.

Teach the three shapes together, in one sitting. Constant, increasing and decreasing are one comparison, not three lessons, and the whole skill reduces to a single question: moving right along the curve, does it get steeper, stay straight, or get flatter?

Teach as a pair, never singly. Each of these is defined by its contrast:

  • Inside / outside the curve — wasteful but possible against impossible; the most-missed pair
  • Bowed outward / shifted outward — a shape against a movement
  • Growth / contraction — the same mechanism in opposite directions
  • Movement along / shift of — a choice about the mix against a change in capacity
  • Efficient in production / efficient in allocation — the quantity against the mix; both
