1. A competitive market generates $36,000 of consumer surplus and $24,000 of producer surplus.

What is total economic surplus, and what does it represent?

  • (A) $12,000 — the gap between the two sides' gains.
  • (B) $60,000 — the whole net gain to buyers and sellers from trading in this market.
  • (C) $36,000, because consumer surplus is what markets exist to create.
  • (D) $60,000, which is the market's total revenue.
  • (E) It cannot be found without the equilibrium price.

2. A student writes: "The competitive equilibrium is efficient, so it must give consumers as much consumer surplus as possible."

What is wrong?

  • (A) Nothing — efficiency means consumers do as well as possible.
  • (B) Consumer surplus is not maximised at any price.
  • (C) Efficiency refers to producer surplus rather than consumer surplus.
  • (D) Efficiency means total surplus is maximised, not either half. A lower price would give consumers more consumer surplus — it would just take more from producers than it gave to buyers.
  • (E) It is wrong because efficiency is about fairness, not about surplus.

3. In a used-textbook market, a book is taken from a buyer who values it at $35 and given instead to a buyer who values it at $25.

What happens to total surplus, and what does the result show?

  • (A) It falls by $10, and the result shows that the market equilibrium already puts goods in the hands of the buyers who value them most — one of the three reallocations that cannot improve on it.
  • (B) It rises by $10, because a second buyer now gets a book.
  • (C) It is unchanged, because the same number of books is traded.
  • (D) It falls by $35, the whole of the first buyer's valuation.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without the price.

4. A market is in competitive equilibrium with no externalities. The government introduces a per-unit tax.

What happens to allocative efficiency, and why?

  • (A) It improves, because the government now has revenue to spend.
  • (B) It is unaffected, because the tax is a transfer rather than a loss.
  • (C) It falls. At the equilibrium the last unit was worth exactly what it cost to produce, so resources were already allocated to what society valued most. Any intervention that changes the quantity — a tax, a subsidy, a price control or a quota — can only move the market away from that point.
  • (D) It falls only if the tax is large enough to eliminate trading entirely.
  • (E) It improves if demand is inelastic, because little trade is lost.

5. A government taxes a competitive market and uses the revenue to support low-income households.

What can economics say about this policy?

  • (A) That it is a good policy, because it helps those in need.
  • (B) That it is a bad policy, because it reduces efficiency.
  • (C) That it has no effect on total surplus, since the money is returned to the economy.
  • (D) That equity and efficiency always move together, so the policy improves both.
  • (E) That the tax reduces total surplus and the transfer may improve equity — and that weighing one against the other is a judgement, not a calculation. There is often a trade-off between the two.

6. Which of the following is a per-unit (excise) tax?

  • (A) A tax of 20% of the selling price of each item.
  • (B) A duty of 58p on every litre of fuel sold.
  • (C) An annual licence fee of £150 paid by each shop.
  • (D) A tax of 40% on a company's profits.
  • (E) A one-off charge of £5,000 for planning permission.

7. A $12 per-unit tax is imposed on the sellers of a good.

What happens to the supply curve?

  • (A) It shifts up by $12, measured vertically at every quantity: sellers now need $12 more per unit to supply the same quantity as before.
  • (B) It becomes steeper, pivoting at the intercept.
  • (C) It shifts down by $12, because sellers keep less.
  • (D) It shifts right by 12 units.
  • (E) It does not move; the demand curve shifts instead.

8. The same per-unit tax is imposed first on sellers, and then — in a second scenario — on buyers instead.

What happens to the quantity traded, the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive?

  • (A) The outcome depends on which curve is steeper, and therefore differs.
  • (B) The quantity is the same but the two prices swap.
  • (C) Buyers bear the whole tax in the second scenario and none of it in the first.
  • (D) All three are identical. One diagram shifts supply up and the other shifts demand down, but both open the same wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers receive, so the market settles in the same place.
  • (E) All three are different, because the two taxes work through different curves.

9. After a $7 per-unit tax, buyers pay $31 per unit.

What do sellers receive, and what was the price before the tax?

  • (A) $38 received; the pre-tax price was $31.
  • (B) $24 received; the pre-tax price was $31.
  • (C) It cannot be determined without the elasticities.
  • (D) $24 received; the pre-tax price was $27.50, halfway between.
  • (E) Sellers receive $24 — the wedge between the two prices is exactly the tax. The pre-tax price was somewhere between $24 and $31, and the question does not give enough to pin it down.

10. A minister announces: "This new per-unit tax is levied on companies, not on families, so households will not pay it."

How should the claim be assessed?

  • (A) It is correct: the law determines who bears a tax.
  • (B) It is correct provided the companies are profitable.
  • (C) It describes the paperwork, not the economics. Who writes the cheque has no bearing on who bears the tax: part of it will show up in a higher price to households, and how much depends on the elasticities of supply and demand.
  • (D) It is wrong because taxes on companies are illegal in competitive markets.
  • (E) It is wrong because companies always pass on the whole of a tax.

11. A per-unit tax is imposed on a good with very inelastic demand and very elastic supply.

Who bears most of the tax, and why?

  • (A) Sellers, because the tax is collected from them.
  • (B) The two sides split it evenly, as always.
  • (C) Sellers, because elastic supply means they can produce more.
  • (D) Buyers. The inelastic side cannot escape — buyers keep buying almost the same quantity whatever the price — while elastic sellers would move resources elsewhere rather than absorb the tax.
  • (E) It cannot be said without knowing the size of the tax.

12. Before a $9 per-unit tax a good sells for $40. After the tax, buyers pay $47.

How is the tax shared, and what does that imply about the two elasticities?

  • (A) Buyers $9, sellers $0; supply must be perfectly inelastic.
  • (B) Buyers $7, sellers $2 — sellers now receive $38. Buyers bear more, so demand is the more inelastic side of this market.
  • (C) Buyers $4.50, sellers $4.50; the two sides always split a tax evenly.
  • (D) Buyers $2, sellers $7; demand is the more elastic side.
  • (E) It cannot be worked out without the quantities.

13. A $4 per-unit tax reduces the quantity traded from 9,000 units to 7,000.

How much revenue does the government collect?

  • (A) $28,000
  • (B) $36,000
  • (C) $8,000
  • (D) $64,000
  • (E) $2,000

14. In Potterville's hotel market a $40 tax raises $200,000 a night. The city doubles down and sets the tax at $60, which raises only $150,000.

What explains it, and what has happened to the deadweight loss?

  • (A) The figures must be wrong; a higher rate always raises more.
  • (B) Hotels evaded the higher tax, which is an administrative cost rather than an economic effect.
  • (C) The quantity traded fell faster than the rate rose — from 5,000 rooms to 2,500 — so revenue fell. Meanwhile the deadweight loss more than doubled, from $100,000 to $225,000: the triangle grows with both the wedge and the lost quantity.
  • (D) Demand became more elastic because of the tax.
  • (E) The extra $20 was absorbed entirely by sellers, so no revenue was gained.

15. An $8 per-unit tax reduces the quantity traded from 5,000 units to 3,500.

What is the deadweight loss?

  • (A) $28,000
  • (B) $6,000
  • (C) $12,000
  • (D) $1,500
  • (E) $40,000

16. A student writes: "The cost of a tax to society is the money collected, because that is what buyers and sellers lose."

What is wrong with the claim?

  • (A) Nothing — the revenue is the cost of a tax.
  • (B) It is wrong because a tax has no cost at all when it is used to fund public services.
  • (C) It is wrong because buyers and sellers lose less than the revenue collected.
  • (D) It is wrong because the government's revenue is always spent inefficiently.
  • (E) The revenue is a transfer: it leaves buyers and sellers and arrives at the government, so it is not lost to society. The cost is the deadweight loss — the value of the trades that no longer happen, which nobody receives.

17. A per-unit tax reduces consumer surplus by $90,000 and producer surplus by $60,000, and raises $120,000 in revenue.

What is the deadweight loss?

  • (A) $150,000
  • (B) $120,000
  • (C) $270,000
  • (D) $30,000
  • (E) It cannot be found without the tax rate

18. A per-unit tax is imposed in a market where supply is perfectly inelastic — the quantity cannot change, whatever the price.

What is the deadweight loss, and who bears the tax?

  • (A) A very large deadweight loss, because supply cannot respond.
  • (B) Zero deadweight loss, and buyers bear the whole tax.
  • (C) Zero deadweight loss, because no trades are lost — and sellers bear the whole tax, since the price buyers pay does not change at all.
  • (D) The deadweight loss equals the revenue, since nothing is produced.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without the elasticity of demand.

19. A government pays producers a per-unit subsidy on each unit sold.

What happens to the supply curve, the price buyers pay and the quantity traded?

  • (A) Supply shifts down by the subsidy; the price buyers pay falls; the price sellers receive, including the subsidy, rises; and the quantity traded goes up — past the efficient level.
  • (B) Supply does not move; demand shifts up instead.
  • (C) Supply shifts up; buyers pay more; quantity falls.
  • (D) Supply shifts down; buyers pay less; quantity falls.
  • (E) Both curves shift, because both sides benefit.

20. Consumer surplus and producer surplus both rise when a per-unit subsidy is introduced. Yet a subsidy creates a deadweight loss.

How can both be true?

  • (A) They cannot; the claim about deadweight loss is wrong.
  • (B) The deadweight loss is the government's cost, which is not a real loss.
  • (C) The gains are temporary and disappear in the long run.
  • (D) Producer surplus actually falls once the subsidy is removed.
  • (E) Because the two surpluses rise by less than the subsidy costs the taxpayer. The extra units produced cost more to make than buyers value them at, and that shortfall — the value destroyed on the units beyond the efficient quantity — is the deadweight loss.

21. A $3 per-unit subsidy raises the quantity traded from 20,000 units to 26,000.

What does the subsidy cost the government?

  • (A) $60,000
  • (B) $78,000
  • (C) $18,000
  • (D) $6,000
  • (E) $138,000

22. Alongside the deadweight loss, a tax imposes administrative costs.

What are they, and where do they appear on the diagram?

  • (A) The tax revenue itself, shown as the rectangle.
  • (B) The loss of consumer surplus, shown as part of the triangle.
  • (C) The money the government spends on public services.
  • (D) The resources used collecting the tax and paying it — forms, accountants, inspectors, and the effort spent avoiding it. They appear nowhere on the diagram, which is why the full cost of a tax is larger than the triangle.
  • (E) The deadweight loss under another name.

23. A per-unit duty on fuel takes 3% of a low-income household's income and 0.5% of a high-income household's — although the richer household pays more in cash.

How is this tax described, and what does it tell you about its efficiency?

  • (A) Proportional, because everyone pays the same rate per litre.
  • (B) Progressive, because the richer household pays more money.
  • (C) Regressive, because it takes a smaller share of income as income rises. It says nothing at all about efficiency: how a burden is distributed and how much surplus it destroys are separate questions.
  • (D) Regressive, and therefore inefficient.
  • (E) Progressive, and therefore efficient.

24. A binding price ceiling is imposed on a competitive market, and the quantity traded falls below the equilibrium quantity.

Is there a deadweight loss, and why?

  • (A) Only if the ceiling is set below the sellers' costs.
  • (B) No, because no government revenue is collected.
  • (C) No — a price ceiling transfers surplus from sellers to buyers, and transfers are not losses.
  • (D) Yes, and it equals the transfer from sellers to buyers.
  • (E) Yes. The quantity has fallen below the efficient level, so trades that would have benefited both sides no longer happen. That lost value is a deadweight loss, exactly as with a tax — the triangle is drawn between the restricted quantity and the equilibrium quantity.

25. In Potterville's hotel market the equilibrium is $80 and 10,000 rooms. A $40 tax cuts the quantity to 5,000; a $40 subsidy raises it to 15,000.

Compare the two interventions.

  • (A) Each moves the quantity 5,000 rooms away from the efficient level and each destroys $100,000 of surplus — but the tax collects $200,000 while the subsidy costs $600,000, because the subsidy's rectangle is three times as wide.
  • (B) Both raise money for the government, but the subsidy raises more.
  • (C) Both leave total surplus unchanged, since the government's gain offsets the private loss.
  • (D) The subsidy is the better policy because it increases the quantity traded.
  • (E) The tax creates a deadweight loss and the subsidy removes one.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Total surplus · Application

Answer: (B)

$36,000 + $24,000 = $60,000. Total economic surplus is the sum of the two, and it measures the whole net gain the market creates.

  • (A) ✗ Subtracts instead of adding; the gap between the two has no standard use.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Consumer surplus is one half of the gain.
  • (D) ✗ Right number, wrong name. Total revenue is price × quantity and is unrelated to these

two figures.

  • (E) ✗ The two surpluses are all that is needed.

Question 2 — Why equilibrium maximises total surplus · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Efficiency is a claim about the total. Krugman's own review question is built on exactly this distinction.

  • (A) ✗ The error under test.
  • (B) ✗ Consumer surplus is maximised at a price of zero; it is just that the total is not.
  • (C) ✗ Neither half is what efficiency refers to.
  • (D) ✓ Correct, and it explains why a lower price would not be an improvement overall.
  • (E) ✗ Fairness is the equity question, which is a different one.

Question 3 — The three experiments · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Reallocating consumption to a buyer who values the good less reduces total surplus by the difference. It is the first of the three experiments that establish efficiency.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, with the conclusion drawn.
  • (B) ✗ No extra book exists; one has been moved, not added.
  • (C) ✗ The quantity is unchanged, but the value created is not.
  • (D) ✗ The second buyer still gains $25 of value; only the $10 difference is lost.
  • (E) ✗ The price cancels out: both buyers paid the same for the same book.

Question 4 — Allocative efficiency · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

The framework's own wording: intervention in a market producing the efficient quantity "can only decrease allocative efficiency". The reason is that the equilibrium already equates value and cost on the last unit.

  • (A) ✗ Revenue is a transfer and does not restore the lost trades.
  • (B) ✗ The revenue is a transfer; the deadweight loss is not, and it is a fall in allocative

efficiency.

  • (C) ✓ Correct, and it lists the four forms of intervention the framework names.
  • (D) ✗ Any tax that changes the quantity reduces efficiency, however small.
  • (E) ✗ Inelastic demand makes the loss smaller, not negative.

Question 5 — Equity against efficiency · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Economics can measure the efficiency cost and describe the distributional effect. Deciding whether the trade is worth making is a value judgement.

  • (A) ✗ A political conclusion, not an economic one.
  • (B) ✗ Also a political conclusion, in the other direction.
  • (C) ✗ Spending the revenue does not restore the trades the tax prevented.
  • (D) ✗ There is "often a trade-off", which is the opposite claim.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 6 — Per-unit tax · Analysis

Answer: (B)

A per-unit (excise) tax is a fixed amount on each unit sold.

  • (A) ✗ A percentage tax. It would pivot the supply curve rather than shift it.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ A fixed annual fee does not vary with units sold — it is a lump-sum tax.
  • (D) ✗ A profits tax, unrelated to units.
  • (E) ✗ A one-off charge, again independent of quantity.

Question 7 — A tax on sellers · Application

Answer: (A)

Sellers need the tax back on every unit, so the whole curve moves up by exactly the tax, measured vertically.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A per-unit tax shifts the curve in parallel. A percentage tax is what pivots it.
  • (C) ✗ Down is the subsidy case.
  • (D) ✗ The shift is vertical and measured in money, not horizontal and measured in units.
  • (E) ✗ Demand shifts when the tax is on buyers; here it is on sellers.

Question 8 — A tax on buyers · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Krugman's Figures 50.6 and 50.7 give the same prices, the same quantity and the same revenue. The diagrams differ; the outcome does not.

  • (A) ✗ The elasticities determine the split, and that split is the same in both scenarios.
  • (B) ✗ Nothing swaps: buyers pay the higher price in both.
  • (C) ✗ Who is named by the law never determines the incidence.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The diagrams differ, which is exactly why the identical answer is worth noticing.

Question 9 — The tax wedge · Application

Answer: (E)

The wedge is the tax: $31 − $7 = $24. The pre-tax price lies between the two, but where exactly depends on the elasticities, which are not given.

  • (A) ✗ Adds the tax instead of subtracting it, and assumes buyers bore none of it.
  • (B) ✗ Right on the $24, wrong on the pre-tax price: $31 is the post-tax price buyers pay.
  • (C) ✗ The elasticities are needed for the pre-tax price, not for the price sellers receive.
  • (D) ✗ A 50/50 split is only one possibility, and it requires equal elasticities.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, including what cannot be determined.

Question 10 — Who pays does not matter · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

The incidence does not depend on who officially pays. Part of the tax reaches households through a higher price, and the elasticities decide how much.

  • (A) ✗ The law decides who remits the money, not who bears it.
  • (B) ✗ Profitability is irrelevant to the incidence.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Taxes on companies are entirely legal and common.
  • (E) ✗ Overcorrects. Firms pass on part of a tax, and the whole of it only in the limiting

case of perfectly elastic supply.

Question 11 — Incidence and elasticity · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The more inelastic side bears more. Inelastic buyers cannot escape; elastic sellers can.

  • (A) ✗ Collection does not determine incidence.
  • (B) ✗ An even split happens only when the two elasticities match.
  • (C) ✗ Elastic supply means sellers can move resources away, which protects them.
  • (D) ✓ Correct, with the escape argument.
  • (E) ✗ The split is a ratio and does not depend on the size of the tax.

Question 12 — Incidence and elasticity · Application

Answer: (B)

Buyers' price rose $40 → $47, so they bear $7. The wedge is $9, so sellers receive $38 and bear $2. Buyers bearing more means demand is the more inelastic side.

  • (A) ✗ Sellers do bear $2; perfectly inelastic supply would mean buyers bore nothing.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ There is no rule that taxes split evenly.
  • (D) ✗ The split reversed, and the elasticity conclusion with it.
  • (E) ✗ The two prices are enough; quantities are not needed for the split.

Question 13 — Tax revenue · Application

Answer: (A)

$4 × 7,000 = $28,000. The width of the rectangle is the quantity that still trades.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Uses the pre-tax quantity, 9,000 — the commonest error on this topic.
  • (C) ✗ $4 × 2,000, the fall in quantity, which is the triangle's base rather than the

rectangle's width.

  • (D) ✗ $4 × 16,000, the two quantities added.
  • (E) ✗ The fall in quantity on its own.

Question 14 — Tax revenue · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Revenue is rate × quantity, and quantity falls as the rate rises. Here it halves, so revenue falls while the deadweight loss grows with both the wedge and the lost trades.

  • (A) ✗ Higher rates frequently raise less. The figures are Krugman's own market.
  • (B) ✗ Evasion is an administrative cost and is not what these numbers show.
  • (C) ✓ Correct, with both halves.
  • (D) ✗ Elasticity is a property of the curves and does not change because a tax was levied.
  • (E) ✗ The price sellers receive fell to $50, so they absorbed $30, not the whole $20 increase.

Question 15 — Deadweight loss · Application

Answer: (B)

½ × (5,000 − 3,500) × $8 = ½ × 1,500 × $8 = $6,000.

  • (A) ✗ $8 × 3,500 — the revenue rectangle, not the triangle.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ 1,500 × $8 without the ½.
  • (D) ✗ The fall in quantity, unmultiplied.
  • (E) ✗ $8 × 5,000, using the pre-tax quantity as the width.

Question 16 — Deadweight loss · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Krugman defines deadweight loss as the fall in total surplus minus the revenue, precisely because the revenue is received by somebody.

  • (A) ✗ The error under test.
  • (B) ✗ Funding useful services does not restore the trades that no longer happen.
  • (C) ✗ Buyers and sellers lose more than the revenue — the extra is the deadweight loss.
  • (D) ✗ How the revenue is spent is a separate question and not what makes the tax costly.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, naming both the transfer and the loss.

Question 17 — The surplus account · Application

Answer: (D)

Buyers and sellers lose $90,000 + $60,000 = $150,000. The government receives $120,000. The missing $30,000 is the deadweight loss.

  • (A) ✗ The total private loss, before subtracting the transfer.
  • (B) ✗ The revenue, which is the transfer rather than the loss.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the revenue to the private loss instead of subtracting it.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The account closes without the rate: losses − revenue = deadweight loss.

Question 18 — When there is no deadweight loss · Analysis

Answer: (C)

If the quantity cannot change, no trades are lost, so the triangle has no height. The entire tax comes out of the price sellers receive.

  • (A) ✗ The reverse — an unresponsive quantity means no lost trades.
  • (B) ✗ Right on the deadweight loss, wrong on the incidence: the price buyers pay does not move,

so buyers bear none of it.

  • (C) ✓ Correct, on both counts.
  • (D) ✗ Production continues at exactly the same level.
  • (E) ✗ Perfectly inelastic supply settles it whatever demand does.

Question 19 — Subsidies · Analysis

Answer: (A)

A subsidy is the tax case with the signs reversed, plus one difference: the quantity ends up above the efficient level rather than below it.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, including the overshoot.
  • (B) ✗ A subsidy to producers shifts supply; demand does not move.
  • (C) ✗ Every direction reversed.
  • (D) ✗ Right on the shift, wrong on the quantity: a subsidy raises it.
  • (E) ✗ Only one curve shifts, just as with a tax.

Question 20 — Subsidies · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Both surpluses rise, but the taxpayer funds the increase — and funds more than it is worth, because the extra units cost more to produce than buyers value them at.

  • (A) ✗ The deadweight loss is real and is on every subsidy diagram.
  • (B) ✗ The government's cost is a transfer from taxpayers; the deadweight loss is the part that

exceeds the gains.

  • (C) ✗ Nothing here depends on the time horizon.
  • (D) ✗ A claim about removing the subsidy, which the question does not ask about.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 21 — Government revenue or cost · Application

Answer: (B)

$3 × 26,000 = $78,000. Use the quantity after the subsidy, which is the larger one — the reason subsidies are expensive.

  • (A) ✗ Uses the pre-subsidy quantity, 20,000.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ $3 × 6,000, the increase in quantity.
  • (D) ✗ The increase in quantity on its own, divided by a thousand.
  • (E) ✗ $3 × 46,000, the two quantities added.

Answer: (D)

Real resources, producing nothing, and invisible on the supply-and-demand diagram.

  • (B) ✗ Lost consumer surplus is already on the diagram.
  • (C) ✗ What the revenue buys is a separate question.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.

Question 23 — Progressive and regressive · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Regressive is defined by the share of income, not the amount of money — and it is a statement about equity, not efficiency.

  • (A) ✗ The rate per litre is the same; the share of income is not, and the share is the test.
  • (B) ✗ Paying more in cash is not what progressive means.
  • (C) ✓ Correct, with the separation of the two questions.
  • (D) ✗ Regressive and inefficient are unrelated. A regressive tax can have a tiny deadweight

loss.

  • (E) ✗ Wrong classification and the same confusion of the two ideas.

Question 24 — Deadweight loss under a price control · Analysis

Answer: (E)

POL-1.A.5 is about government intervention in general, not taxes alone. Any policy that cuts the quantity below the efficient level destroys the surplus those trades would have generated.

  • (A) ✗ A ceiling below the equilibrium price binds and creates a loss long before it reaches

sellers' costs.

  • (B) ✗ Revenue has nothing to do with it; a quota collects no revenue either and still destroys

surplus.

  • (C) ✗ There is a transfer and a loss. The lost trades are not transferred to anybody.
  • (D) ✗ The transfer and the deadweight loss are different areas of the diagram and different

sizes.

  • (E) ✓ Correct, with the triangle located.

Question 25 — A tax and a subsidy side by side · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Both move the quantity 5,000 rooms from the efficient 10,000, so both destroy ½ × 5,000 × $40 = $100,000. The rectangles differ because the subsidy applies to 15,000 rooms and the tax to 5,000.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, with both the symmetry and the asymmetry.
  • (B) ✗ A subsidy costs the government money.
  • (C) ✗ The government's revenue does not offset the lost trades, and a subsidy is an outflow

anyway.

  • (D) ✗ A bigger quantity is not better once it passes the efficient level — those rooms cost

more to supply than guests value them at.

  • (E) ✗ A subsidy creates a deadweight loss of its own; it does not cancel one.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 19 concept entries appear. The tax diagram and its arithmetic take eleven items (7, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 and 24), which matches both the module's page count and the calculate verb in POL-1.A(c). The efficiency argument takes four (1 to 4) and the supplied subsidy material takes four (19, 20, 21 and 25).

Bloom levels. Application 8 · Analysis 11 · Synthesis 6, a ratio of 1 : 1.4 : 0.8 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The paper is more numerical than the standard because the module's own free-response question asks for six numbers in sequence; it is less numerical than the Module 49 paper, because half of this module is an argument rather than a measurement.

Questions 19 to 21 and 25 are the ones to discuss aloud. They are the subsidy case, which the textbook never mentions and the exam tests heavily. Students who have worked only from Krugman will not have seen a subsidy diagram at all, and the four items are designed to surface that in a single sitting.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: the pre-tax quantity used for the revenue rectangle (13B, 15E); the ½ dropped from the triangle (15C); revenue treated as the social cost of a tax (16A, 4B); the incidence read off who the law names (10A, 11A, 8C, 8E); the subsidy's directions reversed (19C, 19D); the subsidy's cost computed on the pre-subsidy quantity (21A); and regressive conflated with inefficient (23D).

Four distractors reach a right-looking conclusion by wrong reasoning, flagged in the solutions: 9B, 14E, 18B and 20B. The most useful is 18B — it gets the deadweight loss right and the incidence exactly backwards, which is the classic half-understanding of the perfectly inelastic case.

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 5 · D 5 · E 5 — exactly even, with no letter repeating in consecutive questions.
