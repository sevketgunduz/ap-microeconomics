Defining Profit

Eighteen terms for the first profit topic of Unit 3. Two ways of counting cost, two kinds of profit, and why a business with $35,000 on its books can still be better off closing.

Krugman, Module 52, pages 530–535· 18 terms·Topic 3.4·1 period

How to use this guide

Entries 1 to 6 are cost: what counts, and why using your own resources is never free. Entries 7 to 15 are the kinds of profit. Entries 16 to 18 are what firms do about profit, and how you will be asked to calculate it.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 52 does not teach it. Entries 12, 17 and 18 carry the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. Accounting profit subtracts the costs you pay. Economic profit also subtracts the costs you give up. Firms decide by economic profit — and zero economic profit, normal profit, is a perfectly good result.

⚑ Three things Module 52 leaves out. Your course names three implicit costs and your textbook teaches two: compensation for risk is missing (entry 12). Your course also asks how firms respond to profit (entry 17) and how to calculate profit from per-unit figures (entry 18); your textbook does both in later modules.

The accountant and the economist look at Babette's café

Babette takes in $100,000 a year. Her accountant subtracts the $65,000 she pays out (food, wages, rent and $5,000 of equipment wear) and reports a profit of $35,000. The economist also subtracts what Babette gives up: $3,000 her equipment would earn if sold and banked, and the salary she could earn as a chef elsewhere. Switch between the cases and watch only the economist's answer change. Every figure is in thousands of dollars.

Revenue$100k
Accounting profit$35k

Grey is the explicit cost both of them subtract, amber the implicit costs only the economist subtracts, green a profit and red a loss. The dashed line is revenue. In the third case Babette needs $2,000 a year as compensation for risk: her implicit costs reach $35,000, exactly her accounting profit, and her economic profit is zero — normal profit.

The eighteen terms

01

Profit

★ Exam
Definition

Total revenue minus total cost. The goal economists assume every firm pursues.

Textbook page 530.

Your textbook starts from one line: Profit = Total Revenue − Total Cost. Everything in this module is about the second term — which costs count. Count only the bills a business pays and you get one kind of profit; count everything its owner gives up and you get another.

Firms may have other goals too, but the model assumes they try to make profit as large as possible. The next module shows how they choose the quantity that does it.

Watch out. Profit is not the same as revenue. A business that takes in $100,000 has not earned $100,000; what it earns depends on what it gave up to take that money in.

1

Babette's café takes in $100,000. That is revenue. Profit is what remains after the costs.

2

A lemonade stand sells $40 of lemonade using $15 of lemons and cups. Profit, counting only those costs, is $25.

3

A firm whose costs exceed its revenue. Its profit is negative: a loss.

How it connects. Its first term is total revenue (entry 2); the two ways of counting the second give accounting profit (entry 7) and economic profit (entry 9).

Check yourself

1.1 A store's revenue is $500,000 and its total cost is $430,000. What is its profit?

Show answer

$500,000 − $430,000 = $70,000.

1.2 Why does the module spend so long on cost rather than revenue?

Show answer

Because revenue is simple — price times quantity — while which costs to count is what separates the two kinds of profit.

02

Total revenue

★ Exam
Definition

The price of the output times the quantity sold: TR = P × Q.

Textbook page 530.

Total revenue is the money a firm takes in from selling its output. If you sell 100 T-shirts at $10 each, total revenue is $1,000 — whatever the shirts cost you to make.

It is the same number from either side of the market: what sellers receive as revenue is what buyers pay as total expenditure.

Watch out. Total revenue is a sales figure, not a profit figure. Exam questions often give price and quantity and expect you to multiply before subtracting anything.

1

100 T-shirts at $10. TR = $1,000.

2

Sunny sells 2,000 sno cones at $2. TR = $4,000.

3

A farm sells 5 bushels at $18. TR = $90.

How it connects. It is the first term of profit (entry 1), and dividing it by quantity gives the price used in profit per unit (entry 18).

Check yourself

2.1 A firm sells 400 books at $15. What is its total revenue?

Show answer

400 × $15 = $6,000.

2.2 If price stays at $15 and the firm sells 50 more books, by how much does total revenue rise?

Show answer

50 × $15 = $750.

03

Explicit cost

★ Exam
Definition

A cost that involves actually laying out money: rent, wages, materials, bills.

Textbook page 530, Table 52.1.

Explicit costs are the ones that show up as payments. For a year at college they are tuition ($17,000), books and supplies ($1,000) and a computer ($1,500) — $19,500 in Table 52.1. For a business they are the wages it pays, the rent it pays, the ingredients it buys.

Your exam uses the phrase often. It is the half of cost that an accountant records.

Watch out. Interest the business pays on a loan is explicit — money changes hands. Interest the owner's own savings would have earned is implicit. Same word, opposite category.

1

Tuition. Explicit: you write a check for it.

2

Babette's food, wages and rent. $60,000 of explicit cost.

3

Interest paid on a bank loan. Explicit: the payment leaves the business.

How it connects. Its partner is implicit cost (entry 4); together they make the full cost of a choice (entry 5), and it is all that accounting profit (entry 7) subtracts.

Check yourself

3.1 A bakery pays $2,000 a month in rent and $1,500 for flour. Which of these are explicit costs?

Show answer

Both: each is a payment of money.

3.2 Karma and Don pay a part-time helper. Is the helper's wage explicit or implicit?

Show answer

Explicit — it is paid out in money.

04

Implicit cost

★ Exam
Definition

A cost that requires no outlay of money. It is measured by the value, in dollars, of the benefits given up.

Textbook page 530, Table 52.1.

An implicit cost is an opportunity cost that nobody sends you a bill for. A year at college costs you the $35,000 salary you could have earned — no money leaves your pocket, but you are $35,000 worse off than in your next-best choice.

Your textbook warns that ignoring implicit costs is “a common mistake, both in economic analysis and in real business situations”. They are often the larger part of the cost.

Watch out. Implicit does not mean imaginary. If the next-best use of a resource would have earned money, using it here costs that money just as surely as a bill would.

1

Forgone salary at college. $35,000, more than the explicit cost of $19,500.

2

The time you spend eating lunch. An implicit cost of going out to lunch.

3

A van the owners inherited and use for deliveries. Implicit: they could have sold it.

How it connects. It is the other half of cost (entry 3), it completes the full cost of a choice (entry 5), it is what separates economic profit (entry 9) from accounting profit, and in a business it comes as the cost of the owner's capital (entry 10).

Check yourself

4.1 A student gives up a $12-an-hour job to study for four hours. What is the implicit cost of studying?

Show answer

4 × $12 = $48.

4.2 Karma and Don use a basement workroom that nobody would ever rent. Is there an implicit cost?

Show answer

No — or rather, it is zero. An implicit cost is a benefit given up, and if the space has no other use, nothing is given up.

05

The full cost of a choice

★ Exam
Definition

The opportunity cost of an activity is its explicit cost plus its implicit cost.

Textbook page 531, Table 52.1.

Table 52.1 adds the two together for one more year of school:

  • Explicit: $17,000 + $1,000 + $1,500 = $19,500
  • Implicit: forgone salary of $35,000
  • Total opportunity cost: $54,500

Leave out the implicit part and the year looks to cost about a third of what it really costs.

Watch out. The full cost is a sum, not a choice between the two. Students sometimes report only the larger figure.

1

A year of college. $19,500 + $35,000 = $54,500.

2

Selling T-shirts for two hours. $500 of shirts plus $16 of time you could have spent at an $8-an-hour job.

3

Babette's café in Case 1. $65,000 on the books plus $37,000 of implicit cost: $102,000.

How it connects. It adds explicit cost (entry 3) to implicit cost (entry 4), and using your own resources (entry 6) explains why the second part is real.

Check yourself

5.1 A summer course costs $1,200 in fees and means giving up $3,000 of wages. What is its full cost?

Show answer

$1,200 + $3,000 = $4,200.

5.2 If the wages given up rose to $4,000, which part of the cost would change?

Show answer

Only the implicit part. The full cost would be $5,200.

06

Using your own resources

★ Exam
Definition

Using a resource you already own is still costly: it costs whatever the resource would have earned in its next-best use.

Textbook page 531.

Your textbook puts it as a rule: “in considering the cost of an activity, you should include the cost of using any of your own resources for that activity.” Your time, your savings, your building and your equipment all have other uses.

To put a number on it, ask what the resource would have earned in its next-best alternative. That is the implicit cost.

Watch out. Owning something does not make it free to use. A farmer who owns her land still gives up the rent a developer would pay for it.

1

Your time at college. Costs the salary it would have earned.

2

Farmland near a growing city. Costs what a developer would pay to use it — your textbook's New England farms.

3

Babette's own equipment. Costs the $3,000 interest it could earn if she sold it.

How it connects. It is why the full cost (entry 5) counts, and it covers both the owner's capital (entry 10) and the owner's time (entry 11).

Check yourself

6.1 Why does owning your building not make your shop's space free?

Show answer

Because you could rent the space to someone else. The rent you give up is an implicit cost.

6.2 How do you put a dollar value on using your own resource?

Show answer

Find what it would earn in its next-best alternative use.

07

Accounting profit

★ Exam
Definition

Total revenue minus explicit costs. The profit a business reports on its books and its tax forms.

Textbook pages 531 and 539.

Babette takes in $100,000. Her food, wages and rent cost $60,000, and her accountant records $5,000 of wear on her equipment. Her accounting profit is $100,000 − $60,000 − $5,000 = $35,000.

Your exam's definition is the short one: total revenue minus explicit costs. Your textbook's margin adds “and depreciation” — that is one of the costs an accountant records, so the two say the same thing (entry 8).

Watch out. Accounting profit is a useful number but the wrong one for deciding whether to stay in business. It leaves out every implicit cost.

1

Babette's café. $100,000 − $65,000 = $35,000.

2

T-shirts at a football game. $1,000 − $500 = $500.

3

Sunny's Sno Cone Stand. $4,000 − $400 − $100 = $3,500.

How it connects. It is profit (entry 1) with only explicit costs (entry 3) subtracted, it includes depreciation (entry 8), and it is never less than economic profit (entry 13).

Check yourself

7.1 A firm sells 400 books at $15 and its explicit costs are $4,500. What is its accounting profit?

Show answer

$6,000 − $4,500 = $1,500.

7.2 Which costs does accounting profit leave out?

Show answer

Every implicit cost: the owner's time, the earnings of the owner's own capital, and the reward for risk.

08

Depreciation

○ Background
Definition

The loss in value of equipment as it wears out. Accountants record it as a yearly cost.

Textbook page 531.

Babette's appliances and furniture wear out and must be replaced every few years. Her accountant spreads that cost over the years by recording $5,000 of depreciation each year.

You will not be asked about depreciation on its own. It matters only because your textbook's definition of accounting profit mentions it: treat it as one of the explicit costs on the accountant's books.

Watch out. Depreciation is not the implicit cost of capital. Depreciation is wear; the implicit cost is the income the equipment's value could have earned elsewhere. Babette has both.

1

Babette's equipment. $5,000 a year of depreciation.

2

A delivery van bought for $30,000 that lasts six years. About $5,000 a year.

3

Sunny's sno cone machine. $100 of depreciation in January.

How it connects. It is one of the costs subtracted in accounting profit (entry 7).

Check yourself

8.1 Is depreciation an explicit or an implicit cost in this course?

Show answer

Treat it as one of the explicit costs an accountant records.

8.2 Babette's equipment depreciates by $5,000 and could earn $3,000 in a bank. How much of that is the implicit cost of capital?

Show answer

$3,000. The $5,000 is depreciation, already on the accountant's books.

09

Economic profit

★ Exam
Definition

Total revenue minus explicit and implicit costs — minus the full opportunity cost of every resource the business uses.

Textbook page 532, Table 52.2.

Economic profit starts where accounting profit stops and subtracts the implicit costs as well:

Economic profit = accounting profit − implicit costs.

In Case 1, Babette's $35,000 of accounting profit loses her $3,000 implicit cost of capital and the $34,000 she could earn as a chef: −$2,000. When economists say profit without a qualifier, they mean this one.

Watch out. Economic profit can be negative while accounting profit is positive. Babette's books show $35,000 and she would still be better off closing.

1

Babette, Case 1. $35,000 − $37,000 = −$2,000.

2

Babette, Case 2. $35,000 − $33,000 = +$2,000.

3

T-shirts at a football game. $500 − $16 = $484.

How it connects. It is profit (entry 1) measured properly: it subtracts implicit costs (entry 4) as well, it is never more than accounting profit (entry 13), and when it is zero it is normal profit (entry 14).

Check yourself

9.1 A firm sells 400 books at $15, its explicit costs are $4,500 and its implicit costs $1,000. What is its economic profit?

Show answer

$6,000 − $4,500 − $1,000 = $500.

9.2 Economic profit can be found from accounting profit by subtracting what?

Show answer

Implicit costs.

10

Cost of financial capital

★ Exam
Definition

The income the owner could have earned from the money or equipment tied up in the business, in its next-best use. Your textbook calls it the implicit cost of capital.

Textbook page 532.

Babette owns her equipment. She pays no rent on it, but she could sell it for $50,000 and put the money in a bank at $3,000 a year. That $3,000 is an implicit cost of running the café.

Your exam calls this the cost of financial capital. It applies to any money an owner puts into the business: it could have earned interest somewhere else.

Watch out. If Babette rented her equipment instead, the rent would be an explicit cost. Owning it does not remove the cost; it only moves it from the explicit column to the implicit one.

1

Babette's $50,000 of equipment. $3,000 a year of interest given up.

2

$20,000 of savings put into a bike shop at 5% interest. $1,000 a year.

3

Farmland a family owns outright. The rent or development income the land could earn.

How it connects. It is one of the implicit costs (entry 4), a special case of using your own resources (entry 6), and the safe return it measures is the starting point for compensation for risk (entry 12).

Check yourself

10.1 An owner puts $80,000 of her savings into a business. A bank would pay 4%. What is the implicit cost of that capital?

Show answer

4% of $80,000 = $3,200 a year.

10.2 Why is this cost left out of accounting profit?

Show answer

Because no money is paid out for it — the owner simply gives up the interest.

11

The entrepreneur's time

★ Exam
Definition

What the owner could have earned by working elsewhere. Your exam calls it an entrepreneur's time; your textbook says the owner “devotes time and energy to the business that could have been used elsewhere”.

Textbook page 532, Table 52.2.

Babette could earn $34,000 as a chef in someone else's restaurant. Running her own costs her that salary — the largest of her implicit costs, and the one that turns her $35,000 of accounting profit into a loss.

In Case 2 a soft labor market cuts what she could earn to $30,000, and her economic profit turns positive. Nothing about the café changed; her next-best alternative did.

Watch out. The owner's time is valued at what it would earn elsewhere, not at the hours worked. Long hours do not make it larger unless the alternative pays by the hour.

1

Babette, Case 1. $34,000 as a chef elsewhere.

2

Babette, Case 2. $30,000: the alternative got worse, so the café looks better.

3

Selling T-shirts for two hours. $16, at the $8 an hour you could earn at your other job.

How it connects. It is the largest kind of own resource (entry 6) for a small business, and with compensation for risk (entry 12) it completes the framework's list.

Check yourself

11.1 An owner could earn $45,000 managing another firm's store. What implicit cost does that create for her own store?

Show answer

$45,000 a year — the salary she gives up.

11.2 Babette's café does not change, but the salary she could earn elsewhere falls. What happens to her economic profit?

Show answer

It rises by the same amount, because her implicit cost has fallen.

12

Compensation for risk

★ ⚑
Definition

The extra return an owner needs for putting money into a risky business rather than a safe one. It is an implicit cost.

Not in Module 52 — or anywhere else in your textbook.

⚑ Not in Module 52 — learn it from here. Your course lists three implicit costs: the cost of financial capital, compensation for risk, and an entrepreneur's time. Your textbook teaches the first and the third and never mentions risk.

A bank account pays its interest whatever happens. A new café might fail and lose the owner's savings. Nobody would take that risk for the same return as the bank, so part of what the owner needs to earn is a reward for bearing it.

Suppose Babette needs $2,000 a year on top of the bank's $3,000 to make the risk worthwhile. In Case 2 her implicit costs become $3,000 + $30,000 + $2,000 = $35,000, and her economic profit falls from +$2,000 to $0. Try it in the diagram above.

Watch out. Risk compensation is not an insurance premium. A premium is paid out in money, so it is explicit. Risk compensation is the return the owner gives up demanding, so it is implicit.

1

Babette with a $2,000 risk premium. Implicit costs $35,000; economic profit $0.

2

Savings in a government bond at 3% or in a new restaurant. The restaurant must earn more than 3% to be worth it.

3

A start-up with a high chance of failing. Needs a large expected return to compensate its founders.

How it connects. It sits alongside the cost of financial capital (entry 10) and the entrepreneur's time (entry 11), and once all three are covered the owner is earning normal profit (entry 14).

Check yourself

12.1 Two owners each put $100,000 to work. One buys a bond paying $3,000; the other opens a restaurant. Why is the restaurant owner's implicit cost of capital more than $3,000?

Show answer

Because the restaurant is riskier. The owner needs compensation for risk on top of the safe return.

12.2 Is compensation for risk part of accounting profit?

Show answer

No. Nobody pays it out in money, so accounting profit ignores it. Economic profit subtracts it.

13

Accounting profit is never less than economic profit

★ Exam
Definition

The two differ by the implicit costs. Implicit costs cannot be negative, so economic profit is at most equal to accounting profit.

Textbook page 532, Table 52.2.

Your textbook says economic profit is “usually less” than accounting profit. It is always less or equal: subtracting implicit costs can never raise a number. The two are equal only when there are no implicit costs at all.

Exam questions test this directly. “Accounting profit is greater than economic profit because accounting profit does not consider implicit costs” is the kind of statement to recognise as true.

Watch out. An option saying economic profit is larger than accounting profit is always wrong, however the question is dressed up.

1

Babette, Case 1. Accounting $35,000; economic −$2,000.

2

T-shirts. Accounting $500; economic $484.

3

A firm with no implicit costs. The two are equal.

How it connects. It compares accounting profit (entry 7) with economic profit (entry 9).

Check yourself

13.1 A firm reports accounting profit of $50,000. Could its economic profit be $60,000?

Show answer

No. Economic profit is accounting profit minus implicit costs, and those cannot be negative.

13.2 When are the two equal?

Show answer

Only when the firm has no implicit costs.

14

Normal profit

★ Exam
Definition

An economic profit of zero. Every cost, explicit and implicit, is just covered, so the resources could not do better anywhere else.

Textbook pages 533–534.

Zero sounds like failure. It is not: an owner earning normal profit is earning exactly what her money, time and risk would earn in their best alternative. Your textbook: a firm earning normal profit “could not do any better using its resources in any alternative activity”.

Two things follow, and exams ask about both:

  • Accounting profit is positive — it equals the implicit costs. Babette with a risk premium shows $35,000 of accounting profit and $35,000 of implicit cost.
  • The owner has no reason to leave the business.

Watch out. Normal profit is zero economic profit, not zero accounting profit. A firm earning normal profit has an accounting profit exactly equal to its implicit costs.

1

Babette, Case 2 with risk. Economic profit $0; accounting profit $35,000 = implicit costs $35,000.

2

A firm with $30,000 of accounting profit and $30,000 of implicit costs. Normal profit.

3

A competitive firm in long-run equilibrium. Earns normal profit — and a positive accounting profit.

How it connects. It is economic profit (entry 9) at zero, reached when risk (entry 12) is covered, it is the point where entry and exit (entry 17) stops, and below it is an economic loss (entry 15).

Check yourself

14.1 A firm earns normal profit and its implicit costs are $40,000. What is its accounting profit?

Show answer

$40,000. At normal profit, accounting profit equals implicit costs.

14.2 Should a firm earning normal profit close?

Show answer

No. It is doing as well as its resources could anywhere else.

15

Economic loss

★ Exam
Definition

Negative economic profit. Total revenue does not cover explicit and implicit costs, so the resources would earn more elsewhere.

Textbook page 533.

Your textbook says a negative economic profit “can also be described as a loss”. Your exam's phrase is economic loss. Babette in Case 1 has one: −$2,000, even with $35,000 of accounting profit.

An economic loss is a signal that “there is a better alternative use for resources”. It does not mean the business is losing money on its books.

Watch out. A firm can make an economic loss and an accounting profit at the same time. The first is the one it responds to.

1

Babette, Case 1. Economic loss of $2,000; accounting profit $35,000.

2

A firm with revenue $90,000, explicit costs $60,000, implicit costs $35,000. Economic loss of $5,000.

3

A firm with revenue below its explicit costs. Both an accounting loss and an economic loss.

How it connects. It is the other side of normal profit (entry 14), and it is one of the two signals in how firms respond (entry 16).

Check yourself

15.1 Revenue is $200,000, explicit costs $150,000 and implicit costs $60,000. Profit or loss?

Show answer

Accounting profit $50,000; economic loss of $10,000.

15.2 What does an economic loss tell the owner?

Show answer

That her resources would earn more in their next-best use.

16

Firms respond to economic profit

★ Exam
Definition

Owners decide whether a business is worth continuing by its economic profit or loss, not its accounting profit.

Textbook pages 533 and 539.

Your textbook: economic profits “signal the best use of resources”. Positive economic profit says the current use is the best one; an economic loss says there is a better one. A firm's decision to stay in business or close for good “should be based on economic profit, not accounting profit”.

Babette's books show $35,000 in both cases. In Case 1 she should close; in Case 2 she should stay. Only economic profit tells the two apart.

Watch out. This is the long-run decision: stay or leave. In the short run a firm that has already paid for its equipment may keep producing at a loss for a while. You will meet that rule in Topic 3.6.

1

Babette, Case 1. Economic loss: close and work as a chef.

2

Babette, Case 2. Economic profit: stay open.

3

New England farms near cities. High land values raise the implicit cost of farming; many farmers sell up.

How it connects. It reads the sign of economic profit or loss (entry 15) and acts on it through entry and exit (entry 17).

Check yourself

16.1 Two firms report the same accounting profit. Why might one stay in business and the other close?

Show answer

Their implicit costs differ. The one whose owner has a better alternative may have an economic loss.

16.2 Which number did Sunny need to know before deciding whether to keep her sno cone stand?

Show answer

The opportunity cost of her own time, so that she could calculate her economic profit.

17

Entry and exit

★ ⚑
Definition

In the long run, with no barriers, firms enter a market where there is economic profit and exit one where there are economic losses.

Not in Module 52 — Textbook pages 601–602, Module 60.

⚑ Not in Module 52 — learn it from here. Your course asks you to explain how firms respond to profit opportunities. Module 52 stops at one owner's decision; the market's response, entry and exit, is in Module 60.

One owner deciding to stay or close is the same decision as a firm deciding whether to be in a market at all. If firms in an industry earn economic profit, outsiders can do better there than in their current use — so they come in. If firms make economic losses, some leave.

Entry adds to supply and pushes the price down; exit does the reverse. Both continue until the firms left are earning normal profit. Module 60 draws the whole process.

Watch out. Firms enter because of economic profit, not accounting profit. An industry where everyone earns normal profit has positive accounting profits and no entry.

1

A new food-truck craze with high economic profits. More trucks arrive until profits fall to normal.

2

Video rental stores making losses. Stores close until the survivors earn normal profit.

3

An industry earning normal profit. No entry, no exit — and positive accounting profits.

How it connects. It is how firms' response to profit (entry 16) works across a market, it stops at normal profit (entry 14), and its long-run outcome is calculated with profit per unit (entry 18).

Check yourself

17.1 Firms in an industry with no barriers earn economic profit. What happens in the long run?

Show answer

New firms enter, market supply rises, the price falls, and economic profit falls to zero.

17.2 Why do firms not leave an industry where they earn normal profit?

Show answer

Because they could not do better anywhere else.

18

Profit per unit

★ ⚑
Definition

Profit can be calculated per unit: Profit = (P − ATC) × Q, where ATC, average total cost, is total cost divided by quantity.

Not in Module 52 — Textbook page 590, Module 59.

⚑ Not in Module 52 — learn it from here. Your course asks you to calculate a firm's profit or loss, and exam questions do it with average total cost. Module 52 works only with totals; the per-unit form is in Module 59, after costs are taught.

Exam questions usually give you a price, an average total cost and a quantity rather than totals. Divide total revenue and total cost by quantity and you get price and average total cost; multiply the gap by quantity and you are back at profit.

Jennifer and Jason (Module 53) sell 5 bushels at $18 with a total cost of $72, so ATC is $72 ÷ 5 = $14.40. Profit = ($18 − $14.40) × 5 = $18 — the same as $90 − $72.

Watch out. Multiply by quantity. (P − ATC) alone is the profit on one unit, and exam options often offer it as a trap.

1

P $18, ATC $14.40, Q 5. Profit $18.

2

P $10, ATC $12, Q 100. Loss of $200.

3

P $10, ATC $10. Zero economic profit: normal profit.

How it connects. It rewrites total revenue minus total cost (entry 2) per unit, and it is the calculation used to show entry and exit (entry 17) on a graph.

Check yourself

18.1 A firm sells 200 units at $8. Its average total cost is $6.50. What is its economic profit?

Show answer

($8 − $6.50) × 200 = $300.

18.2 Price is $10, average total cost $10 and average variable cost $8. What kind of profit does the firm earn?

Show answer

Normal profit: price equals average total cost, so economic profit is zero.

A free-response question

1. (6 points) Lena runs a bike-repair shop. This year it took in $90,000. She paid $50,000 for rent, parts and a helper's wages. She owns her tools, which she could sell for $20,000 and put in a bank paying 5% a year. She left a job paying $36,000, and she needs $3,000 a year to compensate her for the risk of running her own business.

(a) Calculate Lena's accounting profit. (b) Calculate her total implicit costs. (c) Calculate her economic profit. (d) Should Lena keep the shop open? Explain. (e) Revenue rises to $96,000 with no change in costs. Calculate her economic profit. (f) If every shop in the town earns what Lena earns in part (e), and nothing stops new shops from opening, what will happen to economic profit in the long run? Explain.

Show the mark scheme

(a) 1 point — $90,000 − $50,000 = $40,000.

(b) 1 point — $1,000 (5% of $20,000) + $36,000 + $3,000 = $40,000.

(c) 1 point — $40,000 − $40,000 = $0.

(d) 1 point — yes. She earns a normal profit: her resources could not do better anywhere else.

(e) 1 point — $96,000 − $50,000 − $40,000 = $6,000.

(f) 1 point — it falls to zero. Economic profit attracts new shops; as they enter, the price of repairs falls until shops earn normal profit.

Things to get right

  • Accounting profit = revenue − explicit costs. Economic profit subtracts the implicit costs as well.
  • Interest paid is explicit; interest given up is implicit. The same goes for rent.
  • Three implicit costs: the cost of financial capital, the entrepreneur's time, and compensation for risk.
  • Economic profit is never larger than accounting profit.
  • Normal profit is zero economic profit, and then accounting profit equals implicit costs — it is positive, not zero.
  • Firms respond to economic profit: they enter where it is positive and exit where it is negative.
  • Profit = (P − ATC) × Q. Do not forget to multiply by quantity.
