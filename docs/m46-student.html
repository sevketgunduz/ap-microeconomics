Income Effects, Substitution Effects, and Elasticity

Sixteen terms from Module 46 of Krugman’s Economics for AP. First, why demand curves slope downward — the explanation Module 5 never gave you. Then the measurement that the rest of this unit runs on: elasticity.

Textbook pages 458–465·16 terms· Topics 2.1 and 2.3·1½ periods

How to use this guide

Read Module 46 in your textbook first. Entries 1 to 8 are the two effects that explain the law of demand; entries 9 to 14 are elasticity and how to calculate it; entries 15 and 16 are a first look at what the number means, which the next module finishes.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 46 of your textbook does not teach it. Entry 14 carries the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.

The one idea. A price change does two things at once: it changes what this good costs relative to others (substitution), and it changes what your money will buy at all (income). Elasticity then measures how big the resulting move is — as a ratio of two percentage changes, never in units.

⚑ One warning about method. Your textbook builds this module on the midpoint method. Your course defines elasticity as a ratio of two percentage changes and never names a technique, and its own questions hand you the percentages. Learn the method; learn what the number means first. That is entry 14.

The sixteen terms

01

The substitution effect

★ Exam
Definition

When the price of a good rises, you buy less of it and more of other things, because the other things have become relatively cheaper. When its price falls, you do the reverse.

Textbook page 458.

Nothing about your pay has changed. What has changed is the exchange rate between the two goods. Think of it in opportunity cost, the way your textbook does: if a coffee costs $4 and a sandwich costs $8, one sandwich costs you two coffees. Let the sandwich price fall to $6 and a sandwich now costs you one and a half coffees. Sandwiches got cheaper in terms of coffee, so you buy more of them — even though the coffee price never moved.

This is the main reason demand curves slope downward. For most goods it is the only reason that matters.

Watch out. The substitution effect is about relative prices, not about being better or worse off. A student who explains it by saying “the good is dearer so I can afford less” has described the other effect.

1

Cinema tickets rise, streaming does not. You watch more at home. Streaming did not get cheaper in pounds; it got cheaper relative to a ticket.

2

Train fares fall on your route. You take the train more and drive less. Driving costs what it always did — the train just became the better buy.

3

Apples and pears sit side by side. Apples go up 20p; shoppers reach for pears. The closer two goods are, the bigger this effect.

How it connects. It is the first half of the income effect (entry 2), and with the income effect (entry 4) it explains why demand curves slope downward (entry 4). Whether it is the whole story depends on how responsive buyers turn out to be — which is elastic and inelastic demand (entry 15).

Check yourself

1.1 The price of tea doubles. Tea is 0.5% of your spending. Which effect explains what you do?

Show answer

The substitution effect, almost entirely. Because tea is a tiny share of your spending, the fact that you are slightly poorer in real terms changes nothing you can notice.

1.2 Two goods both rise 10% on the same day. Is there a substitution effect between them?

Show answer

No. Neither has become relatively cheaper than the other, so the substitution effect between those two is zero. You may still buy less of both, which is the other effect at work.

02

The income effect

★ Exam
Definition

A price change alters what your money will buy. That change in purchasing power changes what you buy — on top of any substitution.

Textbook page 459.

Your textbook's example: a family spends half its income on rent. Rents rise everywhere. Two different things now happen.

  • Housing is relatively dearer, so the family wants less of it and more of everything else. That is substitution.
  • The family is genuinely poorer. The same pay no longer stretches as far. That alone changes every choice it makes, housing included.

The second is the income effect. It is big only when the good takes a large share of spending, which is why it shows up for rent and almost never for pillow cases.

Watch out. Nobody's income changed. Saying “the income effect is when your income changes” is the single commonest error in this module. The price changed, and that changed what your unchanged income will buy.

1

Rent rises 20% and you spend half your income on it. You are about 10% poorer in real terms. You cut back on everything, not only on housing.

2

Petrol doubles and you drive to work. A large share of your budget, so a large income effect: fewer restaurant meals, not just fewer miles.

3

Shoelaces double from 50p to £1. The income effect is real but invisible — you are 50p poorer a year. Only substitution matters here.

How it connects. It is the second half of the substitution effect (entry 1) and the reason the explanation of the law of demand (entry 4) matters; it works through real income (entry 3), and which way it pushes depends on whether the good is normal (entry 5).

Check yourself

2.1 Bread rises 10p. You spend 0.3% of your income on bread. How big is the income effect?

Show answer

Tiny — about 0.03% of your purchasing power. Real, but not worth modelling. Your textbook would say the substitution effect is the whole story here.

2.2 Why does your textbook use housing rather than chewing gum to explain this effect?

Show answer

Because the effect is proportional to the share of income the good absorbs. Housing can be half a budget; chewing gum never is.

03

Real income, and money income

○ Background
Definition

Real income is what your money will actually buy. Money income (or nominal income) is the number on your payslip, unadjusted.

Textbook page 459.

This is the machinery behind the income effect. When the price of something you buy a lot of goes up, your money income is untouched and your real income falls. Your textbook puts it exactly this way: income adjusted to reflect its true purchasing power is called real income, as against money or nominal income, “which has not been adjusted”.

You will not be asked to define these two terms in this unit. You will be asked why a price rise makes a buyer behave as though they were poorer, and this is the answer.

Watch out. Real income can fall while money income rises. If your pay goes up 2% and the things you buy go up 5%, you are worse off — and you will behave like it.

1

Your wage is unchanged; the bus fare doubles. Money income the same, real income lower. You feel poorer because you are.

2

Everything you buy falls 10% and your pay is frozen. Money income flat, real income up about 11%. A price fall is a pay rise in disguise.

3

Your pay rises 3%; your rent, which is half your spending, rises 10%. Money income up 3%, real income down: the rent rise alone costs you about 5% of your purchasing power.

How it connects. It is how the income effect (entry 2) actually works, and it is why a percentage change — not a change in pounds — is the right unit of measurement, which is the point of percent change (entry 11).

Check yourself

3.1 Prices in general rise 4% and your pay rises 4%. Has your real income changed?

Show answer

No. Money income is up 4%, prices are up 4%, and what your money buys is where it started.

3.2 Does the income effect of a price fall make you richer or poorer in real terms?

Show answer

Richer. A fall in the price of something you buy means the same money income commands more goods, and that pushes your quantity demanded up alongside substitution.

04

Why demand curves slope downward

★ Exam
Definition

Put the two effects together and you have the law of demand explained rather than asserted: a higher price means less of this good, by substitution and usually by income as well.

Textbook page 458.

In Module 5 you were told the demand curve slopes downward and shown that it does. Here is why. A price rise does two things to a buyer at once:

  • Substitution: this good is now relatively dear, so switch away from it.
  • Income: this good now eats more of the budget, so you are poorer in real terms.

For most goods the first does all the work and the second is too small to see. For goods that take a big share of spending, both matter, and for a normal good they push the same way, so the curve slopes downward all the more firmly.

Your course will accept a second, separate explanation as well — diminishing marginal utility, which you met in Unit 1. Either route is a complete answer.

Watch out. “Because people buy less when things cost more” restates the law; it does not explain it. An explanation names an effect: substitution, income, or diminishing marginal utility.

1

Why does less petrol get bought at £2 than at £1.50? Substitution towards buses, cycling and shorter trips, plus a real income loss for anyone who drives a lot.

2

Why does a 10% rise in cinema prices cut ticket sales? Almost all substitution — streaming, bowling, staying in. Cinema is too small a share of spending for income to matter.

3

Why does a rent rise cut the number of rooms rented? Both, and strongly: tenants downsize (substitution) and are poorer (income).

How it connects. It is the joint work of substitution (entry 1) and income (entry 2); the exception that would break it is the Giffen good (entry 8), and your textbook's table of estimates (entry 16) shows how differently it bites from one good to the next.

Check yourself

4.1 A question asks you to explain the law of demand in two sentences. Write them.

Show answer

A rise in the price of a good makes it relatively dearer than its alternatives, so buyers substitute towards those alternatives. It also lowers buyers' real income, which for a normal good reduces the quantity demanded further.

4.2 Does the demand curve slope downward for a good with no close substitutes at all?

Show answer

Yes. The substitution effect is smaller, so the curve is steeper, but a higher price still costs real income and still leaves some buyers out. Fewer substitutes means less responsive, not upward sloping.

05

Normal goods

★ Exam
Definition

A good you buy more of when your income rises, and less of when it falls.

Textbook page 459.

Your textbook's words: normal goods are “goods for which demand decreases when income falls”. The vast majority of what you buy is normal.

This matters here because it fixes the direction of the income effect. A price rise makes you poorer in real terms; being poorer makes you buy less of a normal good; so for a normal good the income effect reinforces the substitution effect. Both push quantity demanded down.

Watch out. Normal is not the same as necessary, and it is not a compliment. Caviar and bus passes can both be normal; it is only about which way demand moves when income moves.

1

Restaurant meals. Pay rise, more of them; pay cut, fewer. Normal.

2

Holidays abroad. Strongly normal — and the most price-responsive good in your textbook's own table.

3

Rented housing, in the textbook's example. Assumed normal, which is why the family's two effects pull the same way.

How it connects. It is the opposite of an inferior good (entry 6), and it is what makes the income effect (entry 2) reinforce the price elasticity you measure (entry 10).

Check yourself

5.1 A price rise for a normal good: which way does each effect push quantity demanded?

Show answer

Substitution pushes it down. Income also pushes it down, because the buyer is poorer in real terms and this is a good they buy less of when poorer. Both agree.

5.2 You later meet “income elasticity of demand”. What sign will it have for a normal good?

Show answer

Positive: income up, quantity demanded up. That is Topic 2.5 and the next module but one — the definition you are learning here is what it measures.

06

Inferior goods

★ Exam
Definition

A good you buy less of when your income rises — and more of when it falls.

Textbook page 459.

Your textbook: an inferior good is “a good for which demand increases when income falls”. Instant noodles, supermarket own-brands, long-distance coach travel, second-hand clothes. People move off them as they get richer.

Inferior goods are where this module gets interesting, because the income effect now runs against the substitution effect rather than with it.

Watch out. Inferior does not mean poor quality. It is defined only by what happens to demand when income changes. Plenty of inferior goods are perfectly good things.

1

Supermarket own-brand pasta. A pay rise and shoppers switch to the branded one. Inferior.

2

The coach instead of the train. Cheap, slow, and the first thing dropped when money allows.

3

Cafeteria meals, in your textbook's own review question. Students eat there more when the meal ticket gets dearer, because they are poorer.

How it connects. It is the opposite of a normal good (entry 5), and it is the case in which the two effects pull in opposite directions (entry 7).

Check yourself

6.1 Your income falls 20%. What happens to your demand for an inferior good?

Show answer

It rises. That is the definition — demand increases when income falls.

6.2 Is an inferior good's demand curve upward sloping?

Show answer

No. Almost never, anyway: see entries 7 and 8. The substitution effect still dominates, so a price rise still cuts the quantity demanded.

07

When the two effects pull apart

★ Exam
Definition

For an inferior good, a price rise makes buyers poorer, and poorer buyers want more of it. The income effect now works against the substitution effect.

Textbook pages 459–460.

Line the two up for a price rise:

GoodSubstitution effectIncome effectTogether
Normalquantity downquantity downdown, firmly
Inferiorquantity downquantity updown, but by less

The substitution effect almost always wins, so quantity demanded still falls and the law of demand still holds. Your textbook's advice is exactly this: “You can generally assume that the income effect for an inferior good is smaller than the substitution effect.”

Watch out. Opposite directions does not mean they cancel. One is nearly always much the larger, and the question to ask is which — not whether.

1

The price of cafeteria meals rises. Substitution says eat out more; income says you are poorer, so eat in more. Your textbook's students eat in more — income wins.

2

Own-brand pasta rises 30p. Substitution says buy the branded one; income says you are poorer, so buy more own-brand. Normally substitution wins and sales fall.

3

Coach fares rise on a student's only route home. Substitution towards the train, income towards the coach. Which wins is an empirical question, not a definition.

How it connects. It is what makes an inferior good (entry 6) different from a normal good, and taken to the extreme it is a Giffen good (entry 8).

Check yourself

7.1 For an inferior good, a price fall: which way does each effect push?

Show answer

Substitution pushes quantity demanded up. Income pushes it down — the buyer is now richer in real terms and buys less of an inferior good. They oppose, and substitution normally wins.

7.2 Why does your textbook say the law of demand still holds for inferior goods?

Show answer

Because the income effect is the smaller of the two in practice, so the downward substitution effect carries the day and quantity demanded still falls when price rises.

08

Giffen goods

○ Background
Definition

The theoretical case where the income effect is bigger than the substitution effect, so a price rise would raise the quantity demanded and the demand curve would slope upward.

Textbook page 459 (the fyi box) and page 460.

The story is nineteenth-century Irish potatoes: potatoes were inferior and took a huge share of a poor family's budget, so a price rise made families so much poorer that they ate more potatoes and less of everything else.

Your textbook is blunt about it: the story “has never been validated in any real situation, nineteenth-century Ireland included”, and “the law of demand really is a law, with few exceptions”. Learn it as the limiting case that shows you understand the two effects — not as a counter-example you will ever have to use.

Watch out. A Giffen good needs both conditions: inferior and a large share of spending. An inferior good on its own is nowhere near enough.

1

Irish potatoes in the famine era. The classic claim, and still unproven.

2

An inferior good that is 2% of your budget. Cannot be Giffen. The income effect has nothing to work with.

3

Your textbook's own cafeteria question. Its solution says that because students eat in more as the ticket price rises, cafeteria meals there are a Giffen good — the one worked case in the book.

How it connects. It is the extreme of the two effects pulling apart (entry 7), and it is the only thing that would break the law of demand (entry 4).

Check yourself

8.1 Why can a normal good never be Giffen?

Show answer

Because for a normal good the income effect pushes the same way as substitution. There is nothing pulling the other way, so quantity demanded must fall when price rises.

8.2 Name the two conditions a Giffen good must satisfy.

Show answer

It must be inferior, and it must absorb a large enough share of spending for the income effect to outweigh the substitution effect.

09

Elasticity

★ Exam
Definition

A measure of how responsive one thing is to a change in another — expressed as a ratio of percentage changes, so it has no units.

Textbook page 460.

Up to now you have answered direction questions: price up, quantity down. Elasticity answers the question a business actually has to ask: by how much?

Your textbook's framing is the 2004 flu-vaccine panic. A distributor wondering whether to raise its price does not need to know that sales will fall — everyone knows that. It needs to know whether they will fall a lot or a little.

Elasticity is a general tool. You can measure the responsiveness of quantity to the good's own price, to income, or to the price of some other good. This module does the first; your course does all three.

Watch out. Elasticity is a ratio of two percentage changes, not a change. “Demand fell by 300 units” is not an elasticity and cannot be turned into one without knowing what it fell from.

1

Quantity falls 10% when price rises 5%. Elasticity 2. Responsive.

2

Quantity falls 1% when price rises 5%. Elasticity 0.2 — your textbook's vaccine figure. Barely responsive.

3

Quantity rises 8% when income rises 4%. Elasticity 2 as well — but of income, not price. Same tool, different variable.

How it connects. The version of it this module measures is the price elasticity of demand (entry 10), and the numbers it produces are collected in your textbook's table of estimates (entry 16).

Check yourself

9.1 Why does elasticity use percentages rather than units?

Show answer

So the answer does not depend on how the good is measured. Doses or boxes, pounds or pence — the percentage change is the same, so the elasticity is the same.

9.2 Can you compute an elasticity from “price rose $1 and quantity fell 200”?

Show answer

No. You need the starting values to turn both into percentages. $1 on a $2 good is a very different thing from $1 on a $200 one.

10

Price elasticity of demand

★ Exam
Definition

The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, reported without its minus sign.

Textbook pages 460–461, Figure 46.1 and Equation 46-3.

This is the one formula the whole topic is built on:

price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

Your textbook's worked case is Figure 46.1, drawn above. A vaccination costs $20 and 10.0 million are bought. Raise the price to $21 and 9.9 million are bought.

  • Quantity: a fall of 0.1 million from 10.0 million, so −1%.
  • Price: a rise of $1 from $20, so +5%.
  • Elasticity: 1% ÷ 5% = 0.2.

0.2 is a small number, and it is exactly what the vaccine distributor hoped for: buyers hardly respond, so a price rise does not cost it many sales.

Watch out. Answer with a number, not a direction. “Quantity demanded falls” earns nothing on a question that asks for the price elasticity of demand.

1

$20 → $21, 10.0m → 9.9m. 1% ÷ 5% = 0.2. Your textbook's own figure.

2

Price up 2%, quantity down 10%. 10% ÷ 2% = 5. The textbook's MCQ 5.

3

Price up 25%, quantity down 10%. 10% ÷ 25% = 0.4 — about the figure your textbook reports for beef.

How it connects. It is the version of elasticity (entry 9) this topic uses; it needs percent change (entry 11), it is reported in absolute value (entry 12), and it can be computed two ways, which is the midpoint method (entry 13) and why normal and inferior goods differ only in the income part (entry 5).

Check yourself

10.1 A shop raises a price 4% and sells 2% fewer. What is the price elasticity of demand?

Show answer

2% ÷ 4% = 0.5.

10.2 Elasticity comes out as 3. Say in words what that means.

Show answer

Quantity demanded changes by three times the percentage that price changes, in the opposite direction. Buyers are very responsive.

11

Percent change

★ Exam
Definition

Change ÷ starting value × 100. It is how both halves of the elasticity ratio are built.

Textbook pages 460–461, Equations 46-1 and 46-2.

Your textbook writes the two equations separately because students get them the wrong way up:

% change in quantity demanded = (change in quantity demanded ÷ initial quantity demanded) × 100

% change in price = (change in price ÷ initial price) × 100

Both are divided by where you started. That is what makes this the initial-value method, and it is the method the review question on ice-cream sandwiches tells you to use.

Watch out. Divide by the starting value, not by the new one and not by the change. Going from 100 to 120 is a 20% rise; going back from 120 to 100 is a 16.7% fall. Same move, two numbers — and that is the problem entry 13 solves.

1

800 → 500. −300 ÷ 800 = −37.5%.

2

$0.50 → $0.55. $0.05 ÷ $0.50 = +10% — and with an elasticity of 1.2 that is a 12% fall, or 12,000 fewer ice-cream sandwiches.

3

10.0m → 9.9m. −0.1 ÷ 10.0 = −1%.

How it connects. It builds the price elasticity of demand (entry 10), it is measured against the value you started from (entry 3), and comparing it with the average instead of the start is the midpoint method (entry 13).

Check yourself

11.1 Quantity goes from 250 to 200. What is the percent change?

Show answer

−50 ÷ 250 = −20%.

11.2 Why does the direction you travel change the answer?

Show answer

Because the starting value changes. From 100 up to 120 you divide by 100; from 120 down to 100 you divide by 120. Different denominators, different percentages.

12

Absolute value — dropping the minus sign

★ Exam
Definition

Price and quantity demanded always move in opposite directions, so the raw ratio is negative. Elasticity is reported as its magnitude, without the sign.

Textbook pages 461–462.

Your textbook says economists “usually drop the minus sign and report the absolute value of the price elasticity of demand”. Examiners write the same thing as absolute value, and you will see questions phrased as “if the absolute value of the price elasticity of demand is 0.92 …”.

So write 0.2, or write |Ed| = 0.2. Do not write −0.2 unless the question asks for the signed value — but do be able to say why it is negative: the law of demand.

The sign carries no information for price elasticity of demand, because it is always the same sign. For the other elasticities you meet later, the sign is the whole point.

Watch out. “Larger” elasticity means larger in magnitude. 0.1 is smaller than 0.5, and −4 is bigger than −2 in the only sense this topic uses.

1

The ratio works out at −0.2. Report 0.2.

2

An exam says “the absolute value of the price elasticity of demand is 0.92”. Nothing is missing. That is the ordinary way to say it.

3

A student writes “elasticity = −1.67, so demand is negative”. The sign is not a property of demand; it is the law of demand showing up in the arithmetic.

How it connects. It is how the answer from the elasticity formula (entry 10) is reported, and it is the one thing the two methods in the method question (entry 14) always agree on.

Check yourself

12.1 Why is the price elasticity of demand negative before you drop the sign?

Show answer

Because the numerator and the denominator always have opposite signs: price up means quantity demanded down, and the other way round.

12.2 Which is more elastic, −0.3 or −2.5?

Show answer

−2.5. Compare magnitudes: 2.5 is far bigger than 0.3.

13

The midpoint method

★ Exam
Definition

Compute each percent change against the average of the two values rather than the starting one, so travelling up or down gives the same answer.

Textbook pages 462–464, Equations 46-4 and 46-5.

Your textbook motivates it with petrol. European petrol costs about three times American petrol. Measured from America it is 200% higher; measured from Europe, American petrol is 66.7% lower. Same two prices, two different percentages, and therefore two different elasticities for the same pair of points.

The fix is to divide by the midpoint of the two values:

% change in X = (change in X ÷ average value of X) × 100, where the average value of X = (starting value + final value) ÷ 2

Do it for both the price and the quantity and the elasticity is the same in both directions. Your textbook's strawberry case: $1.50 → $1.00 and 100,000 → 200,000 cartons gives −40% on price and +66.7% on quantity, so an elasticity of 1.67 whichever way you travel.

Watch out. Use the average in both halves or not at all. Mixing a midpoint quantity with an initial-value price is the most frequent way to lose the mark.

1

$1.50 → $1.00, 100,000 → 200,000. −40% and +66.7%, so elasticity 1.67. Travel the other way and it is still 1.67.

2

4,000 → 5,000 tickets. 1,000 ÷ 4,500 = 22.2%. If elasticity is 1, the price must fall by the same 22.2%.

3

$2 → $4 with 800 → 500. −46.2% and +66.7%, so 0.69 — your textbook's own free-response answer.

How it connects. It is a different way to build the percent changes (entry 11) for the price elasticity of demand (entry 10), and whether an exam wants it is a question worth settling now (entry 14).

Check yourself

13.1 Why does the midpoint method give the same answer in both directions?

Show answer

Because the denominator no longer depends on where you started. The average of the two values is the same whichever end you begin at.

13.2 Price goes from $4 to $6. What is the percent change by each method?

Show answer

Initial value: $2 ÷ $4 = 50%. Midpoint: $2 ÷ $5 = 40%. Over a jump that big the two methods are genuinely different.

14

Which calculation your exam expects

★ ⚑
Definition

Your course defines the price elasticity of demand as the ratio of two percentage changes and never names a technique for getting them. Exam questions nearly always hand you the percentages, or the elasticity itself.

Not in Module 46 — this entry is the comparison your textbook never makes.

⚑ Not in Module 46 — learn it from here. Your textbook teaches one technique as though it were the definition. Your course defines the ratio and leaves the technique open, and its own questions hand you the percentages. Learn the technique, but learn what the number means first.

Your textbook makes the midpoint method the centre of this module: it defines it in the margin, spends three pages on it, and requires it in four of its own review questions. That is a sound way to compute an elasticity over a large price change, and you should be able to do it.

But it is a technique, not the definition, and it is not the form exam questions take. Real exam items look like this:

  • “A 2 percent increase in the price of bologna causes a 5 percent decrease in the quantity demanded of cheese” — the percentages are given; divide.
  • “If the absolute value of the price elasticity of demand is 0.92 …” — the elasticity is given; use it.

So carry both. If a question gives you two price–quantity pairs and asks for a number, either method earns the mark — say which one you used, and use the same one on both halves of the ratio.

Watch out. Do not spend your revision time on the midpoint formula at the expense of knowing what an elasticity means. The interpretation questions are the ones that come up.

1

“Price rises 10%, quantity demanded falls 4%. Find the elasticity.” 0.4. No method needed — both percentages are handed to you. This is the common shape.

2

“From the table: at $2, 800 units; at $4, 500 units.” Now you choose. Midpoint gives 0.69; the starting values give 0.375. State which you used.

3

“The absolute value of the price elasticity of demand is 0.92. Does spending rise?” No calculation of elasticity at all — you are being asked what 0.92 implies, which is the next module.

How it connects. It is the practical form of the midpoint method (entry 13), it decides how you use the absolute value convention (entry 12), and it is why elastic and inelastic (entry 15) is a vocabulary question rather than an arithmetic one.

Check yourself

14.1 An item gives “a 6% fall in price led to a 3% rise in quantity demanded”. Which method should you use?

Show answer

Neither — they are not needed. The percentages are already there: 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5.

14.2 You compute 0.69 by midpoint and your friend computes 0.75 from the starting values. Who is wrong?

Show answer

Neither, as long as each says what they did and uses one method consistently. They are two ways of expressing the same movement along the same demand curve.

15

Elastic and inelastic, a first look

★ Exam
Definition

A big elasticity means buyers respond a lot — demand is elastic. A small one means they hardly respond — demand is inelastic.

Textbook page 461, and Table 46.1 on page 463.

Module 46 uses both words without pinning them down. Your textbook says a price elasticity of 0.2 “indicates a small response of quantity demanded to price … This is what economists call inelastic demand”, and that when buyers respond a lot “demand is highly elastic”.

Notice that Table 46.1 is already split into two halves — inelastic above, elastic below — with eggs at 0.1 at the top and foreign travel at 4.1 at the bottom. Look at where your textbook draws the line between the halves and you can read off the number that separates them.

The next module makes that number official, adds unit elastic, and shows what each case does to a seller's revenue. For now: bigger means more responsive.

1 point — the price elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.

1 point — (change in quantity demanded ÷ average quantity demanded) ÷ (change in price ÷ average price).

1 point — 0.69. Quantity: −300 ÷ 650 = −46.2%. Price: $2 ÷ $3 = +66.7%. 46.2 ÷ 66.7 = 0.69.

2. Assume the price of an inferior good increases.

(a) In what direction will the substitution effect change the quantity demanded? Explain.

(b) In what direction will the income effect change the quantity demanded? Explain.

(c) Given that the demand curve for the good slopes downward, what is true of the relative sizes of the income and substitution effects? Explain.

Show the mark scheme

(a) Decrease. The good is now relatively more expensive than its alternatives, so buyers substitute away from it.

(b) Increase. The price rise lowers buyers’ real income, and demand for an inferior good rises when income falls.

(c) The substitution effect is larger. The two effects pull in opposite directions, so if the curve slopes downward — quantity demanded falls when price rises — the downward substitution effect must outweigh the upward income effect.

Part (c) is the whole module in one sentence. If the income effect were the larger, you would have a Giffen good and an upward-sloping demand curve.

Things to get right

  • Nobody’s income changed. The income effect comes from a price change altering what your existing income will buy.
  • Quantity on top. Price elasticity of demand is %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP. Upside down is the most expensive slip in this topic.
  • Percentages, not units. “Sales fell by 300” is not an elasticity.
  • Say the method. If you are given two points and asked for a number, state whether you used the starting values or the midpoint, and use it on both halves.
  • Report the magnitude. Drop the minus sign — but know that it is there because of the law of demand.
  • Opposite is not equal. For an inferior good the two effects oppose; substitution almost always still wins.
  • Inferior is not bad, normal is not necessary. Both words are only about what happens when income moves.
