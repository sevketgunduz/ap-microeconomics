1. An analyst wants to know how much the quantity of butter demanded will change when the price of margarine changes.

Which elasticity answers the question?

  • (A) The cross-price elasticity of demand for butter with respect to the price of margarine
  • (B) The price elasticity of demand for butter
  • (C) The income elasticity of demand for butter
  • (D) The price elasticity of supply of butter
  • (E) None — elasticity can only relate a good to its own price

2. The price of good B rises from $8 to $12. The quantity of good A demanded rises from 100 to 150.

Using the midpoint method, what is the cross-price elasticity of demand, and what are the two goods?

  • (A) −1.0, and they are complements
  • (B) +0.4, and they are substitutes
  • (C) +1.0, and they are substitutes
  • (D) +2.5, and they are substitutes
  • (E) It cannot be found without the price of good A

3. A student computes the cross-price elasticity between tea and coffee by dividing the percentage change in the price of tea by the percentage change in the quantity of coffee demanded.

What has gone wrong?

  • (A) Nothing — the two divisions give the same number.
  • (B) The sign will be wrong, although the magnitude will be right.
  • (C) Cross-price elasticity cannot be computed for drinks, which are substitutes by definition.
  • (D) The student should have used the quantity of tea rather than the quantity of coffee.
  • (E) The ratio is upside down. The cross-price elasticity puts the percentage change in the quantity of one good on top and the percentage change in the price of the other underneath.

4. When the price of cinema tickets rises, the quantity of streaming subscriptions demanded rises. The cross-price elasticity is +1.6.

What does that tell you?

  • (A) Cinema tickets are a normal good.
  • (B) The two are substitutes, and strong ones: the quantity of subscriptions moves 1.6 times as fast, in percentage terms, as the cinema price that caused it.
  • (C) The two are complements, because one is entertainment and the other is entertainment.
  • (D) Streaming subscriptions are income-elastic.
  • (E) Cinema demand is price-elastic.

5. The price of printers rises from $200 to $300. The quantity of ink cartridges demanded falls from 5,000 to 3,000.

Using the midpoint method, what is the cross-price elasticity, and what does it say?

  • (A) +1.25, so the goods are substitutes
  • (B) −0.8, so the goods are complements
  • (C) −2.0, so the goods are complements
  • (D) −1.25, so the goods are complements
  • (E) 1.25 — the sign is dropped, as for any elasticity

6. The price of bicycle tyres rises by 15%. The quantity of toothpaste demanded does not change at all.

What is the cross-price elasticity, and what does it tell you?

  • (A) Zero, and the two goods are not related — a third case alongside substitutes and complements. Your textbook's summary table lists only the positive and negative cases, but the framework names all three.
  • (B) Zero, which means the two goods are very weak substitutes.
  • (C) Zero, which means toothpaste is perfectly inelastic.
  • (D) It cannot be computed, because dividing zero by a number is undefined.
  • (E) Infinite, because the denominator is what matters.

7. Two pairs of goods have cross-price elasticities of +0.05 and −3.0.

Which pair is more strongly related, and what kind of relationship does each have?

  • (A) The first, because +0.05 is positive and positive relationships are stronger.
  • (B) They are equally related, because both are non-zero.
  • (C) The second. Strength is read from the magnitude, so 3.0 is a far stronger link than 0.05 — and the signs say the second pair are complements while the first are very weak substitutes.
  • (D) The first pair are substitutes and the second are unrelated, since −3.0 is below zero.
  • (E) It cannot be said without knowing the prices of the four goods.

8. A student calculates a cross-price elasticity of −2.4, writes "2.4, dropping the minus sign as usual", and concludes that the goods are substitutes.

What is wrong, and why did it happen?

  • (A) Nothing is wrong; cross-price elasticities are always reported positive.
  • (B) The arithmetic is wrong; a cross-price elasticity cannot be negative.
  • (C) The conclusion is right but the method is wrong.
  • (D) The student should have used the initial-value method instead.
  • (E) The habit came from the price elasticity of demand, where the sign is always negative and carries no information. Here the sign is the information: −2.4 means complements, and dropping it turns the answer into its opposite.

9. A household's income rises from $40,000 to $60,000 and the number of restaurant meals it buys each year rises from 24 to 48.

Using the midpoint method, what is the income elasticity of demand for restaurant meals?

  • (A) +0.6
  • (B) +1.67
  • (C) +2.0
  • (D) +40
  • (E) −1.67

10. An exam question asks: "What is the income elasticity of demand for a normal good?"

What is the complete and correct answer?

  • (A) Exactly 1, because demand rises in step with income.
  • (B) Greater than 1, because normal goods are luxuries.
  • (C) Between 0 and 1, because most normal goods are necessities.
  • (D) Positive — that is the whole of what "normal" means. Its size then separates income-elastic normal goods from income-inelastic ones, but any positive value qualifies.
  • (E) It depends on the price of the good.

11. A family's income falls after a job loss. Their spending on supermarket own-brand goods rises and their spending on branded goods falls.

What is the sign of the income elasticity of demand for own-brand goods for this family, and what does it make them?

  • (A) Negative, so own-brand goods are inferior for this family: quantity demanded moves opposite to income.
  • (B) Positive, so own-brand goods are normal.
  • (C) Zero, because the family's total spending did not change.
  • (D) Negative, so own-brand goods are complements of branded goods.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without the prices.

12. A student says: "Restaurant meals are income-elastic, so they must be an inferior good — rich people eat out more."

Two things are wrong. What are they?

  • (A) Income-elastic goods have a negative elasticity, and rich people do not eat out more.
  • (B) Nothing is wrong; income-elastic and inferior mean the same thing.
  • (C) Income-elastic means the income elasticity is above 1, which requires it to be positive — so an income-elastic good is normal, not inferior. And the observation that richer people eat out more is exactly what "normal" means, so it contradicts the conclusion rather than supporting it.
  • (D) The student should have said price-elastic, and inferior goods have no income elasticity.
  • (E) Restaurant meals are actually income-inelastic, and inferior goods are a type of luxury.

13. A good's income elasticity of demand is +0.3.

How should it be classified?

  • (A) Inferior, because 0.3 is small
  • (B) Normal and income-elastic, because 0.3 is positive
  • (C) Unit elastic, because the value is between 0 and 1
  • (D) It cannot be classified without the price elasticity
  • (E) Normal, because the sign is positive, and income-inelastic, because 0.3 is below 1 — the pattern of a necessity

14. The price of a crop rises from $15 to $25 a tonne. The quantity farmers supply rises from 600 to 900 tonnes.

Using the midpoint method, what is the price elasticity of supply, and is supply elastic or inelastic?

  • (A) 1.25, elastic
  • (B) 0.8, inelastic
  • (C) 0.5, inelastic
  • (D) 1.5, elastic
  • (E) −0.8, inelastic

15. A student is asked for the price elasticity of supply and computes it from a table of prices and quantities demanded.

Why is the answer wrong, and what else would have shown it?

  • (A) Nothing is wrong; the same table serves both.
  • (B) Because the elasticity of supply must be computed with the initial-value method.
  • (C) Because the elasticity of supply can only be calculated in the long run.
  • (D) Because elasticity of supply is measured in units, not percentages.
  • (E) The two measure movements along different curves — supply along the supply curve, demand along the demand curve. One clue is the sign: quantity supplied moves the same way as price, so a supply elasticity comes out positive before any adjustment, while the demand figure starts out negative.

16. A market has a price elasticity of supply of 0.4. A student concludes: "Supply is inelastic, so a price rise will increase producers' total revenue."

Which part is right and which is wrong?

  • (A) "Inelastic" is right — 0.4 is below the benchmark of 1. The revenue conclusion is misapplied: the total-revenue rules belong to the price elasticity of demand, and no equivalent rule is attached to the supply elasticity.
  • (B) Both parts are right.
  • (C) "Inelastic" is wrong; 0.4 means elastic supply.
  • (D) Both parts are wrong, because supply elasticities cannot be below 1.
  • (E) "Inelastic" is right, and the revenue conclusion is right for the wrong reason.

17. A stadium seats 60,000. However high ticket prices go on the night, 60,000 seats are supplied.

How is this supply best described, and what does the curve look like?

  • (A) Elastic, because tickets can be resold.
  • (B) Unit elastic, because quantity and price are proportional.
  • (C) Perfectly elastic, and the supply curve is horizontal.
  • (D) Perfectly inelastic, and the supply curve is a vertical line at 60,000. The quantity is fixed, which is not the same as there being no supply.
  • (E) There is no supply curve, because the quantity cannot respond.

18. A mass-produced screw costs $0.04 to make, counting every opportunity cost, and the inputs are abundant. Buyers can have as many as they like at $0.04, and nobody would supply any at $0.03.

What does the supply curve look like, and what fixes the height of the line?

  • (A) Vertical at the current quantity, fixed by capacity.
  • (B) Horizontal at $0.04 — perfectly elastic supply. The height is set by the cost of production including opportunity cost: below it producers do better doing something else.
  • (C) Horizontal at $0.04, fixed by what buyers are willing to pay.
  • (D) Upward sloping, because all supply curves slope upward.
  • (E) Horizontal, which means the elasticity of supply is zero.

19. Pizza supply is close to perfectly elastic. Supply of radio spectrum for mobile phones is perfectly inelastic.

What explains the difference?

  • (A) Pizza is cheap and spectrum is expensive.
  • (B) Pizza is a necessity and spectrum is a luxury.
  • (C) Demand for pizza is elastic and demand for spectrum is inelastic.
  • (D) The inputs. Flour, tomatoes and cheese are abundant and can be moved into pizza-making cheaply, so output expands easily. The radio spectrum cannot be manufactured at all, so the quantity is fixed whatever the price.
  • (E) Pizza producers are numerous and spectrum is sold by a government, and the number of sellers is what determines supply elasticity.

20. An exam question asks how "the price of alternative inputs" affects the price elasticity of supply. The textbook only discussed the availability of inputs.

Are these the same idea, and what is the answer?

  • (A) They are the same idea stated from two directions: an input is "available" to an industry precisely when it is cheap enough to bid away from its other uses. When alternative inputs are cheap, output can be expanded without costs rising much, so supply is more elastic.
  • (B) They are different: the price of alternative inputs affects demand, not supply.
  • (C) They are different: "alternative inputs" means substitute goods that consumers could buy instead.
  • (D) They are the same, and cheap alternative inputs make supply less elastic.
  • (E) The question cannot be answered, because the textbook does not cover it.

21. A sharp rise in the price of copper produces almost no extra copper for three years. After ten years, output is far higher, with the price no higher than it was in year three.

What does this show about the price elasticity of supply?

  • (A) That it fell over the decade as producers adjusted.
  • (B) That it is larger in the long run than in the short run: opening a mine takes years, so quantity supplied can barely respond at first and responds strongly once producers have had time to change capacity.
  • (C) That copper has a perfectly inelastic supply curve permanently.
  • (D) That the price must have risen again in year ten.
  • (E) That copper is an inferior good.

22. Farming in the United States has become steadily more productive for a century and a half, and the share of the population working on farms has collapsed.

Which pair of facts explains it?

  • (A) Demand for food is price-elastic, so lower prices raised farm revenue, and incomes fell.
  • (B) Demand for food is income-elastic and price-elastic, so the sector grew more slowly than the economy.
  • (C) Food is an inferior good, so richer consumers buy less of it in absolute terms.
  • (D) Farm productivity rose while farm employment was capped by regulation.
  • (E) Demand for food is income-inelastic, so spending on food rises more slowly than income and farming's share of the economy shrinks; and demand for food is price-inelastic, so the lower prices that rising yields produce reduce farmers' total revenue.

23. Assume that a 2 percent increase in the price of bologna causes a 5 percent decrease in the quantity demanded of cheese. What is the cross-price elasticity of demand between these goods, and how are these goods related?

  • (A) Cross-price elasticity of demand equals −0.4, and these goods are complements.
  • (B) Cross-price elasticity of demand equals +0.4, and these goods are substitutes.
  • (C) Cross-price elasticity of demand equals −2.5, and these goods are complements.
  • (D) Cross-price elasticity of demand equals +2.5, and these goods are substitutes.
  • (E) Cross-price elasticity of demand equals −0.4, and these goods are substitutes.

24. Four cross-price elasticities are reported: +2.8, −0.2, 0, and +0.1.

Which describes the strongest relationship, and which describes no relationship at all?

  • (A) +0.1 is strongest and −0.2 is no relationship.
  • (B) −0.2 is strongest because it is negative; +2.8 is no relationship.
  • (C) +2.8 is strongest and +0.1 is no relationship, since 0.1 is almost nothing.
  • (D) +2.8 describes the strongest relationship — close substitutes — and 0 describes no relationship at all. The other two are genuine but weak: −0.2 weak complements, +0.1 weak substitutes.
  • (E) They cannot be ranked, because positive and negative values measure different things.

25. Two industries face the same 30% price rise. A furniture workshop can hire more carpenters and buy more timber at little extra cost, and doubles output within a year. A vineyard producing a wine that grows only on one hillside cannot increase output at all for a decade.

Which determinants explain the difference, and in which direction?

  • (A) Income elasticity: furniture is normal and wine is inferior.
  • (B) The price elasticity of demand for each product.
  • (C) Both the availability and price of inputs, and time. The workshop's inputs are abundant and cheap to attract, so its supply is elastic; the vineyard's essential input is a fixed piece of land, so its supply is inelastic — and even time does not help, because the input cannot be reproduced.
  • (D) Only time, since both would respond equally given long enough.
  • (E) The number of competitors in each market.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Elasticity beyond price · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The quantity of one good responding to the price of another is exactly what the cross-price elasticity of demand measures.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, and it names which good is on top.
  • (B) ✗ That measures butter's response to the price of butter.
  • (C) ✗ That measures the response to income.
  • (D) ✗ That is the producers' side of the butter market.
  • (E) ✗ The opening claim of the module is the opposite: elasticity works for any two variables.

Question 2 — Cross-price elasticity · Application

Answer: (C)

Midpoint method. Quantity of A: +50 ÷ 125 = +40%. Price of B: +$4 ÷ $10 = +40%. The ratio is +1.0, and the positive sign means substitutes.

  • (A) ✗ Right magnitude, wrong sign — and the wrong sign gives the wrong relationship.
  • (B) ✗ 0.4 divides the raw quantity change by the raw price change, which is not an elasticity.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ 2.5 uses initial values on one half and midpoints on the other.
  • (E) ✗ The price of A never enters the cross-price formula.

Question 3 — Cross-price elasticity · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Equation 48-1 divides the percentage change in the quantity of one good by the percentage change in the price of the other. The student has inverted it.

  • (A) ✗ The two divisions give reciprocals, not the same number.
  • (B) ✗ The sign survives inversion; it is the magnitude that is wrong.
  • (C) ✗ Tea and coffee are a perfectly ordinary pair, and nothing makes them substitutes by

definition.

  • (D) ✗ Using the quantity of tea would measure the own-price elasticity of tea, a different

thing again.

  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 4 — Substitutes · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Positive means substitutes; 1.6 means the response is proportionally larger than the cause, so the two are close substitutes.

  • (A) ✗ Normal and inferior belong to the income elasticity.
  • (B) ✓ Correct, with both the sign and the size read.
  • (C) ✗ Being in the same category is not the test. Complements require a negative sign.
  • (D) ✗ Income-elastic is again the income elasticity.
  • (E) ✗ The own-price elasticity of cinema tickets is not given and is not what +1.6 measures.

Question 5 — Complements · Application

Answer: (D)

Quantity of cartridges: −2,000 ÷ 4,000 = −50%. Price of printers: +$100 ÷ $250 = +40%. The ratio is −1.25, and the negative sign means complements.

  • (A) ✗ Right magnitude, sign dropped — and the sign was the answer.
  • (B) ✗ 0.8 is the ratio upside down, 40 ÷ 50.
  • (C) ✗ −2.0 would need a 80% fall in cartridges.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The sign is dropped only for the price elasticity of demand. Here it is the

conclusion.

Question 6 — Goods that are not related · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Zero on top makes the whole ratio zero, and MKT-3.E.11's third category is exactly this: goods that are not related. The textbook teaches two outcomes; the framework names three.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, and it says why the case is easy to miss.
  • (B) ✗ Weak substitutes have a small positive value, not zero. Zero means no relationship at

all.

  • (C) ✗ Confuses the cross-price elasticity with the good's own price elasticity.
  • (D) ✗ Zero divided by 15% is zero, which is perfectly well defined. It is the other way round

that would be undefined.

  • (E) ✗ The denominator is 15%, not zero.

Question 7 — How big the number is · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The sign names the relationship and the magnitude measures its strength. Compare 3.0 with 0.05, not −3.0 with +0.05.

  • (A) ✗ Positive relationships are not stronger; they are differently signed.
  • (B) ✗ Non-zero means related, not equally related.
  • (C) ✓ Correct, with both the strength and the two relationships.
  • (D) ✗ −3.0 is a very strong complement relationship. Only zero means unrelated.
  • (E) ✗ The elasticities already encode the price changes.

Question 8 — Keeping the minus sign · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The most expensive habit in Unit 2, and it comes from a good habit two modules earlier. Here the sign is the finding.

  • (A) ✗ They are reported with their signs, precisely because the sign distinguishes the two

cases.

  • (B) ✗ A negative cross-price elasticity is not an arithmetic error; it is complements.
  • (C) ✗ The conclusion is wrong too: −2.4 means complements.
  • (D) ✗ The method is not the problem; both methods would give a negative number.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, and it diagnoses where the habit came from.

Question 9 — Income elasticity · Application

Answer: (B)

Quantity: +24 ÷ 36 = +66.7%. Income: +$20,000 ÷ $50,000 = +40%. The income elasticity is +1.67 — normal, and comfortably income-elastic.

  • (A) ✗ The ratio upside down: 40 ÷ 66.7.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ 2.0 is the initial-value answer, 100% ÷ 50%. The question specifies midpoint.
  • (D) ✗ The income percentage on its own.
  • (E) ✗ Both figures rose, so the elasticity is positive.

Question 10 — Normal goods · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Krugman's own MCQ 3 is built on this. Normal is defined by the sign alone; the magnitude then subdivides normal goods into income-elastic and income-inelastic.

  • (A) ✗ Some normal goods sit at 1, but most do not.
  • (B) ✗ That describes income-elastic normal goods only.
  • (C) ✗ That describes income-inelastic normal goods only.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The good's own price plays no part in the income elasticity.

Question 11 — Inferior goods · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Income fell and quantity demanded rose, so the two move in opposite directions and the income elasticity is negative — the definition of an inferior good.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Positive would mean they moved together, which is the branded goods.
  • (C) ✗ The elasticity is about this good's quantity, not about total spending.
  • (D) ✗ Complements belong to the cross-price elasticity. This is an income story.
  • (E) ✗ Normal and inferior are defined by the response to income, which is given.

Question 12 — Income-elastic and income-inelastic · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Two separate errors: income-elastic is a sub-category of normal, and the observation offered as evidence actually contradicts the conclusion.

  • (A) ✗ Income-elastic means greater than 1, which is positive, and rich people eating out more

is true.

  • (B) ✗ They are opposites: income-elastic is positive and above 1, inferior is negative.
  • (C) ✓ Correct, and it identifies both errors.
  • (D) ✗ Inferior goods do have income elasticities — negative ones.
  • (E) ✗ Restaurant meals are a standard income-elastic example, and inferior goods are not

luxuries.

Question 13 — Income-elastic and income-inelastic · Application

Answer: (E)

Sign first: positive, so normal. Then size: below 1, so income-inelastic — demand grows more slowly than income, which is the pattern of a necessity.

  • (A) ✗ Inferior requires a negative value, not a small one.
  • (B) ✗ Right on normal, wrong on the subdivision: 0.3 is below 1, so income-inelastic.
  • (C) ✗ Unit elastic means exactly 1.
  • (D) ✗ The price elasticity is a different measurement and is not needed.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, reading the sign and then the size.

Question 14 — Price elasticity of supply · Application

Answer: (B)

Quantity supplied: +300 ÷ 750 = +40%. Price: +$10 ÷ $20 = +50%. The elasticity of supply is 0.8, below 1, so supply is inelastic.

  • (A) ✗ The ratio upside down: 50 ÷ 40.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ 0.5 would need a 25% quantity rise.
  • (D) ✗ 1.5 mixes the methods.
  • (E) ✗ A supply elasticity is positive. Price and quantity supplied move together.

Question 15 — Price elasticity of supply · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Supply elasticity is measured along the supply curve. The sign is the quickest check: a supply elasticity is positive without adjustment, while a demand calculation starts out negative.

  • (A) ✗ One table gives one relationship, and it is not both.
  • (B) ✗ The midpoint method works for supply exactly as for demand.
  • (C) ✗ Supply elasticity is measured over any period; the long run simply gives a larger value.
  • (D) ✗ All elasticities are ratios of percentages.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, with the diagnostic.

Question 16 — The benchmark of 1 for supply · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The benchmark is the same for supply as for demand, so "inelastic" is right. The revenue rules are not: they come from MKT-3.E.5, which is about the price elasticity of demand.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, separating the two halves.
  • (B) ✗ The revenue half does not follow.
  • (C) ✗ 0.4 is below 1, so inelastic.
  • (D) ✗ Supply elasticities below 1 are ordinary — perfectly inelastic supply is 0.
  • (E) ✗ The revenue conclusion is not right at all. Nothing in the module ties a supply elasticity

to total revenue.

Question 17 — Perfectly inelastic supply · Analysis

Answer: (D)

A fixed quantity whatever the price: elasticity 0, vertical curve. Note what it does not mean — the seats exist and are sold.

  • (A) ✗ Resale moves tickets between buyers; it does not create seats.
  • (B) ✗ Unit elastic means quantity moves in proportion to price, which here it does not move at

all.

  • (C) ✗ Horizontal is the opposite case.
  • (D) ✓ Correct, and it heads off the "no supply" misreading.
  • (E) ✗ There is a supply curve; it is vertical.

Question 18 — Perfectly elastic supply · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Horizontal at the price that just covers the cost of production, including opportunity cost. Below that line producers do something else; at it, they will supply any quantity.

  • (A) ✗ Capacity is not binding — the inputs are abundant.
  • (B) ✓ Correct, with the right explanation of the height.
  • (C) ✗ Right shape, wrong reason. The height is set by cost, not by willingness to pay.
  • (D) ✗ The two extreme cases are exceptions to the usual upward slope.
  • (E) ✗ A horizontal supply curve has infinite elasticity. Zero is the vertical case.

Question 19 — Availability of inputs · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The determinant is the inputs: abundant and cheap to redirect gives elastic supply, fixed and unobtainable gives inelastic supply.

  • (A) ✗ Price level is not a determinant of supply elasticity.
  • (B) ✗ Necessity and luxury belong to the demand side.
  • (C) ✗ The elasticity of demand says nothing about how easily output can be expanded.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Reaches a plausible-sounding conclusion through the wrong mechanism. Number of sellers is

a market-structure question; what fixes spectrum supply is physics, not ownership.

Question 20 — The price of alternative inputs · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The framework's wording and the textbook's are the same economics: an input is available when it is cheap to bid away from its other uses, and cheap inputs mean elastic supply.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, with the direction stated.
  • (B) ✗ Input prices are a supply-side matter by definition.
  • (C) ✗ "Alternative inputs" is about what producers buy, not what consumers buy.
  • (D) ✗ Right identification, wrong direction: cheap alternative inputs make supply more

elastic.

  • (E) ✗ The textbook covers the idea thoroughly; only the phrase is missing.

Question 21 — Time · Analysis

Answer: (B)

The long-run elasticity of supply exceeds the short-run elasticity, because producers need time to change capacity. The price not rising again in year ten is the detail that rules out other explanations.

  • (A) ✗ It rose, not fell: the response grew.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Permanently inelastic is contradicted by the ten-year response.
  • (D) ✗ The question states that it did not.
  • (E) ✗ Inferior is an income-elasticity classification, and this is about supply.

Question 22 — Why farming shrank · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Both elasticities are needed, and both are below 1. One shrinks food's share of spending as incomes rise; the other turns rising yields into falling farm revenue.

  • (A) ✗ Gets the price elasticity backwards. If demand were elastic, lower prices would raise

farm revenue.

  • (B) ✗ Both elasticities are mislabelled as elastic.
  • (C) ✗ Food is not inferior: spending on it rises with income, just slowly.
  • (D) ✗ No regulation caps farm employment; the explanation is entirely about elasticities.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, with both halves and both directions.

Question 23 — Cross-price elasticity, the published sample question · Application

Answer: (C)

A 5% decrease in the quantity of cheese divided by a 2% increase in the price of bologna is −5 ÷ 2 = −2.5. Negative means complements, and 2.5 means a strong relationship.

  • (A) ✗ −0.4 is the ratio upside down, with the sign kept correctly.
  • (B) ✗ Upside down and the sign dropped.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Right magnitude, sign dropped — which converts complements into substitutes.
  • (E) ✗ Right sign, wrong magnitude, and then the wrong relationship read from a correct

negative sign.

Question 24 — Sign and size together · Application

Answer: (D)

Magnitude for strength, sign for kind, and zero for no relationship. All four values are read the same way.

  • (A) ✗ 0.1 is the second weakest, and −0.2 is a real if weak relationship.
  • (B) ✗ A negative sign does not confer strength, and +2.8 is the strongest of the four.
  • (C) ✗ Right on the strongest, wrong on the rest: +0.1 is a weak substitute relationship, and

only 0 means none.

  • (D) ✓ Correct, with all four read.
  • (E) ✗ They can be ranked by magnitude, which is exactly what strength means here.

Question 25 — The determinants together · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Inputs first, time second — and the vineyard shows the limit of the time argument, because the essential input cannot be reproduced at all.

  • (A) ✗ Income elasticity is a demand-side measure and says nothing about supply.
  • (B) ✗ Demand elasticity does not determine how fast output can expand.
  • (C) ✓ Correct, including why time does not rescue the vineyard.
  • (D) ✗ Time is the weaker of the two here; one input is irreproducible.
  • (E) ✗ The number of competitors is market structure, not supply elasticity.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 19 concept entries appear. Cross-price elasticity takes five items and income elasticity four — together the whole of Topic 2.5 — while the supply elasticity and its determinants take eight, which is the whole of Topic 2.4. Question 23 is the only published College Board item in either topic and is reproduced word for word.

Bloom levels. Application 7 · Analysis 12 · Synthesis 6, a ratio of 1 : 1.7 : 0.9. The same shape as the Modules 46 and 47 papers, so the three can be compared directly. Both topics here carry the quantitative suggested skill 3.C, which is why seven items are numeric.

The sign is the spine of this paper. Questions 5, 8, 11 and 23 all punish the dropped minus sign, and they do it in four different ways: computing a negative value, naming the habit, reading a sign from behaviour, and choosing between two options that differ only in sign. A student who loses all four has one fixable problem, not four.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: the ratio inverted (2A, 3A, 5B, 9A, 14A, 23A); the sign dropped (5E, 8A, 23D); cross-price confused with income elasticity (4A, 4D, 11D); "zero means weak" (6B, 24C); the revenue rules exported from demand to supply (16B, 16E); and the two extreme supply curves swapped (17C, 18E).

Three distractors reach a right-looking conclusion by wrong reasoning, flagged in the solutions: 18C, 19E and 23E. The most useful is 19E — the number of sellers sounds like it ought to matter, and a student who picks it is reasoning about market structure instead of about inputs.

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 5 · D 5 · E 5 — exactly even, with no letter repeating in consecutive questions.
