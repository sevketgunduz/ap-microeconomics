The 13 terms

01

Trade

★ Exam
Definition

In a market economy people trade: they provide goods and services to others and receive goods and services in return.

Textbook page 23.

Start with the alternative, because that is what makes trade interesting. Your textbook opens with a family that grows its own food, sews its own clothing, provides its own entertainment and writes its own economics textbooks. It is not impossible. It is just very hard, and everyone in that family would be poorer for it.

Trade is not a topic about shipping containers. It is what happens whenever two people divide tasks between them. The economics of two castaways and the economics of two countries are the same economics, which is why this module can teach both in eight pages.

Watch out. Trade is not the same thing as money. A trade is an exchange of goods or services in both directions; money makes it convenient but it is not what makes it a trade. Tom and Hank never use any.

Textbook

The self-sufficient family. Growing your own food, making your own clothes and writing your own textbooks. Your book's verdict: “trying to live that way would be very hard.”

Numeric

Two castaways, four numbers. Tom can catch 40 fish a week or gather 30 coconuts. Hank can manage 10 fish or 20 coconuts. Those four numbers are the whole of this module — everything else is arithmetic on them.

Illustrative

A doctor and a pilot. Your book's example. A doctor could learn to fly and a pilot could learn medicine, but neither would be as good as the specialist. Each is better off doing one thing and buying the other.

How it connects. Trade is only worth doing because opportunity costs (entry 5) differ. It is the alternative to self-sufficiency (entry 2), and what it produces is gains from trade (entry 3).

Check yourself

1.1 Why is a market economy a precondition for specialising?

Show answer

Because you will only stop producing something for yourself if you are confident you can buy it. Your book makes the point with the doctor and the pilot: each specialises because a market exists where the other's service can be found.

1.2 Does a trade require money?

Show answer

No. Tom gives Hank fish and Hank gives Tom coconuts. Money makes trade easier by removing the need to find someone who wants exactly what you have, but the exchange is the trade.

02

Self-sufficiency

★ Exam
Definition

A producer is self-sufficient when it consumes only what it produces itself. It is the no-trade baseline — the thing every gain in this module is measured against.

Textbook pages 23 and 27.

This entry exists to give you something to compare with. Every claim in Module 4 has the form “better than under self-sufficiency”, so you need to know what the self-sufficient outcome actually was.

For the castaways it is stated exactly: without trade Tom consumes 28 fish and 9 coconuts; Hank consumes 6 fish and 8 coconuts. Write those down before you compute anything, because the gains are differences from them.

Note what self-sufficiency does not mean. It does not mean producing badly, and it does not mean being on the wrong side of your curve. Both castaways are producing efficiently — on their own curves — and they are still worse off than they need to be.

Watch out. Self-sufficiency is not inefficiency. A self-sufficient producer can be perfectly efficient in production and still be beaten by trade. That is the surprise the module is built on: being on your curve is not the best you can do once someone else exists.

Textbook

The two starting points. Tom: 28 fish, 9 coconuts. Hank: 6 fish, 8 coconuts. Both on their own curves, neither trading.

Numeric

Check they are on the curves. Tom's curve is coconuts = 30 − ¾ × fish, so at 28 fish it allows 30 − 21 = 9 — exactly what he consumes. Hank's is coconuts = 20 − 2 × fish, so at 6 fish it allows 20 − 12 = 8. Both are producing efficiently.

Illustrative

Cooking every meal yourself. You can do it, and do it well, and still eat worse than someone who works an extra hour and buys dinner from a cook. Nothing you did was wasteful.

How it connects. It is what trade (entry 1) replaces, and the baseline for measuring gains from trade (entry 3). In Module 3 language, a self-sufficient producer is stuck on its own curve.

Check yourself

2.1 Tom consumes 28 fish and 9 coconuts without trade. Is he being inefficient?

Show answer

No. His curve allows 30 − ¾(28) = 9 coconuts at 28 fish, so he is exactly on it. He is efficient in production and still worse off than he will be after trading.

2.2 Why do you need the no-trade numbers before you can talk about gains?

Show answer

Because a gain is a difference. “Tom gains 2 fish” only means something against the 28 he had before.

03

Gains from trade

★ Exam
Definition

There are gains from trade: by dividing tasks and trading, people can each get more of what they want than they could by being self-sufficient.

Textbook page 23, and Table 4.2 on page 25.

The claim is stronger than it first sounds. It is not that trade makes the pair better off on average, or that the winner gains more than the loser loses. Both parties end up with more of both goods. There is no loser in the model.

Table 4.2 on page 25 is the whole argument in one table. Tom specialises in fish and catches 40, giving 10 to Hank. Hank specialises in coconuts and gathers 20, giving 10 to Tom. Then:

Without tradeWith tradeGain
Tom · fish2830+2
Tom · coconuts910+1
Hank · fish610+4
Hank · coconuts810+2

Nothing was invented and nobody worked harder. The same two people, the same hours, the same skills — and four more fish and three more coconuts exist than before, purely because each of them stopped doing the thing he was comparatively bad at.

Watch out. Gains from trade do not come from one party being better at everything. Tom is better at both goods and still gains. Where the gains come from is different opportunity costs, which is entry 7.

Textbook

Your book's own summary. “By dividing tasks and trading, two people (or 7 billion people) can each get more of what they want than they could get by being self-sufficient.”

Numeric

Add up the total. Before: 28 + 6 = 34 fish and 9 + 8 = 17 coconuts. After: 30 + 10 = 40 fish and 10 + 10 = 20 coconuts. The pair gains 6 fish and 3 coconuts from the same resources.

Illustrative

Two students revising. One is quicker at algebra, the other at essays. Each teaches the other their strong subject for an hour instead of struggling alone for two. Both learn more in the same time.

How it connects. They are produced by specialisation (entry 4), made possible by differing comparative advantage (entry 7), and their size depends on the terms of trade (entry 10). They are measured against self-sufficiency (entry 2).

Check yourself

3.1 Total fish produced rose from 34 to 40 with no extra effort. Where did the 6 fish come from?

Show answer

From Hank stopping fishing. His 6 fish cost him 12 coconuts of forgone gathering; Tom's extra fish cost much less. Moving fish production to the low-cost producer is what created them.

3.2 A commentator says trade must have a winner and a loser. What does Table 4.2 show?

Show answer

That both parties gained in both goods — Tom +2 fish and +1 coconut, Hank +4 fish and +2 coconuts. A voluntary trade is refused by anyone it would make worse off, which is why both columns are positive.

04

Specialization

★ Exam
Definition

Specialization is a situation in which different people each engage in a different task — each person concentrating on the one thing they are comparatively good at.

Textbook pages 23–24, with the Adam Smith pin factory.

Specialisation is the action; gains from trade are the result. Keep them separate in a written answer, because the exam asks about the mechanism: specialisation according to comparative advantage is what produces the gain.

Your textbook opens with Adam Smith's 1776 pin factory, where ten workers each doing one of eighteen operations made “upwards of forty-eight thousand pins in a day” — against perhaps twenty each if every worker made whole pins alone.

Watch out. The pin factory and comparative advantage are two different mechanisms, and only the second is what this topic tests. Smith's workers gain from practice and from not switching tasks. Tom and Hank gain purely because their opportunity costs differ — no learning, no practice, no economies of scale. Use the pin factory as a hook, then drop it.

Textbook

The pin factory. Ten workers, eighteen operations, 48,000 pins a day. Working separately: perhaps twenty pins each. The gain comes from splitting the task.

Numeric

Specialisation in the castaway model. Tom goes from 28 fish and 9 coconuts to 40 fish and 0 coconuts; Hank from 6 and 8 to 0 fish and 20 coconuts. Complete specialisation, at the two corners of their curves.

Illustrative

A restaurant kitchen. One person on the grill, one on sauces, one plating. The same three people produce far more covers than three cooks each making whole dishes.

How it connects. It is what creates gains from trade (entry 3), and it should follow comparative advantage (entry 7) rather than absolute advantage (entry 6). It requires trade (entry 1) to be possible at all — nobody specialises with no one to trade with.

Check yourself

4.1 In Table 4.2 both castaways specialise completely. Where are they on their PPCs?

Show answer

At the corners: Tom at 40 fish and 0 coconuts, Hank at 0 fish and 20 coconuts. With straight-line curves and differing opportunity costs, complete specialisation is the usual result.

4.2 Why is the pin factory not an example of comparative advantage?

Show answer

Because its gain comes from splitting a task and from practice, not from workers having different opportunity costs. Comparative advantage works even when nobody gets better at anything.

05

Opportunity cost as a ratio

★ Exam
Definition

On a straight-line PPC, the opportunity cost of one unit of a good is the ratio of the two intercepts — and it is the same everywhere on the curve.

Textbook pages 24–25, Figure 4.1 and Table 4.1. Topic 1.3 supplies the method.

Everything from here on is arithmetic on this one ratio, so get it fluent now. If a producer can make at most a of good A or at most b of good B, then

1 unit of A costs b ÷ a units of B   ·   1 unit of B costs a ÷ b units of A

The two are reciprocals, and you will need both directions in almost every question. Table 4.1 on page 25 sets them out for the castaways:

Tom's opportunity costHank's opportunity cost
One fish¾ coconut2 coconuts
One coconut4⁄3 fish½ fish

Tom: 30 ÷ 40 = ¾ coconut per fish, and 40 ÷ 30 = 4⁄3 fish per coconut. Hank: 20 ÷ 10 = 2 coconuts per fish, and 10 ÷ 20 = ½ fish per coconut. Four numbers, two divisions each.

Watch out. Always say what is being costed and in what. A bare “0.75” is not an answer — ¾ of a coconut per fish and 4⁄3 fish per coconut are both true of the same producer, and an unlabelled number could be either. This is where marks are lost.

Textbook

Figure 4.1's two slopes. Tom's curve has slope −¾; Hank's has slope −2. The steeper the curve, the more of the vertical good each unit of the horizontal good costs.

Numeric

Do it on the exam graph. Country A can make 150 bushels of wheat or 100 units of textiles. One bushel costs 100 ÷ 150 = ⅔ of a unit of textiles. One unit of textiles costs 150 ÷ 100 = 1½ bushels.

Illustrative

Exchange rates. If £1 buys $1.25, then $1 buys £0.80. Nobody quotes one without knowing which direction they mean; opportunity cost is the same.

How it connects. The method is Topic 1.3, from Module 3. Here it becomes the test for comparative advantage (entry 7) and the boundary of the terms of trade (entry 10).

Check yourself

5.1 A country can produce 500 tonnes of steel or 2,000 tonnes of grain. What is the opportunity cost of one tonne of steel, and of one tonne of grain?

Show answer

One tonne of steel costs 2,000 ÷ 500 = 4 tonnes of grain. One tonne of grain costs 500 ÷ 2,000 = ¼ tonne of steel. Reciprocals, and both need their units.

5.2 Why does a straight-line PPC make this so much easier than a bowed one?

Show answer

Because the slope is the same everywhere, so one ratio describes the whole curve. On a bowed curve the opportunity cost changes as you move, and you have to say where.

06

Absolute advantage

★ Exam
Definition

A producer has an absolute advantage in a good if it can make more of it with a given amount of time and resources than any other producer. Having an absolute advantage is not the same as having a comparative advantage.

Textbook page 27, margin definition.

This is the intuitive idea, and in this topic it is mostly a distractor. It compares quantities: who produces more? Tom can catch more fish than Hank and gather more coconuts than Hank, so Tom has an absolute advantage in both.

Your textbook's own margin definition ends with the warning, which tells you how often students get this wrong. The exam's framework writes the correction into the required knowledge itself: specialisation should follow comparative advantage, not absolute advantage.

Watch out. An absolute advantage in both goods does not mean there is nothing to gain from trade. Tom is better at both and still gains. If a question asks who should produce what, absolute advantage is not the tool — entry 7 is.

Textbook

Tom's position. 40 fish against 10, and 30 coconuts against 20. “Tom has an absolute advantage in both activities: he can produce more output with a given amount of input.”

Numeric

Read it off the exam graph. Country A: 150 bushels of wheat or 100 units of textiles. Country B: 100 bushels or 200 units. A has the absolute advantage in wheat (150 > 100); B has it in textiles (200 > 100). Compare the two numbers for the same good — nothing else.

Illustrative

Bangladesh and the United States. Your book's FYI on page 27: almost every industry in Bangladesh is less productive than its American counterpart. The United States has an absolute advantage in nearly everything — and most of the world's shirts are still made elsewhere.

How it connects. It is the comparison this module warns you against using. The one you want is comparative advantage (entry 7). Both are read off the same numbers, and which way the table runs (entry 9) decides whether bigger or smaller wins.

Check yourself

6.1 Country X makes 900 cars or 300 ships. Country Y makes 400 cars or 200 ships. Who has the absolute advantage in each good?

Show answer

X in both: 900 > 400 cars and 300 > 200 ships. Note that this settles nothing about who should make what.

6.2 Why does your textbook's definition end with a warning?

Show answer

Because assuming absolute advantage determines who should specialise is the standard error in this topic, and the framework says so explicitly: specialisation follows comparative advantage, not absolute advantage.

07

Comparative advantage

★ Exam
Definition

A producer has a comparative advantage in a good if the opportunity cost of producing it is lower for that producer than for others.

Textbook page 26, margin definition. This is the centre of the module.

Comparative advantage compares costs, not quantities. It is the answer to “who should produce this?”, and it is the one thing in this module that the exam will ask you to determine over and over.

From Table 4.1: a fish costs Tom ¾ of a coconut and costs Hank 2 coconuts, so Tom has the comparative advantage in fish. A coconut costs Tom 4⁄3 fish and costs Hank ½ a fish, so Hank has the comparative advantage in coconuts — even though he is worse at gathering them in absolute terms.

There is a shortcut worth knowing. On a graph with the same two axes, the producer with the flatter PPC has the comparative advantage in the good on the horizontal axis. That follows straight from Module 3: the slope is the opportunity cost of the horizontal good.

Watch out. Comparative advantage is never “both” and never “neither”, unless the two producers have identical opportunity costs — in which case there is no comparative advantage anywhere and no gains from trade at all. Otherwise each producer has exactly one.

Textbook

The definition, and Hank. “Hank has a comparative advantage in gathering coconuts and Tom has a comparative advantage in catching fish.” Hank gathers fewer coconuts than Tom and still has the advantage.

Numeric

The exam graph, both directions. A bushel of wheat costs Country A ⅔ of a textile unit and costs Country B 200 ÷ 100 = 2 units. A has the comparative advantage in wheat. A unit of textiles costs A 1½ bushels and costs B ½ a bushel, so B has it in textiles.

Illustrative

A surgeon and the filing. A surgeon may type faster than her receptionist. She still should not do the filing: an hour of filing costs her an hour of surgery, and costs the receptionist far less.

How it connects. It is measured with opportunity cost as a ratio (entry 5) and is the correct basis for specialisation (entry 4). Do not confuse it with absolute advantage (entry 6). Its mirror image is comparative disadvantage (entry 8).

Check yourself

7.1 A fish costs Tom ¾ of a coconut and costs Hank 2 coconuts. Who should catch the fish, and why is that not because he is the better fisherman?

Show answer

Tom, because each fish he catches sacrifices only ¾ of a coconut against Hank's 2. Being the better fisherman is absolute advantage; what settles it is that fish are cheaper for him in coconuts forgone.

7.2 Two countries have identical opportunity costs in both goods. What happens?

Show answer

Neither has a comparative advantage in anything, and there are no gains from trade. Specialising would reshuffle who produces what without making either better off.

08

Comparative disadvantage

★ Exam
Definition

As long as opportunity costs differ, everyone has a comparative advantage in something and a comparative disadvantage in something.

Textbook page 27.

This is the reassuring half of the theory and it is quotable almost verbatim, so learn the sentence: “as long as people have different opportunity costs, everyone has a comparative advantage in something, and everyone has a comparative disadvantage in something.”

It follows from arithmetic rather than from fairness. If a fish is cheaper for Tom in coconuts, then a coconut must be dearer for Tom in fish — the two ratios are reciprocals, so they cannot both be lower. Nobody can hold a comparative advantage in everything, however productive they are.

Watch out. “Comparative disadvantage” is not “bad at it”. Tom has a comparative disadvantage in coconuts while being better at gathering them than Hank. It means only that coconuts cost him more, in forgone fish, than they cost Hank.

Textbook

Tom's disadvantage. “Meanwhile Tom, who can use his time better by catching fish, has a comparative disadvantage in coconut-gathering” — despite gathering more of them than Hank can.

Numeric

Why it cannot go both ways. A fish costs Tom ¾ coconut and Hank 2, so Tom is cheaper in fish. Invert both: a coconut costs Tom 4⁄3 fish and Hank ½ a fish, so Hank is cheaper in coconuts. Lower one way forces higher the other.

Illustrative

Every team. In any pair of people with different strengths, each is the cheaper choice for one job. There is no arrangement in which one person should do everything.

How it connects. It is the arithmetic mirror of comparative advantage (entry 7), and the reason a producer with an absolute disadvantage in everything still has something to sell — which is what makes trade between countries (entry 12) work.

Check yourself

8.1 Can a producer have a comparative advantage in both of two goods? Explain using ratios.

Show answer

No. The two opportunity costs are reciprocals of each other, so if one is lower than the rival's the other must be higher. Being lower in both is arithmetically impossible.

8.2 Bangladesh has an absolute disadvantage in almost every industry. Why does it still export clothing?

Show answer

Because the productivity gap is much smaller in clothing than in sophisticated goods such as aircraft. Clothing is what it does comparatively well, so clothing is its comparative advantage.

09

Output per worker against input per unit

★ Exam
Definition

Data can be given as output per input (bushels per worker) or as input per output (workers per car). Which way the table runs decides whether a bigger number is better.

Textbook page 29, Check Your Understanding 1 and Free-response 2. Topic 1.4, LO MKT-2.A(b).

The framework requires you to determine advantage “using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate”, and your textbook's review gives you tables running in opposite directions on facing pages. A student who has memorised a procedure instead of the definition gets one of them backwards.

ItemWhat the numbers areAbsolute advantage goes to
Check Your Understanding 1Italy: an automobile takes 8 workers/day, a washing machine 3 workers/daythe smaller number
Free-response 2Country A: 15 bushels of wheat per worker per week, 60 units of textilesthe larger number

Before any arithmetic, ask one question: is this output per input, or input per output? Then compute the opportunity cost as a ratio within one producer's own column and compare that ratio across producers. Never compare raw numbers across producers to get comparative advantage.

Watch out. The two formats are reciprocals of one another. 15 bushels per worker is the same fact as 1⁄15 of a worker per bushel. Converting is legitimate; forgetting which one you were handed is what costs the mark.

Textbook

The output-per-worker table. Free-response 2: Country A makes 15 bushels of wheat or 60 units of textiles per worker per week; Country B makes 10 bushels or 60 units.

Numeric

Work that table right through. A: one bushel costs 60 ÷ 15 = 4 units of textiles. B: one bushel costs 60 ÷ 10 = 6 units. So A has the comparative advantage in wheat. Inverting: a unit of textiles costs A ¼ bushel and costs B 1⁄6 bushel, so B has the comparative advantage in textiles — even though the two make textiles equally fast.

Illustrative

Minutes per kilometre. A runner's speed can be quoted as kilometres per hour or minutes per kilometre. Faster means a bigger number in the first and a smaller number in the second. Same runner, same fact.

How it connects. It is the practical side of opportunity cost as a ratio (entry 5), and the commonest way to get absolute (entry 6) and comparative advantage (entry 7) the wrong way round.

Check yourself

9.1 In Italy an automobile takes 8 workers a day and a washing machine 3. In the United States an automobile takes 6 and a washing machine 2. Who has the absolute advantage in each good, and who has the comparative advantage in washing machines?

Show answer

The United States has the absolute advantage in both — 6 < 8 and 2 < 3, and fewer workers is better here. For comparative advantage, work within each country: one washing machine costs Italy 3⁄8 = 0.375 of an automobile and costs the United States 2⁄6 ≈ 0.333. So the United States has the comparative advantage in washing machines, and Italy in automobiles (8⁄3 ≈ 2.67 washing machines against 3).

9.2 A table says “workers needed per tonne”. Is a bigger number better or worse?

Show answer

Worse. Needing more workers per tonne means being less productive. Check the heading before you compare anything.

10

Terms of trade

★ ⚑
Definition

The terms of trade are the rate at which the two goods are exchanged — the “price” of one good in units of the other. A trade is mutually beneficial only if that rate lies between the two producers' opportunity costs.

The phrase does not appear in your textbook. The reasoning is on pages 26–27. Topic 1.4, MKT-2.B.2.

Your textbook teaches this properly and never names it, so the label has to come from here. The reasoning on page 26 is exactly right: Hank “will not accept any deal with Tom in which he must give up more than 2 coconuts per fish — otherwise, he's better off not trading at all.” And Tom “will reject a deal that requires him to give up more than 4⁄3 of a fish per coconut.”

Turn that into a range and the calculation becomes mechanical:

Tom's costHank's costMutually beneficial range
1 fish¾ coconut2 coconutsbetween ¾ and 2 coconuts
1 coconut4⁄3 fish½ fishbetween ½ and 4⁄3 fish

The seller will not accept less than its own opportunity cost; the buyer will not pay more than its own. So the deal has to sit strictly between them. Write the range first, then test the proposed deal against it — that is precisely what the exam asks you to calculate.

Watch out. The boundaries themselves are not mutually beneficial. At exactly ¾ of a coconut per fish, Tom is indifferent and gains nothing; at exactly 2, Hank gains nothing. A deal at a boundary gives the entire gain to one side, which is why the framework's wording is about a range rather than a number.

Textbook

The deal your book rules out. 15 coconuts for 5 fish is 3 coconuts per fish — above Hank's own cost of 2, so he refuses. Your book rejects it for exactly this reason without using the phrase.

Numeric

Test three deals against the range ¾ to 2. 10 fish for 10 coconuts is 1 coconut per fish — inside, so both accept. 1 fish for 1½ coconuts is 1.5 — inside, so both accept. 5 fish for 15 coconuts is 3 — outside, so Hank refuses.

Illustrative

Haggling. A seller has a price below which he will not sell and a buyer has a price above which she will not buy. If the two overlap there is a deal, and where in the overlap it lands decides who gets more of the gain.

How it connects. The boundaries are the two comparative advantage (entry 7) opportunity costs from entry 5. Where inside the range a deal falls decides how the gains from trade (entry 3) are split — but any deal inside it makes both sides better off, which is why it puts consumption beyond the PPC (entry 11).

Check yourself

10.1 Why are Tom and Hank both willing to trade 1 fish for 1½ coconuts?

Show answer

Because 1.5 lies between ¾ and 2. Hank pays 1.5 coconuts for a fish that would have cost him 2 to catch, so he saves ½ a coconut. Tom receives 1.5 coconuts for a fish that cost him only ¾ of a coconut, so he gains ¾. Both sides beat their own cost.

10.2 Country A's wheat costs ⅔ of a textile unit; Country B's costs 2 units. What is the highest price B will pay for a bushel, and the lowest A will accept?

Show answer

B will pay at most 2 units — above that it is cheaper to grow its own. A will accept no less than ⅔ of a unit. Any price strictly between the two works.

11

Consumption possibilities beyond the PPC

★ ⚑
Definition

Specialising according to comparative advantage and trading lets a producer consume outside its own production possibilities curve — a combination it could never have produced alone.

Shown in Figure 4.2 on page 26; the phrase is never used. Topic 1.4, MKT-2.B.1.

This is the strongest statement in the module and your textbook makes a weaker one. It says each castaway “can consume more of both goods than either could without trade” — true, but that only means more than before. The examinable claim is sharper: consumption moves beyond the curve, to a point production could not reach.

Prove it with the numbers from Table 4.2 rather than taking it on trust:

Own PPCConsumes with tradeBest possible alone at that many fishBeyond?
Tom40 fish or 30 coconuts30 fish, 10 coconuts30 − ¾(30) = 7.5 coconutsyes
Hank10 fish or 20 coconuts10 fish, 10 coconuts20 − 2(10) = 0 coconutsyes

Hank's case is the vivid one. Producing 10 fish would use his entire week, leaving him nothing to gather coconuts with. With trade he has 10 fish and 10 coconuts. The dashed lines below show each gap:

Each castaway's consumption with trade sits above his own curve. Production stays on the curve; only consumption escapes it.

Watch out. It is consumption that goes beyond the curve, never production. Tom still produces a point on his PPC — the corner, 40 fish and 0 coconuts. If a question asks you to label a production point after trade, it is on the curve; the consumption point is outside.

Textbook

Figure 4.2. Both consumption-with-trade points are plotted outside the curve they belong to. The caption stops at “more of both goods”, which is the weaker claim.

Numeric

Hank, in one line. At 10 fish his own curve allows 0 coconuts. With trade he consumes 10. There is no way to produce that bundle; there is a way to consume it.

Illustrative

A single household. You consume food you could not grow, in a house you could not build, using a phone you could not make. Your consumption sits far outside anything you could produce yourself — that is this entry, applied to a whole life.

How it connects. It is the payoff from specialising (entry 4) at terms of trade (entry 10) inside the range. It also qualifies Module 3’s rule that outside the curve is unattainable: that is true of production, and trade is the exception for consumption.

Check yourself

11.1 Tom consumes 30 fish and 10 coconuts. His curve allows 7.5 coconuts at 30 fish. What does that show, and what is he actually producing?

Show answer

That his consumption bundle lies outside his own PPC — 10 > 7.5, so he could not have produced it. He is producing 40 fish and 0 coconuts, a point on his curve, and trading 10 fish for 10 coconuts.

11.2 Module 3 said points outside the curve are unattainable. Is that now wrong?

Show answer

No — it is still exactly right about production. What trade changes is what you can consume. The curve still limits what this producer can make.

12

Comparative advantage between countries

★ Exam
Definition

The same model applies to countries. On a graph with the same axes, the country with the flatter PPC has the comparative advantage in the good on the horizontal axis.

Textbook pages 28–29, Figure 4.3.

Nothing new is required here — that is the point. Your textbook's Figure 4.3 puts pork on the horizontal axis and aircraft on the vertical, draws a flatter curve for the United States and a steeper one for Canada, and concludes that the United States has the comparative advantage in pork and Canada in aircraft.

Why the flatter curve wins is Module 3 arithmetic: the slope of a straight-line PPC is the opportunity cost of the horizontal good. A flatter curve means each extra unit of that good costs less of the vertical one.

And the reassurance from entry 8 carries over to countries in full: “even if one country has an absolute advantage in both industries — there are still mutual gains from trade.”

Watch out. Do not conclude that the richer or more productive country should produce everything. That is absolute advantage again. Bangladesh is less productive in almost every industry and still exports clothing, because the productivity gap is smaller there than in aircraft.

Textbook

Pork and aircraft. “The U.S. production possibilities curve is flatter than the Canadian … implying that producing one more ton of pork costs fewer aircraft in the United States than it does in Canada.”

Numeric

Two countries, four numbers. Country A can grow 800 bushels of corn or build 200 computers; Country B, 400 bushels or 500 computers. A bushel costs A 200 ÷ 800 = ¼ of a computer and costs B 500 ÷ 400 = 1¼ computers. A has the comparative advantage in corn, B in computers.

Illustrative

Reading the labels. Your book's suggestion: check where your clothes were made. A country worse at making almost everything makes a large share of the world's shirts, and comparative advantage is the whole explanation.

How it connects. It is comparative advantage (entry 7) with countries in place of people, read off a slope as in entry 5. It determines who imports and who exports (entry 13).

Check yourself

12.1 Two countries produce rice and steel, rice on the horizontal axis. Country P's PPC is flatter than Country Q's. Who has the comparative advantage in rice?

Show answer

Country P. A flatter curve means each tonne of rice costs less steel, which is exactly a lower opportunity cost of the horizontal good.

12.2 A country has an absolute advantage in both goods. Should it still trade?

Show answer

Yes. Your textbook says so directly: mutual gains do not depend on either country being better at producing one kind of good. What matters is that the two opportunity costs differ.

13

Imports and exports

★ Exam
Definition

After specialising, a producer exports the good in which it has the comparative advantage and imports the other one.

Textbook pages 28–29, and multiple-choice 4. The welfare analysis of trade is Topic 2.9.

This is a one-line inference, and it is worth a mark on almost every trade question. Find the comparative advantage; that good is the export. The other good is the import. Nothing else is needed.

From the exam graph: Country A has the comparative advantage in wheat, so A exports wheat and imports textiles. Country B has it in textiles, so B exports textiles and imports wheat.

Watch out. This module explains the direction of trade and nothing more. It gives you no world price, no quantity imported read off a supply-and-demand diagram, no tariff and no surplus analysis. All of that is Topic 2.9, and it comes from a different model later in the course. Do not try to answer a Topic 2.9 question with a PPC.

Textbook

The general principle. “If the United States concentrates on producing pork and ships some of its output to Canada, while Canada concentrates on aircraft … both countries can consume more.”

Numeric

Complete the pair. Country A: comparative advantage in wheat → exports wheat, imports textiles. Country B: comparative advantage in textiles → exports textiles, imports wheat. The two answers are always mirror images.

Illustrative

The clothing label again. Bangladesh exports shirts and imports aircraft. Not because it cannot make anything else, but because shirts are what it gives up least to produce.

How it connects. It follows directly from comparative advantage between countries (entry 12) and from specialisation (entry 4). The consumption gain it produces is beyond the PPC (entry 11).

Check yourself

13.1 Country A has the comparative advantage in wheat, Country B in textiles. What does each import?

Show answer

A imports textiles; B imports wheat. Each imports the good it does not have the comparative advantage in.

13.2 A question gives you a domestic supply-and-demand diagram and a world price, and asks how much is imported. Can you answer it from this module?

Show answer

No. This module gives the direction of trade, not the quantity. The world-price model is Topic 2.9 and uses supply and demand rather than a PPC.

Tackle the test — free response

Your textbook's Module 4 free-response questions are the best practice in Unit 1, because together they use both data formats the exam allows — a graph and a table. Write full answers before revealing the rubrics.

1. Country A can produce 800 bushels of corn or 200 computers. Country B can produce 400 bushels of corn or 500 computers.

  1. What is the opportunity cost of a bushel of corn in each country?
  2. Which country has an absolute advantage in computer production? Explain.
  3. Which country has a comparative advantage in corn production? Explain.
  4. If each country specialises, what good will Country B import? Explain.
  5. What is the minimum price Country A will accept to export corn to Country B? Explain.

9 points, as printed in your textbook

Show the rubric
  • 1 ptCountry A: ¼ of a computer per bushel (200 ÷ 800). Country B: 1¼ computers per bushel (500 ÷ 400).
  • 1 ptCountry B has the absolute advantage in computers.
  • 1 ptBecause B can produce more computers than A — 500 against 200.
  • 1 ptCountry A has the comparative advantage in corn.
  • 1 ptBecause A produces corn at a lower opportunity cost — ¼ against 1¼ computers.
  • 1 ptCountry B will import corn.
  • 1 ptBecause B has the comparative advantage in computers, so it specialises in computers and imports corn.
  • 1 pt¼ of a computer per bushel.
  • 1 ptBecause A's own opportunity cost of corn is ¼ of a computer, so that is the lowest price it will accept.

Two things to notice. Parts (b) and (c) each award a mark for the answer and a second for the justification, and the two justifications are different kinds of sentence — output for absolute advantage, opportunity cost for comparative. Part (e) is a terms-of-trade boundary: the whole mutually beneficial range here is between ¼ and 1¼ computers per bushel, and the rubric asks for the bottom of it.

The five things to get right from Module 4

  1. Comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, decides who produces what. The framework writes the correction into the requirement itself, and your textbook's own definition of absolute advantage ends with the same warning.
  2. State opportunity cost with its units, both ways round. “¾ of a coconut per fish” and “4⁄3 fish per coconut” are the same fact; a bare 0.75 is not an answer.
  3. Ask which way a table runs before you compare anything. Output per worker — bigger is better. Workers per unit — smaller is better.
  4. Write the terms-of-trade range first, then test the proposed deal against it. The price of a good must fall between the two producers' opportunity costs of that good.
  5. Consumption goes beyond the PPC; production does not. After trade each producer still produces a point on its own curve, and consumes one outside it.
