Interpreting Price Elasticity of Demand

Eighteen terms from Module 47 of Krugman’s Economics for AP. Module 46 gave you a number. This one tells you what the number means: whether a price rise makes a seller richer or poorer, and why the answer changes as you move along the curve.

Textbook pages 466–474·18 terms· Topic 2.3·2 periods

How to use this guide

Read Module 47 in your textbook first. Entries 1 to 5 classify the number; entries 6 to 11 are what it does to revenue; entries 12 to 15 are how it changes along a demand curve; entries 16 to 18 are what decides it in the first place.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 47 of your textbook does not teach it. Entries 11 and 14 carry the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. A price rise always means more per unit and fewer units. The elasticity says which of the two wins: below 1 the price wins and revenue rises; above 1 the quantity wins and revenue falls; at exactly 1 they tie.

⚑ Two words your textbook never uses. Your course talks about the effect of a price change on “total revenue or total expenditure”, and says outright that slope is not elasticity. Module 47 teaches both ideas and prints neither phrase. Entries 11 and 14 supply them.

Demand is inelastic where revenue is still climbing and elastic where it has started to fall.

One price rise, three demand curves

Your textbook’s toll bridge. The toll goes up from $0.90 to $1.10 — a 20% rise by the midpoint method — in all three cases. The only difference is how many drivers leave.

What happens to the bridge’s revenue?

The same 20% price rise. Inelastic demand loses few drivers, so revenue rises; elastic demand loses many, so revenue falls; unit-elastic demand loses exactly enough to leave revenue where it was.

Elastic or inelastic?

Twelve cases. Decide from the numbers where you are given them, and from the four determinants where you are not: how many substitutes, necessity or luxury, how big a share of spending, and how much time has passed.

The eighteen terms

01

Perfectly inelastic demand

★ Exam
Definition

Quantity demanded does not respond to price at all. The elasticity is 0 and the demand curve is a vertical line.

Textbook page 466, Figure 47.1 panel (a).

Your textbook's example is shoelaces: suppose buyers take 1 billion pairs a year whatever the price. Double the price and they still take 1 billion. The percentage change in quantity is zero, so the elasticity is zero ÷ anything = 0.

Nothing in the real world is exactly like this, but some things come close: a life-saving medicine over a modest price range, an addictive product, a tiny purchase you barely notice.

Watch out. A vertical demand curve has an undefined (or infinite) slope and a zero elasticity. The two numbers are at opposite extremes, which is the clearest proof you will see that slope and elasticity are different things.

1

A snake-bite victim and the antidote. Your textbook's own review question. Any price, same quantity: one dose.

2

Insulin for a diabetic over an ordinary price range. Close to vertical. The quantity is set by the illness, not the price.

3

A 2p rise in the price of a box of matches. Effectively no response — too small to notice, with no substitute worth the trouble.

How it connects. It is one end of the scale whose other end is perfectly elastic demand (entry 2), it is the extreme case of inelastic demand (entry 4), and together with its opposite it is the cleanest proof of slope not being elasticity (entry 14).

Check yourself

1.1 A demand curve is vertical at 500 units. The price doubles. What is the elasticity?

Show answer

Zero. The percentage change in quantity demanded is 0, and 0 divided by any non-zero percentage change in price is 0.

1.2 Why can a perfectly inelastic demand curve not be the whole story for any real good?

Show answer

Because at a high enough price buyers run out of money or find some alternative. The vertical curve is a good description over a range of prices, not over all of them.

02

Perfectly elastic demand

★ Exam
Definition

Any rise in price drives quantity demanded to zero. The elasticity is infinite and the demand curve is a horizontal line.

Textbook page 467, Figure 47.1 panel (b).

Your textbook's example is pink tennis balls when green and yellow ones sell at $5 a dozen and nobody cares about colour. At $5 buyers will take any quantity of pink. At $5.01 they take none. At $4.99 they want nothing else.

Divide a percentage change in quantity by a percentage change in price of zero and you get infinity. That is what a horizontal demand curve means.

Watch out. Perfectly elastic demand is not “very responsive”. It is the case where the seller has no power over price at all — which is exactly the position of a single firm in a perfectly competitive market, as you will see later in the course.

1

Blue pencils, when every shop sells identical ones. Your textbook's review question. Charge a penny more and you sell none.

2

One farmer's wheat at the market price. The farmer can sell any amount at the going price and nothing above it.

3

A commodity traded on an exchange. Thousands of identical sellers, so each one faces a horizontal demand curve.

How it connects. It is the opposite extreme from perfectly inelastic demand (entry 1), it is the limiting case of elastic demand (entry 3), and the pair of them is the argument of slope not being elasticity (entry 14).

Check yourself

2.1 A horizontal demand curve sits at $5. What is its slope, and what is its elasticity?

Show answer

Slope zero; elasticity infinite. Compare that with the vertical curve, where the two numbers swap — the pair of cases is the argument of entry 14.

2.2 Why would a seller facing perfectly elastic demand never raise its price?

Show answer

Because it would sell nothing at all. Every buyer has a perfect substitute at the old price.

03

Elastic demand

★ Exam
Definition

The price elasticity of demand is greater than 1: quantity demanded moves proportionally more than price.

Textbook page 467, and Figure 47.2 panel (c).

Your textbook's toll bridge, panel (c): raise the toll from $0.90 to $1.10 — a 20% rise by the midpoint method — and crossings fall from 1,200 to 800, a 40% fall. The elasticity is 40 ÷ 20 = 2.

Buyers of an elastic good are responsive: they have somewhere else to go, they can wait, or the purchase is big enough to be worth rethinking.

Watch out. Elastic is about the proportions, not the size of the fall. Crossings fell by 400 in panel (c) and by 200 in panel (a), but what makes (c) elastic is that 40% is bigger than 20%.

1

The toll bridge, panel (c). 1,200 → 800 against a 20% toll rise: elasticity 2.

2

Foreign travel, at 4.1 in the Module 46 table. Deeply elastic — a holiday can always be postponed.

3

One brand of crisps among twenty. Close substitutes on the same shelf make almost any single brand elastic.

How it connects. It sits above the benchmark of 1 (entry 5) on the scale, it is the near neighbour of perfectly elastic demand (entry 2), and it is usually produced by a good having close substitutes (entry 16).

Check yourself

3.1 A 10% price rise cuts quantity demanded by 25%. Elastic or inelastic?

Show answer

Elastic: 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5, which is greater than 1.

3.2 Name two features that tend to make a good's demand elastic.

Show answer

Close substitutes, and being a luxury you can do without. A large share of your budget and plenty of time to adjust do the same — entries 16 to 18.

04

Inelastic demand

★ Exam
Definition

The price elasticity of demand is less than 1: quantity demanded moves proportionally less than price.

Textbook page 467, and Figure 47.2 panel (b).

The same toll bridge, panel (b): the same 20% rise cuts crossings from 1,050 to 950, a 10% fall. The elasticity is 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5.

Buyers of an inelastic good are stuck: no substitute, a necessity, a small purchase, or no time to rearrange their lives.

Watch out. Inelastic does not mean no response. It means a proportionally smaller one. Only perfectly inelastic means the quantity does not move at all — and that is entry 1, not this one.

1

The toll bridge, panel (b). 1,050 → 950 against a 20% toll rise: elasticity 0.5.

2

Eggs, at 0.1 in the Module 46 table. Barely responsive: no substitute for an egg and almost no money at stake.

3

Petrol in the week after a price rise. People still have to get to work. Give them two years and it is a different number — entry 18.

How it connects. It sits below the benchmark of 1 (entry 5), it shades into perfectly inelastic demand (entry 1) at the extreme, and it is what short-run demand (entry 18) turns into given long enough.

Check yourself

4.1 Quantity demanded falls 3% when price rises 12%. Elastic or inelastic?

Show answer

Inelastic: 3 ÷ 12 = 0.25, well under 1.

4.2 A student says demand for insulin is inelastic, so quantity demanded does not change when the price changes. What is wrong?

Show answer

The conclusion is too strong. Inelastic means the response is proportionally small, not zero. Zero response is perfectly inelastic.

05

The benchmark of 1, and unit-elastic demand

★ Exam
Definition

1 is the dividing line. Above it demand is elastic, below it inelastic, and at exactly 1 demand is unit elastic — the two percentage changes are proportional.

Textbook page 467, and Figure 47.2 panel (a).

Why 1? Because that is the point at which a percentage change in price produces exactly the same percentage change in quantity — and, as the next few entries show, the point at which a price change leaves total revenue untouched.

The toll bridge, panel (a): the 20% rise cuts crossings from 1,100 to 900, which is a 20% fall. Elasticity 20 ÷ 20 = 1.

Your course writes it unit elastic; your textbook writes unit-elastic. Both are accepted.

Watch out. Always compare the magnitude with 1, never the signed number. An elasticity reported as −2.5 is elastic, because 2.5 > 1.

1

Elasticity 0.8. Inelastic — below the line.

2

Elasticity 1.0. Unit elastic — exactly on it.

3

Elasticity 1.2. Elastic — above it. Housing, in the Module 46 table.

How it connects. It separates elastic (entry 3) from inelastic demand (entry 4), it decides what a price rise does to revenue (entry 9), and at exactly 1 it is where total revenue reaches its highest point (entry 15).

Check yourself

5.1 Rank these by responsiveness: 0.2, 1.0, 3.5.

Show answer

0.2 inelastic, 1.0 unit elastic, 3.5 elastic. Responsiveness rises with the number.

5.2 Why is the dividing line at 1 rather than at some other number?

Show answer

Because at 1 the two percentage changes are equal and opposite, so the revenue gained from the higher price exactly cancels the revenue lost from the smaller quantity.

06

Total revenue

★ Exam
Definition

The total value of sales: price × quantity sold.

Textbook page 468, Equation 47-1.

total revenue = price × quantity sold

At a toll of $0.90 with 1,100 crossings a day, the bridge takes $0.90 × 1,100 = $990 a day.

This is the number the whole module is about. Elasticity matters to a seller because it decides what happens to this one figure when the price moves.

Watch out. Total revenue is not profit. Nothing has been subtracted. A price rise that raises revenue can still leave a firm worse off if costs rise too — but that is Unit 3.

1

$0.90 × 1,100 crossings. $990 a day — your textbook's green rectangle.

2

$1.10 × 950 crossings. $1,045 a day: the inelastic case, where revenue rose.

3

$1.10 × 800 crossings. $880 a day: the elastic case, where revenue fell.

How it connects. It is drawn as a rectangle (entry 7), it is the thing a price rise (entry 9) moves, and the buyer's name for it is total expenditure (entry 11).

Check yourself

6.1 A shop sells 240 shirts at £25. What is its total revenue?

Show answer

£6,000. Price times quantity, nothing else.

6.2 Revenue rises after a price increase. Has the firm definitely made more profit?

Show answer

No. Revenue is not profit — costs have not been mentioned. Though if the firm sells fewer units, its costs have probably fallen too.

07

Total revenue as a rectangle

★ Exam
Definition

On a demand diagram, total revenue at any price is the area of the rectangle whose height is the price and whose width is the quantity demanded at that price.

Textbook page 469, Figure 47.3 panel (a).

Pick a point on the demand curve. Drop a line to the quantity axis and across to the price axis. The box you have made is total revenue.

That is why a price change has two effects on revenue: the rectangle gets taller and narrower at the same time. Your textbook shades the piece you gain and the piece you lose — which is the next entry.

Watch out. The rectangle's corner must sit on the demand curve. A box drawn to a price and a quantity that do not belong together is not any firm's revenue.

1

$0.90 and 1,100 crossings. A wide, short rectangle: $990.

2

$1.10 and 900 crossings. Taller and narrower — and, when demand is unit elastic, exactly the same area: $990.

3

$5 and 5 units on a straight-line demand curve. A 5 × 5 square: $25, the largest rectangle that curve allows — entry 15.

How it connects. It is the picture of total revenue (entry 6), it is cut into the two pieces of the price and quantity effects (entry 8), and the same area read from the other side is total expenditure (entry 11).

Check yourself

7.1 Two points on one demand curve give rectangles of equal area. What does that tell you?

Show answer

That demand is unit elastic over that stretch: the gain and the loss cancel.

7.2 Why is the revenue rectangle never drawn to a point off the demand curve?

Show answer

Because at a price off the curve that quantity would not actually be bought. The rectangle has to describe a transaction that happens.

08

The price effect and the quantity effect

○ Background
Definition

Your textbook's names for the two halves of a revenue change. A price rise means more per unit (the price effect) but fewer units (the quantity effect).

Textbook page 469, Figure 47.3 panel (b).

Raise the toll from $0.90 to $1.10 and crossings fall from 1,100 to 900.

  • Gained: the 900 drivers who stay now pay $0.20 more each — $0.20 × 900 = $180 a day. Your textbook calls this area C.
  • Lost: the 200 drivers who leave used to pay $0.90 each — $0.90 × 200 = $180 a day. Area A.

Here the two are equal, so revenue does not move. That is what unit-elastic means.

Watch out. These two names are your textbook's, not your course's — no released exam paper has ever used them. The reasoning is examinable and the labels are not, so explain the mechanism in your own words and do not worry about the vocabulary.

1

The 900 who stay pay $0.20 more. $180 gained.

2

The 200 who leave paid $0.90 each. $180 lost.

3

A price cut. The same two effects, both reversed: less per unit, more units.

How it connects. They are the two pieces of the revenue rectangle (entry 7), and which one wins is what the price-rise rule (entry 9) records.

Check yourself

8.1 A toll rise gains $220 from the drivers who stay and loses $160 from those who leave. Elastic or inelastic?

Show answer

Inelastic. The price effect beat the quantity effect, so revenue rose — which happens when the elasticity is below 1.

8.2 Why does a perfectly inelastic good have no quantity effect?

Show answer

Because no units are lost when the price rises. Revenue rises by the full price effect.

09

A price rise and total revenue

★ Exam
Definition

Inelastic, revenue rises. Elastic, revenue falls. Unit elastic, revenue is unchanged.

Textbook pages 470 and 471, Table 47.1.

Your textbook's three toll-bridge cases, all facing the same 20% rise from $0.90 to $1.10:

DemandElasticityCrossingsRevenue beforeRevenue after
Unit-elastic11,100 → 900$990$990
Inelastic0.51,050 → 950$945$1,045
Elastic21,200 → 800$1,080$880

One sentence to remember it: revenue moves with price when demand is inelastic, and against it when demand is elastic.

Watch out. Answer with both effects, not one. “Revenue rises because the price is higher” is half the argument and earns half the marks. Say which effect dominates and why.

1

A rail operator with elasticity 0.6 raises fares. Revenue rises: inelastic, so the price effect wins.

2

A cinema with elasticity 1.8 raises ticket prices. Revenue falls: elastic, so the quantity effect wins.

3

A toll with elasticity exactly 1. Revenue is unchanged, whatever the toll — your textbook's panel (a).

How it connects. It is the central use of the benchmark of 1 (entry 5), it is total revenue (entry 6) deciding which way the two effects (entry 8) go, it is the mirror image of a price fall (entry 10), and it applies just as much to what buyers spend (entry 11).

Check yourself

9.1 Demand is inelastic and a firm wants more revenue. Which way should the price move?

Show answer

Up. With inelastic demand, a higher price raises revenue, because quantity falls proportionally less than price rises.

9.2 A firm raises its price and revenue does not change. What is its elasticity over that range?

Show answer

Exactly 1 — unit elastic.

10

A price fall and total revenue

★ Exam
Definition

Reverse every arrow. Inelastic, revenue falls. Elastic, revenue rises. Unit elastic, revenue is unchanged.

Textbook pages 470–471.

The two effects are still there and still opposed; they have simply swapped roles. A lower price means less from each unit sold (the price effect now reduces revenue) and more units sold (the quantity effect now raises it).

This is the half students forget, and it is where the famous sales-and-discounts question lives: cutting prices only raises revenue if demand is elastic.

Watch out. Do not memorise four separate results. There are two: revenue follows the price when demand is inelastic, and follows the quantity when demand is elastic. Both directions fall out of that.

1

A streaming service with elastic demand cuts its price. Revenue rises: enough new subscribers to more than cover the lower price.

2

A water company with inelastic demand cuts its price. Revenue falls: hardly anyone uses more water, and everyone pays less.

3

A discount on a unit-elastic good. Revenue unchanged, exactly as for a rise.

How it connects. It is the price-rise rule (entry 9) read backwards, and together they are what the changing elasticity along a demand curve (entry 12) predicts.

Check yourself

10.1 A shop cuts prices by 20% and revenue falls. What does that tell you about demand?

Show answer

That it is inelastic over that range: the quantity gained was proportionally too small to replace the revenue given up.

10.2 Your textbook says a cartel can raise revenue by cutting output. How?

Show answer

Cutting output raises the price. If demand is inelastic, the higher price more than compensates for the smaller quantity, so revenue rises — the review question's case (d).

11

Total expenditure — the same number, from the buyer's side

★ ⚑
Definition

Total expenditure (or consumer spending) is what buyers pay in total: price × quantity. It is the same rectangle as total revenue, seen from the other end of the transaction.

Not in Module 47 — your textbook only ever calls it total revenue.

⚑ Not in Module 47 — learn it from here. Your course asks about the impact of a price change on “total revenue or total expenditure”. Module 47 uses the first phrase eight times and the second not at all — it appears twice in the whole book, both times in a different context. The idea is identical; only the word is missing.

Your course names both, side by side: the impact of a price change “on total revenue or total expenditure”. They are the same money. Every pound a seller receives is a pound a buyer spends.

So every rule in entries 9 and 10 works unchanged if you swap the words:

  • Demand inelastic, price rises → consumers spend more in total.
  • Demand elastic, price rises → consumers spend less in total.

Exam questions do ask it this way round. One asks whether consumer spending on potatoes rises when a price floor pushes the price up and the absolute value of the elasticity is 0.92 — 0.92 is below 1, so demand is inelastic, so spending rises.

Watch out. Do not treat “what happens to consumer spending?” as a new kind of question. It is the revenue question with the label changed.

1

Elasticity 0.92, price rises. Inelastic, so total expenditure rises — and so does the sellers' revenue. One event, two names.

2

Elasticity 3, price rises. Elastic, so buyers spend less in total even though each unit costs more.

3

A 10% rise in the price of bread. Bread is inelastic, so households' total spending on bread goes up.

How it connects. It is total revenue (entry 6) under another name, it is pictured by the same rectangle (entry 7), and it obeys the price-rise rule (entry 9) exactly.

Check yourself

11.1 The price of a good with elasticity 0.4 falls. What happens to total consumer spending on it?

Show answer

It falls. Demand is inelastic, so the quantity gained is proportionally too small to make up for the lower price — the same answer as for the seller's revenue.

11.2 Why is it the same number for buyers and sellers?

Show answer

Because it is the same transaction. Price times quantity is simultaneously what is received and what is paid.

12

Elasticity changes along the demand curve

★ Exam
Definition

On almost any demand curve, the elasticity at one point is different from the elasticity at another. An elasticity is always measured at a price, or over a range.

Textbook page 471, Figure 47.4.

Take the straight-line demand curve drawn above, where quantity demanded is 10 minus the price. Work out total revenue at every price and you get a hill: $0 at a price of zero, climbing to $25 at a price of $5, then falling back to $0 at a price of $10.

Revenue rising as price rises means demand is inelastic there. Revenue falling as price rises means demand is elastic there. One straight line, both behaviours.

So when an economist says “the price elasticity of demand for coffee is 0.25”, they mean at the current price.

Watch out. “Coffee is inelastic” is not a fact about coffee. It is a fact about coffee at a particular price. Move far enough up the demand curve and the same good becomes elastic.

1

The straight line, at a price of $1. Raising the price to $2 lifts revenue from $9 to $16. Inelastic down here.

2

The same line, at a price of $8. Raising the price to $9 cuts revenue from $16 to $9. Elastic up there.

3

The same line, at $5. Revenue is at its peak, $25. Unit elastic exactly here.

How it connects. It is why a price cut (entry 10) has different answers at different prices, it is mapped by the position on the curve (entry 13), and it is the evidence for slope not being elasticity (entry 14).

Check yourself

12.1 Your textbook says demand is inelastic from a price of $0 to $5 on this curve. How can you tell from the revenue column alone?

Show answer

Because revenue rises all the way from $0 to $25 as the price climbs from $0 to $5. Revenue moving with price is the signature of inelastic demand.

12.2 Is it possible for a demand curve to have the same elasticity everywhere?

Show answer

Yes, but only for special shapes — a horizontal line (infinite everywhere) or a vertical one (zero everywhere). For the straight sloping lines you will draw, the elasticity always varies.

13

Where the elastic and inelastic ranges sit

★ Exam
Definition

On a straight-line demand curve: elastic above the midpoint, unit elastic at the midpoint, inelastic below it.

Textbook page 471, Figure 47.4 and its upper panel.

The upper panel of your textbook's figure labels the curve in three bands — elastic at the top, unit-elastic in the middle, inelastic at the bottom — and the revenue hill below it says why.

The quickest way to find the midpoint: it is the price at which total revenue is highest. On the curve above, that is $5, where 5 units are bought and revenue is $25.

This is also why a seller should never choose a price in the inelastic range: raising the price would increase revenue and cut the quantity, so costs would fall too.

Watch out. High price means elastic, not inelastic. Students reason that expensive goods are necessities and get this exactly backwards — it is the top of the curve, where few units sell, that is elastic.

1

Price $9 on the straight line. Top of the curve, elastic. A price rise cuts revenue.

2

Price $5. The midpoint. Unit elastic, revenue at its maximum of $25.

3

Price $2. Bottom, inelastic. A price rise lifts revenue from $16 to $21.

How it connects. It is the map of the elasticity changing along the curve (entry 12), and it locates the revenue peak (entry 15).

Check yourself

13.1 On a straight-line demand curve from $12 down to $0, where is demand unit elastic?

Show answer

At $6, the midpoint — which is also where total revenue is at its maximum.

13.2 A firm is selling at a price in the inelastic range. What should it do, and why?

Show answer

Raise the price. Revenue goes up because demand is inelastic, and it sells fewer units, so its costs go down as well.

14

Slope is not elasticity

★ ⚑
Definition

A straight demand curve has one slope and every elasticity. The two are different measurements and they do not move together.

Not in Module 47 — your textbook proves it and never says it.

⚑ Not in Module 47 — learn it from here. Your course states this outright: elasticity varies along a linear demand curve, meaning slope is not elasticity. Your textbook demonstrates it with the figure above and then never draws the conclusion — and its own review question asks for the slope of a perfectly inelastic demand curve, which is exactly the confusion this sentence exists to prevent.

Hold the last two entries side by side. The line above is perfectly straight: its slope is the same at every point. Yet its elasticity runs from enormous at the top to almost nothing at the bottom.

The cleanest demonstration is the pair of extremes from entries 1 and 2:

CurveSlopeElasticity
Vertical (perfectly inelastic)undefined / infinite0
Horizontal (perfectly elastic)0infinite

The two numbers are at opposite extremes in both rows. They cannot be the same thing.

Watch out. Slope is a ratio of units — pounds per unit. Elasticity is a ratio of percentages, and has no units at all. That is why slope depends on how you measure the good and elasticity does not.

1

One straight line, two ends. Same slope throughout; elastic at the top, inelastic at the bottom.

2

A vertical demand curve. Slope undefined, elasticity zero. Opposite extremes.

3

Two demand curves crossing at one point. At the crossing they share a price and a quantity, so the flatter one is the more elastic there — which is the only case where comparing slopes is safe.

How it connects. It is the conclusion the changing elasticity along a curve (entry 12) is evidence for, and it is why the vertical curve (entry 1) and the horizontal one (entry 2) have their two numbers at opposite extremes.

Check yourself

14.1 Two goods have demand curves with the same slope. Must they have the same elasticity at the same price?

Show answer

No. Elasticity also depends on the price and quantity at that point, so two curves with identical slopes can have different elasticities.

14.2 Why is elasticity, and not slope, the measure economists use for responsiveness?

Show answer

Because it has no units. Slope changes if you switch from litres to pints or from pounds to pence; elasticity does not, so it can be compared across goods and countries.

15

Total revenue is highest where demand is unit elastic

★ Exam
Definition

Revenue climbs while demand is inelastic, peaks where the elasticity is 1, and falls once demand is elastic.

Textbook page 471, the lower panel of Figure 47.4.

Put the three rules together and the shape is forced. Below the midpoint, raising the price raises revenue. Above it, raising the price lowers revenue. So the top of the hill is exactly where the switch happens — at unit elasticity.

On the curve above, the peak is $25, at a price of $5 and a quantity of 5. Your textbook's own review question asks precisely this: total revenue is maximised when demand is unit-elastic.

Watch out. The peak is a revenue maximum, not a profit maximum. Firms care about profit, and you will see in Unit 3 that the profit-maximising price is almost never the revenue-maximising one.

1

The straight line's peak. $25 at a price of $5 — the largest rectangle the curve allows.

2

A price below the peak. Inelastic: raise the price and revenue climbs towards $25.

3

A price above the peak. Elastic: cut the price and revenue climbs back towards $25.

How it connects. It is where the elasticity equals 1 (entry 5), and it sits at the midpoint (entry 13) of the straight line.

Check yourself

15.1 A firm wants to maximise revenue and knows its elasticity is 0.4. What should it do?

Show answer

Raise the price. Demand is inelastic, so revenue rises; it should keep raising until the elasticity reaches 1.

15.2 Why does revenue fall on both sides of the peak?

Show answer

Below it, you are giving up price for quantity that is not worth it. Above it, you are giving up quantity for price that is not worth it. Only at the peak do the two effects exactly balance.

16

Determinant 1 — the availability of substitutes

★ Exam
Definition

The more close substitutes a good has, the more elastic its demand. With no substitutes, demand is inelastic.

Textbook page 472.

This is the determinant your course names explicitly, and it is the one that explains most of the Module 46 table. Eggs at 0.1 have no substitute; one brand of crisps among twenty has nineteen.

It also explains the 2004 flu-vaccine story this unit opened with. There was no substitute for a flu shot, so distributors could raise the price a long way without losing many buyers.

Watch out. Substitutes are judged by the buyer, not the chemist. Two goods are substitutes if people are willing to swap them, however different they look.

1

Petrol. No substitute in the short run, which is why it is inelastic.

2

One airline's seats on a busy route. Several rivals flying the same route: very elastic.

3

Salt. No substitute, and nothing else does the job. Deeply inelastic.

How it connects. It is the strongest of the four determinants, it works alongside necessity and budget share (entry 17), and it is what makes elastic demand (entry 3) possible at all.

Check yourself

16.1 Why is demand for “cola” less elastic than demand for one brand of cola?

Show answer

Because the brand has close substitutes — all the other brands — while the category as a whole has far fewer.

16.2 A monopolist is the only seller of a good. What does that imply about elasticity?

Show answer

Its demand tends to be inelastic, because buyers have nowhere else to go. That is the connection to Unit 4.

17

Determinants 2 and 3 — necessity or luxury, and share of income

★ Exam
Definition

Necessities are inelastic; luxuries are elastic. And the larger the share of income a good absorbs, the more elastic its demand.

Textbook page 472.

Necessity or luxury. A life-saving medicine is something you must have, so the quantity barely moves with price. A cruise is something you can live without, so it moves a lot.

Share of income. If a good takes a trivial share of your spending, a price change hardly registers, and you do not bother to react. If it takes a large share, you are forced to react — which is the same budget-share logic that made the income effect large in Module 46.

Watch out. These two often point the same way but they are different tests. Rent is both a necessity and a huge share of income; the first makes it inelastic, the second makes it elastic. Which wins is an empirical question, and your textbook's own table puts housing at 1.2.

1

Insulin. A necessity with no substitute: as inelastic as goods get.

2

A second holiday abroad. A luxury: foreign travel is 4.1 in the Module 46 table.

3

Shoelaces at 50p. A trivial share of income — nobody shops around, so demand is inelastic.

How it connects. They sit alongside the availability of substitutes (entry 16), and they are joined by the effect of time (entry 18) as the fourth determinant.

Check yourself

17.1 Two goods are both necessities. One is 20% of a household's budget and the other 0.2%. Which has the more elastic demand?

Show answer

The one taking 20%. A price rise there is worth reacting to; a price rise on the other is not worth noticing.

17.2 Is salt a necessity? Does that settle its elasticity?

Show answer

It behaves like one, and that points to inelastic demand — but it is also a tiny share of spending, which points the same way. Here the two tests agree.

18

Determinant 4 — time

★ Exam
Definition

Demand becomes more elastic the longer buyers have to adjust. The long-run elasticity is usually larger than the short-run one.

Textbook pages 472–473.

Your textbook's example is the 1970s oil shocks. When petrol prices jumped, American consumption barely moved at first: people still had the same cars, the same jobs and the same commutes. Over the following decade consumption fell steadily — smaller cars, different journeys, different habits — even though the price had stopped rising.

Time lets buyers find substitutes that do not exist today. That is why this determinant works through the first one.

Watch out. Short run and long run here mean how long buyers have had to react, not how long the price stays changed. A price that jumps and stays put still gets a bigger response over time.

1

Petrol next week. Almost no response. You still have to get to work.

2

Petrol over ten years. A large response — a different car, possibly a different commute.

3

Electricity after a tariff rise. Little change this winter; insulation, appliances and habits change over years.

How it connects. It is the fourth determinant alongside necessity and budget share (entry 17), and it is how inelastic demand (entry 4) today becomes elastic demand tomorrow.

Check yourself

18.1 A government raises fuel duty and sees almost no fall in consumption in the first month. Does that mean the policy failed?

Show answer

Not yet. Short-run demand for fuel is inelastic; the response builds over years as people change vehicles and habits.

18.2 Why does time work through the availability of substitutes?

Show answer

Because given long enough, buyers find or invent alternatives that did not exist when the price first changed — and more substitutes means more elastic demand.

Things to get right

  • Compare with 1, in magnitude. Above 1 elastic, below 1 inelastic, exactly 1 unit elastic. An elasticity of −2.5 is elastic.
  • Both effects, every time. A revenue answer names the price effect and the quantity effect and says which wins. Half the argument earns half the marks.
  • Revenue follows price when demand is inelastic and follows quantity when demand is elastic. Everything else falls out of that one line.
  • Total expenditure is total revenue. Same rectangle, same rules, different speaker.
  • Slope is not elasticity. One straight line has one slope and every elasticity.
  • Elasticity belongs to a price, not to a good. Always say “at this price”.
  • Inelastic is not zero. Only perfectly inelastic means no response at all.
  • The top of a demand curve is the elastic end. High price, few units, very responsive.
