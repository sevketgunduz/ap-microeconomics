Firm Costs

Sixteen terms for one picture. A firm's costs are fixed or variable; divide them by output and you get three averages; take the change and you get marginal cost. Marginal cost cuts through the bottom of both U-shaped averages, and changes in prices or productivity move the curves.

Krugman pp. 548–558· 16 terms·Topic 3.2·3 periods

How to use this guide

Entries 1 to 4 are the totals and marginal cost; entries 5 and 6 explain the shape of marginal cost; entries 7 to 13 are the three averages and how marginal cost meets them; entries 14 to 16 move the curves.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 55 does not teach it. Entries 11, 13, 14, 15 and 16 carry the flag.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one picture. Marginal cost is a swoosh. Average variable cost and average total cost are U-shaped, and marginal cost crosses each at its lowest point, from below. Average fixed cost falls forever, and it is the gap between the other two.

⚑ One thing to translate. Your textbook says fixed cost and variable cost; your exam often writes total fixed cost and total variable cost. Your textbook allows average cost; write average total cost. And your exam calls the cause of falling marginal cost specialization and the division of labor.

The sixteen terms

01

Fixed cost

★ Exam
Definition

A cost that does not depend on the quantity of output: the cost of the fixed input. It is the same at every output — including zero.

Textbook page 548.

Selena's Gourmet Salsas rents its food-preparation equipment for $108 a day. It pays the $108 whether it makes no salsa at all or ten cases, so the $108 is a fixed cost:

Cases of salsa012345
Fixed cost$108$108$108$108$108$108

George and Martha pay $400 for their land whether they grow one bushel of wheat or a hundred. Businesses call a cost like this overhead; your exam calls it total fixed cost.

Watch out. Fixed means fixed as output changes, not fixed for ever. If the landlord raises the rent, fixed cost changes — and that moves some of the cost curves (entry 15).

1

Selena's at 0 cases and at 10 cases. Fixed cost is $108 both times.

2

George and Martha's land. $400, whether the farm grows 19 bushels or 96.

3

A food truck's parking permit. $40 a day, however many meals it sells.

How it connects. Added to variable cost (entry 2) it gives total cost (entry 3); spread over the output it is average fixed cost (entry 8); and when it changes, see a change in fixed cost (entry 15).

Check yourself

1.1 A firm's total cost is $250 when it produces nothing. What is its fixed cost?

Show answer

$250. At zero output variable cost is zero, so all of the total cost is fixed cost.

1.2 Selena's makes 7 cases instead of 3. What happens to its fixed cost?

Show answer

Nothing: it stays $108.

02

Variable cost

★ Exam
Definition

A cost that depends on the quantity of output: the cost of the variable input. It is zero when nothing is produced and rises as output rises.

Textbook pages 548–549.

George and Martha pay each worker $200. The more wheat they grow, the more workers they need, so the cost of labor — workers × $200 — is a variable cost. Selena's variable cost is its ingredients, jars and wages:

Cases of salsa012345
Variable cost$0$12$48$108$192$300

Your exam calls this total variable cost: the same thing, as a total.

Watch out. Variable cost is a total, not a cost per case. Selena's variable cost of 4 cases is $192; the cost per case is a different number, $48 (entry 9).

1

The farm with 3 workers. 3 × $200 = $600 of variable cost, for 51 bushels.

2

Selena's at 0 cases and at 5. $0, then $300.

3

A bakery's flour and hourly wages. Variable: both rise with the number of loaves.

How it connects. With fixed cost (entry 1) it makes total cost (entry 3); its change from one unit to the next is marginal cost (entry 4); per unit it is average variable cost (entry 9); and a change in the price of the variable input is a shift of the curves (entry 14).

Check yourself

2.1 A bakery pays rent of $900 a month, buys flour, pays hourly wages and an insurance premium of $100 a month. Which are variable costs?

Show answer

The flour and the hourly wages: they rise with output. The rent and the insurance are fixed.

2.2 Total cost of 6 units is $540 and fixed cost is $108. What is variable cost?

Show answer

$540 − $108 = $432.

03

Total cost

★ Exam
Definition

Fixed cost plus variable cost at each quantity: TC = FC + VC. Drawn against output it is the total cost curve.

Textbook pages 548–550, Figure 55.1 and Table 55.1.

Add the two rows. George and Martha's farm, with $400 of land and $200 workers:

The total cost curve starts at $400, the fixed cost, at zero output. It slopes upward, because more wheat needs more workers. And it gets steeper: each extra bushel costs more than the last (entry 5 (entry 5)).

Watch out. The total cost curve starts at the fixed cost, not at the origin. Only variable cost starts at zero.

1

Selena's at 4 cases. $108 + $192 = $300.

2

The farm with 1 worker. $400 + $200 = $600, for 19 bushels.

3

Any firm at zero output. Total cost equals fixed cost.

How it connects. It is fixed cost (entry 1) plus variable cost (entry 2); its slope is marginal cost (entry 4); and per unit it is average total cost (entry 7).

Check yourself

3.1 Fixed cost is $108 and variable cost is $432. What is total cost?

$108 + $432 = $540.

3.2 Why does the total cost curve get steeper as output rises?

Show answer

Because each extra unit costs more than the one before: marginal cost, the slope, is rising.

04

Marginal cost

★ Exam
Definition

The change in total cost from producing one more unit: MC = ΔTC / ΔQ. Fixed cost does not change, so it is also the change in variable cost.

Textbook pages 550–551, Table 55.1 and Figure 55.2.

Take the differences in Selena's total cost:

Case12345678910
Marginal cost$12$36$60$84$108$132$156$180$204$228

The second case adds $36 to total cost; the eighth adds $180. Marginal cost is the slope of the total cost curve — rise over run, with a run of one case — and it is plotted halfway between the two quantities, as marginal product was.

If the data come in bigger steps, divide: if output rises from 100 to 150 units and total cost from $500 to $800, marginal cost is $300 ÷ 50 = $6 a unit.

Watch out. Marginal cost is the cost of the next unit, not of all units. Selena's fourth case adds $84; the four cases cost $75 each on average.

1

Selena's 2nd case. $156 − $120 = $36.

2

Selena's 8th case. $876 − $696 = $180.

3

The 4th case, from variable cost. $192 − $108 = $84: the same as from total cost.

How it connects. It is the change in total cost (entry 3) and in variable cost (entry 2); it rises because of diminishing returns (entry 5); and it crosses average total cost (entry 12) and average variable cost (entry 13) at their lowest points.

Check yourself

4.1 Selena's total cost is $216 at 3 cases and $300 at 4. What is the marginal cost of the 4th case?

Show answer

$300 − $216 = $84.

4.2 Output rises from 20 to 30 units and total cost from $400 to $520. What is marginal cost?

Show answer

$120 ÷ 10 = $12 a unit.

05

Diminishing returns raise marginal cost

★ Exam
Definition

If each unit of the variable input costs the same, the cost of one more unit of output is that input's price divided by its marginal product. As marginal product falls, marginal cost rises.

Textbook page 552.

A worker on George and Martha's farm costs $200. The first worker adds 19 bushels, so each of those bushels costs $200 ÷ 19, about $10.53. The eighth adds only 5, so each of those costs $200 ÷ 5 = $40:

Worker12345678
Marginal product1917151311975
Cost of an extra bushel$10.53$11.76$13.33$15.38$18.18$22.22$28.57$40.00

Your textbook puts it this way: the flattening of the total product curve and the steepening of the total cost curve are “flip-sides of the same phenomenon”. Fewer bushels per worker means more dollars per bushel.

Watch out. It is marginal product that matters, not total product. Total product keeps rising; it is the extra output per worker that falls, and so the extra cost per unit that rises.

1

The farm's first worker. $200 ÷ 19 ≈ $10.53 a bushel.

2

The farm's eighth worker. $200 ÷ 5 = $40 a bushel.

3

Bayside Car Wash's sixth worker, who washes 20 cars. $120 ÷ 20 = $6 a car, more than the fifth worker's $120 ÷ 24 = $5.

How it connects. It explains why the marginal cost curve (entry 4) slopes up; specialization (entry 6) is the phase before it; and a change in the marginal product itself is a change in productivity (entry 16).

Check yourself

5.1 A worker costs $150 a day. One worker adds 30 units; the next adds 25. What does each extra unit cost with each worker?

Show answer

$150 ÷ 30 = $5, then $150 ÷ 25 = $6. Marginal cost rises as marginal product falls.

5.2 Every worker is paid the same. Why does marginal cost still rise?

Show answer

Because each added worker produces less than the one before, so each extra unit needs more of a worker's time.

06

Specialization lowers marginal cost

★ Exam
Definition

With only a few workers, each one added lets the tasks be divided, so marginal product rises and marginal cost falls. Once those gains are used up, diminishing returns set in and marginal cost rises. The marginal cost curve is a swoosh.

Textbook pages 556–557, Figure 55.6.

One person running a car wash must soap, rinse, dry and take payment alone. A second and a third can each take a job. Bayside Car Wash pays $120 a day:

Worker1234567
Cars washed in all205090120144164179
Marginal product20304030242015
Marginal cost per car$6$4$3$4$5$6$8

Marginal cost falls from $6 to $3 a car while the workers specialize, and rises from the fourth worker on, as diminishing returns set in. Your exam calls the cause specialization and the division of labor.

Watch out. Specialization lowers marginal cost only at low output. It does not cancel diminishing returns; it comes before them.

1

Bayside's second worker. Adds 30 cars: $120 ÷ 30 = $4 a car, down from $6.

2

Bayside's third worker. Adds 40 cars: $3 a car, the lowest marginal cost.

3

Bayside's fourth worker. Adds 30 cars: $4 a car. Diminishing returns have set in.

How it connects. It is the phase before diminishing returns (entry 5), and it is why average variable cost (entry 13) is U-shaped.

Check yourself

6.1 Workers cost $100 a day. The first four add 10, 20, 25 and 20 units. What is the marginal cost per unit with each, and where is it lowest?

Show answer

$10, $5, $4 and $5. It is lowest with the third worker, after which diminishing returns set in.

6.2 Why does a swoosh-shaped marginal cost curve give a U-shaped average variable cost curve?

Show answer

While marginal cost is below average variable cost it pulls the average down; once it rises above, it pulls the average up.

07

Average total cost

★ Exam
Definition

Total cost per unit of output: ATC = TC ÷ Q. Your textbook also calls it average cost; your exam writes average total cost.

Textbook page 552, Table 55.2.

Divide Selena's total cost by the number of cases:

Cases12345678910
Total cost$120$156$216$300$408$540$696$876$1,080$1,308
Average total cost$120$78$72$75$81.60$90$99.43$109.50$120$130.80

Four cases cost $300, so the average case costs $300 ÷ 4 = $75. Average total cost falls, reaches its lowest point, $72 at 3 cases, and rises again: a U.

Watch out. Divide total cost by the quantity. Average total cost is the cost of the typical unit; marginal cost is the cost of the next one, and they are usually different numbers.

1

Selena's at 4 cases. $300 ÷ 4 = $75.

2

Selena's at 3 cases. $216 ÷ 3 = $72, the lowest.

3

Selena's at 10 cases. $1,308 ÷ 10 = $130.80.

How it connects. It is total cost (entry 3) per unit; the U (entry 10) explains its shape; average variable cost, by the gap (entry 11) lies below it; and the minimum-cost output (entry 12) is its lowest point.

Check yourself

7.1 Selena's total cost at 6 cases is $540. What is average total cost?

Show answer

$540 ÷ 6 = $90.

7.2 A firm's average total cost of 5 units is $40. What is its total cost?

Show answer

5 × $40 = $200.

08

Average fixed cost

★ Exam
Definition

Fixed cost per unit of output: AFC = FC ÷ Q. It falls at every output, because the same fixed cost is spread over more units — the spreading effect.

Textbook pages 553–554, Table 55.2.

Selena's $108 spread over more and more cases:

Cases12345678910
Average fixed cost$108$54$36$27$21.60$18$15.43$13.50$12$10.80

Double the output and average fixed cost halves. It falls fast at first and slowly later, and it never reaches zero.

Watch out. The average fixed cost curve never slopes upward and never touches the axis. A U-shaped AFC is a drawing error.

1

Selena's at 4 cases. $108 ÷ 4 = $27.

2

Selena's at 9 cases. $108 ÷ 9 = $12.

3

Alicia's Apple Pies, with $9 of rent. $9 ÷ 6 = $1.50 a pie at 6 pies.

How it connects. It is fixed cost (entry 1) per unit; it is the spreading half of the U (entry 10); it is the gap between the curves (entry 11); and a change in fixed cost (entry 15) moves it.

Check yourself

8.1 Fixed cost is $240. What is average fixed cost at 8 units?

Show answer

$240 ÷ 8 = $30.

8.2 Output doubles. What happens to average fixed cost?

Show answer

It halves: the same fixed cost is spread over twice as many units.

10

Why average total cost is U-shaped

★ Exam
Definition

ATC = AFC + AVC. As output rises, average fixed cost falls and average variable cost rises. At low output the fall is bigger, so ATC falls; at high output the rise is bigger, so ATC rises.

Textbook pages 553–554, Figure 55.3.

Watch the two halves of Selena's average total cost:

Cases12345
Average fixed cost$108$54$36$27$21.60
Average variable cost$12$24$36$48$60
Average total cost$120$78$72$75$81.60

From 2 to 3 cases, average fixed cost falls by $18 and average variable cost rises by $12, so average total cost falls by $6. From 3 to 4 cases the fall is only $9 against a rise of $12, so it rises by $3. At the bottom of the U the two effects balance.

Watch out. Both effects are present at every output. The U is about which one is bigger, not which one is happening.

1

From 1 to 2 cases. AFC falls $54, AVC rises $12: ATC falls $42, from $120 to $78.

2

From 2 to 3 cases. AFC falls $18, AVC rises $12: ATC falls $6, to $72.

3

From 3 to 4 cases. AFC falls $9, AVC rises $12: ATC rises $3, to $75.

How it connects. It explains the shape of average total cost (entry 7), from the fall in average fixed cost (entry 8) and the rise in average variable cost (entry 9).

Show answer

It rises by $6.60, from $75 to $81.60.

10.2 At 3 cases Selena's AFC is $36 and its AVC is $36. What is its ATC?

Show answer

$36 + $36 = $72.

11

Average fixed cost is the gap between the curves

★ ⚑
Definition

On a graph, the vertical distance between the ATC curve and the AVC curve at any quantity is average fixed cost: ATC − AVC = AFC. Because AFC falls at every output, the two curves draw closer as output rises, and never meet.

Not in Module 55 as a graph. Your textbook gives the sum on page 553.

⚑ Not in Module 55 — learn it from here. Your textbook says average total cost is the sum of average fixed and average variable cost, but never shows the sum on the graph. Your exam does: give it ATC and AVC at an output, and it asks for the fixed cost.

Ridgeway Pottery has a fixed cost of $60 a day:

Dozen mugs123456
Average total cost$86$50$40$37$36$36
Average variable cost$26$20$20$22$24$26
Gap = average fixed cost$60$30$20$15$12$10

At 1 dozen mugs the gap is $86 − $26 = $60; at 5 dozen it is $36 − $24 = $12. Multiply the gap by the quantity and you get the fixed cost back: ($36 − $24) × 5 = $60. Choose a quantity in the third diagram to see the gap drawn.

Watch out. The gap is average fixed cost, not marginal cost and not profit. And ATC and AVC never cross: they could meet only if fixed cost were zero.

1

Ridgeway at 1 dozen. $86 − $26 = $60 of fixed cost per dozen.

2

Ridgeway at 4 dozen. $37 − $22 = $15 per dozen; 4 × $15 = $60.

3

A firm making 200 units at ATC $8 and AVC $5. AFC is $3, so fixed cost is 200 × $3 = $600.

How it connects. It is the distance between average total cost (entry 7) and average variable cost (entry 9), and the height of average fixed cost (entry 8); a rise in fixed cost (entry 15) widens it.

Check yourself

11.1 At 50 units, ATC is $30 and AVC is $22. What is the fixed cost?

Show answer

($30 − $22) × 50 = $400.

11.2 Do the ATC and AVC curves get closer together or further apart as output rises? Why?

Show answer

Closer. The gap is average fixed cost, which falls at every output.

12

Minimum-cost output

★ Exam
Definition

The quantity at which average total cost is lowest — the bottom of the U. Marginal cost crosses ATC there. Below it, MC is less than ATC and ATC is falling; above it, MC is greater than ATC and ATC is rising.

Textbook pages 554–555, Figures 55.4 and 55.5.

Think of your grade-point average. A new grade below your average pulls it down; a new grade above it pulls it up. Marginal cost is the new grade:

Case23456
Marginal cost$36$60$84$108$132
ATC before it$120$78$72$75$81.60
ATC after it$78$72$75$81.60$90

The 3rd case costs $60, below the average of $78, so the average falls to ($156 + $60) ÷ 3 = $72. The 4th costs $84, above $72, so the average rises to $75. Selena's minimum-cost output is 3 cases. On your exam, write “the quantity at which average total cost is lowest”.

Watch out. The rule compares marginal cost with the average, not with the previous marginal cost. Marginal cost can be rising while ATC still falls, as long as it is below ATC.

1

Selena's 3rd case, at $60. Below the average of $78, so ATC falls, to $72.

2

Selena's 4th case, at $84. Above the average of $72, so ATC rises, to $75.

3

Ridgeway's 6th dozen, at $36. Equal to the average of $36, so ATC does not move.

How it connects. It is where marginal cost (entry 4) meets average total cost (entry 7); marginal cost through minimum AVC (entry 13) is the same rule for the other average; and a change in fixed cost (entry 15) moves it.

Check yourself

12.1 A firm makes 10 units at an ATC of $20. The 11th unit costs $9. What is the new ATC?

Show answer

(10 × $20 + $9) ÷ 11 = $209 ÷ 11 = $19. Marginal cost was below the average, so it fell.

12.2 Marginal cost is rising, but it is below average total cost. What is ATC doing?

Show answer

Falling. What matters is whether marginal cost is above or below the average, not which way it is moving.

13

Marginal cost crosses average variable cost at its minimum

★ ⚑
Definition

The rule of entry 12 holds for every average. AVC falls while marginal cost is below it and rises while marginal cost is above it, so MC crosses AVC at AVC's lowest point — at a smaller output than the bottom of ATC.

Not in Module 55. Your textbook reaches it in Module 59, page 593.

⚑ Not in Module 55 — learn it from here. Your textbook says marginal cost crosses average total cost at its minimum. It says the same about average variable cost only in a later module, but every cost-curve graph on your exam shows both crossings.

Ridgeway Pottery's marginal cost falls and then rises, so its averages both make a U:

Dozen mugs123456
Marginal cost$26$14$20$28$32$36
Average variable cost$26$20$20$22$24$26
Average total cost$86$50$40$37$36$36

The 3rd dozen costs $20, exactly the average of $20, so AVC is flat there: marginal cost crosses AVC at its bottom, $20, at 2.5 dozen. The 6th dozen costs $36, exactly ATC, so marginal cost crosses ATC at its bottom, $36, at 5.5 dozen.

Why is the bottom of AVC to the left? Because ATC = AVC + AFC, and at the bottom of AVC average fixed cost is still falling — so ATC is still falling there too.

Watch out. Marginal cost crosses AVC and ATC at their lowest points, from below. It never crosses AFC, which falls everywhere.

1

Ridgeway's 2nd dozen, at $14. Below the AVC of $26, so AVC falls, to $20.

2

Ridgeway's 3rd dozen, at $20. Equal to the AVC of $20, so AVC stays at $20: the bottom.

3

Ridgeway's 4th dozen, at $28. Above the AVC of $20, so AVC rises, to $22.

How it connects. It is the rule of marginal cost (entry 4) applied to average variable cost (entry 9); specialization (entry 6) gives AVC its U; and the minimum-cost output (entry 12) is the same crossing for ATC.

Check yourself

13.1 The average variable cost of 4 units is $15. The 5th unit's marginal cost is $25. What is the new AVC?

Show answer

(4 × $15 + $25) ÷ 5 = $85 ÷ 5 = $17. Marginal cost was above the average, so it rose.

13.2 Which comes at the larger output, the bottom of AVC or the bottom of ATC? Why?

Show answer

The bottom of ATC. At the bottom of AVC, average fixed cost is still falling, so ATC is still falling.

14

A change in an input price shifts the cost curves

★ ⚑
Definition

If the price of a variable input changes — a wage, a material, a per-unit tax — variable cost changes at every output. MC, AVC and ATC all shift the same way. AFC does not move.

Not in Module 55, or anywhere in your textbook.

⚑ Not in Module 55 — learn it from here. Your textbook draws every cost curve and never moves one. Your exam asks what happens to them when a wage, the price of a material or a tax changes.

Suppose everything Selena buys for each case — tomatoes, jars, hours of work — costs a third more. Variable cost rises by a third at every output, and so does marginal cost:

Cases12345
MC before$12$36$60$84$108
MC after$16$48$80$112$144
ATC before$120$78$72$75$81.60
ATC after$124$86$84$91$101.60

At 3 cases marginal cost rises from $60 to $80, AVC from $36 to $48 and ATC from $72 to $84. Average fixed cost stays at $36: the rent did not change. Try it in the fourth diagram.

Watch out. A higher wage is a change in a variable cost, so it moves marginal cost. A higher rent does not.

1

The wage rises. MC, AVC and ATC shift up; AFC does not move.

2

Tomatoes become cheaper. MC, AVC and ATC shift down.

3

A per-unit tax of $2 on every case. MC, AVC and ATC all shift up by $2 at every output.

How it connects. It changes variable cost (entry 2) at every output, and so average variable cost (entry 9); compare a change in fixed cost (entry 15), which moves fewer curves, and a rise in productivity (entry 16), which works the other way.

Check yourself

14.1 The government puts a per-unit tax of $5 on a firm's output. What happens to MC, AVC, ATC and AFC?

Show answer

MC, AVC and ATC each shift up by $5 at every output. AFC does not change.

14.2 Selena's variable cost rises by a third, so its AVC at 3 cases is $48. What is its ATC at 3 cases?

Show answer

$36 + $48 = $84: the unchanged AFC plus the new AVC.

15

A change in fixed cost

★ ⚑
Definition

If fixed cost changes — rent, insurance, a licence fee, a lump-sum tax — AFC and ATC shift. MC and AVC do not, because the cost of one more unit has not changed. The bottom of ATC moves: to a larger output when fixed cost rises.

Not in Module 55. Your textbook asks about it once, in the Section 10 review on page 581.

⚑ Not in Module 55 — learn it from here. Your textbook only asks, in a review exercise, what an increase in fixed cost does. The answer: it moves ATC and AFC up, leaves MC and AVC alone, and moves the bottom of ATC to a larger output.

Watch out. A rise in fixed cost does not raise marginal cost. If a question names a lump-sum tax, the marginal cost curve stays where it is.

1

The rent rises. AFC and ATC shift up; MC and AVC do not move.

2

A lump-sum tax of $1,000 a year. The same: AFC and ATC up, MC and AVC unchanged.

3

The insurance premium falls. AFC and ATC shift down; MC and AVC do not move.

How it connects. It changes fixed cost (entry 1) and so average fixed cost (entry 8); it widens the gap (entry 11); it moves the minimum-cost output (entry 12); and it leaves alone the curves that an input price (entry 14) moves.

15.2 Does an increase in fixed cost increase marginal cost?

Show answer

16

A change in productivity shifts the cost curves

★ ⚑
Definition

If each worker produces more — better machines, training, technology — the same output needs fewer hours of work. With the wage unchanged, MC, AVC and ATC shift down; AFC does not move. A fall in productivity shifts them up.

Not in Module 55, or anywhere in your textbook.

⚑ Not in Module 55 — learn it from here. Your textbook links marginal cost to marginal product but never changes productivity. Your exam does: better technology lowers marginal cost and average cost.

Marginal cost is the wage divided by marginal product (entry 5 (entry 5)). Raise marginal product and marginal cost falls. If new equipment doubled the output of every Bayside worker:

On Selena's numbers, a rise in productivity of a half cuts variable cost to two-thirds at every output: at 3 cases marginal cost falls from $60 to $40 and ATC from $72 to $60.

Watch out. Productivity and input prices push the same curves in opposite directions. A rise in productivity lowers costs; a rise in an input price raises them.

1

New software for every worker. Productivity rises: MC, AVC and ATC shift down.

2

A training course. Each worker produces more: the same curves shift down.

3

Worn-out machines. Productivity falls: MC, AVC and ATC shift up.

How it connects. It works through marginal cost = wage ÷ marginal product (entry 5), and it mirrors a change in an input price (entry 14).

Things to get right

  • Total cost starts at the fixed cost, not at zero.
  • Marginal means the change. Marginal cost is the difference between neighbouring totals, of total cost or of variable cost.
  • Average means divide. ATC, AVC and AFC are their totals over the quantity.
  • Compare marginal with average. Below it, the average falls; above it, the average rises.
  • Marginal cost crosses AVC and ATC at their lowest points, from below. The bottom of AVC comes first.
  • AFC falls forever, and it is the gap between ATC and AVC.
  • An input price or productivity moves MC, AVC and ATC. A fixed cost moves AFC and ATC only.
  • Producing more is a movement along the curves, never a shift.
