The Production Function

Fifteen terms for one idea. A firm turns inputs into output; with one input fixed, each extra unit of the other adds less than the last. Three measures — total, marginal and average product — describe the same schedule.

Krugman pp. 542–547· 15 terms·Topic 3.1·2 periods

How to use this guide

Entries 1 to 3 set the scene — inputs, outputs and the short run; entries 4 to 7 are total and marginal product; entries 8 to 11 are diminishing returns and the averages; entries 12 to 15 look past a single curve.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 54 does not teach it. Entries 9, 10 and 11 carry the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the module but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. Hold one input fixed and add more of the other. Total product keeps rising, but each new unit usually adds less than the last: marginal product falls. The average moves toward the marginal.

⚑ One thing to translate. Your textbook says diminishing returns. Your exam writes diminishing marginal returns, and it asks for average product of labor and marginal product of labor in tables. Learn all three, and always say which input is fixed.

The fifteen terms

01

Production function

★ Exam
Definition

The relationship between the quantity of inputs a firm uses and the quantity of output it produces.

Textbook pages 542–543.

A firm turns inputs into output. Your textbook's example is a farm that grows wheat from two inputs, land and labor. How much wheat the farm grows depends on how many workers it hires: that dependence is the production function.

George and Martha's farm has 10 acres, and the table below is its production function. Every number in it is a quantity — workers in, bushels out. There is no price anywhere, and none is needed in this module.

Workers012345678
Bushels of wheat01936516475849196

Watch out. A production function is about physical quantities — bushels, loaves, pounds — for a given technology. Money does not enter until the next module.

1

George and Martha's farm. 4 workers produce 64 bushels; 8 workers produce 96.

2

A bakery with one oven. Each number of bakers goes with a number of loaves a day: its table of bakers and loaves is its production function.

3

Bernie's ice company. Using 3 kilowatt-hours of electricity with its 10-ton machine, it makes 2,400 pounds of ice.

How it connects. It has two kinds of input, fixed and variable (entry 2), it is read as total product (entry 4), and it moves up when the fixed input or the technology improves (entry 12).

Check yourself

1.1 What does a production function relate?

Show answer

The quantity of inputs a firm uses to the quantity of output it produces.

1.2 A bakery's production function says 5 bakers make 115 loaves a day. Is the 115 a quantity or an amount of money?

Show answer

A quantity: 115 loaves. A production function stays in physical units.

02

Fixed input and variable input

★ Exam
Definition

A fixed input is one whose quantity cannot be changed for a period of time. A variable input is one whose quantity the firm can change at any time.

Textbook page 542.

On George and Martha's farm the land is fixed: 10 acres, and they cannot buy or sell any for now. The labor is variable: they can hire as many workers as they like.

Every production function in this module works the same way. One input is held constant, and the table shows what happens to output as the other changes. The firms change; the pattern does not.

Watch out. Whether an input is fixed depends on the time period, not on what kind of input it is. Land is fixed for a season; over ten years the same farm could buy more.

1

The farm. Land (10 acres) is fixed; workers are variable.

2

A bakery with one oven. The oven is fixed this month; the bakers are variable.

3

Bernie's ice company. The 10-ton machine is fixed; electricity, in kilowatt-hours, is variable.

How it connects. They are the two kinds of input in the production function (entry 1), they define the short run (entry 3), the long run releases the fixed one, the variable input is the one marginal product (entry 5) measures, changing the fixed one is a shift of the curves (entry 12), and changing every input is a question of scale (entry 14).

Check yourself

2.1 In the short run, a farm can change the number of workers but not its acreage. Which is the fixed input?

Show answer

The land. The number of workers is the variable input.

2.2 Can an input that is fixed this month be variable next year?

Show answer

Yes. Fixed means it cannot be changed during the period you are looking at; with more time, a firm can change it.

03

Short run and long run

★ Exam
Definition

The short run is the period in which at least one input is fixed. The long run is the period in which all inputs can be varied.

Textbook page 542.

These are not lengths of time. The short run is any period in which the firm is stuck with at least one input; the long run is any period long enough for it to change every input.

Everything in this module is a short-run story, because the farm's 10 acres never change. In the long run there is no fixed input, and the question becomes a different one — what happens when the farm changes everything at once (see entry 14 (entry 14)).

Watch out. The short run is a condition, not a number of days or months. For a pizza shop it may be a year; for an online seller, a week.

1

A farm with 10 acres. Short run: the land cannot change.

2

The same farm, deciding what to do over twenty years. Long run: it can buy land, build barns and change every input.

3

A restaurant that signed a five-year lease. Short run in its building, for as long as the lease runs.

How it connects. The short run is the period in which one input is fixed (entry 2); its other input is the variable input (entry 2); and the long run's question (entry 14) describes what changes when both are varied.

Check yourself

3.1 Is “the short run” the same as “less than a year”?

Show answer

No. It is the period in which at least one input is fixed, however long that takes.

3.2 A bakery can hire bakers next week but cannot add an oven for six months. Over the next month, is it in the short run or the long run?

Show answer

The short run: the oven is fixed.

04

Total product

★ Exam
Definition

The quantity of output produced at each quantity of the variable input, with the fixed input held constant. Drawn as a graph, it is the total product curve.

Textbook pages 542–543, Figure 54.1.

The second row of George and Martha's table is their total product: 0, 19, 36, 51 … bushels as the number of workers goes 0, 1, 2, 3 … Plot it, with workers across and bushels up, and you have the total product curve in the diagram above.

The curve slopes up: more workers, more wheat. It also gets flatter as it goes, and that flattening is the subject of the rest of the module.

Watch out. Total product is output, not revenue. It is measured in bushels, loaves or pounds, and it rises as long as another worker adds anything at all.

1

5 workers on the farm. Total product is 75 bushels.

2

8 workers on the farm. Total product is 96 bushels.

3

No workers. Total product is 0: the land produces nothing by itself.

How it connects. It is the production function (entry 1) drawn as a curve, its change from one worker to the next is marginal product (entry 5), its slope is that marginal product (entry 6), its output divided by the workers is average product (entry 9), and it moves up when the fixed input grows (entry 12).

Check yourself

4.1 The farm's total product is 64 bushels with 4 workers. What is it with 6 workers?

Show answer

84 bushels.

4.2 Does total product measure output or the money it earns?

Show answer

Output, in physical units. Money does not appear in a production function.

05

Marginal product

★ Exam
Definition

The additional output from using one more unit of an input, with all other inputs held fixed: MP = ΔQ / ΔL for labor.

Textbook pages 543–544, equation 54-1.

Take the differences in George and Martha's total product:

Worker12345678
Marginal product1917151311975

The first worker adds 19 bushels, the second 17, the third 15. Sometimes data come in bigger steps. Then divide the change in output by the change in the input: if 40 workers produce 400 units and 50 workers produce 480, the marginal product is (480 − 400) ÷ (50 − 40) = 8 units per worker.

Watch out. Marginal product is a change, not a total. The fifth worker's marginal product is 11 bushels — not the 75 bushels of total product.

1

The 2nd worker on the farm. 36 − 19 = 17 bushels.

2

The 7th worker. 91 − 84 = 7 bushels.

3

40 workers make 400 units; 50 make 480. (480 − 400) ÷ 10 = 8 units per worker.

How it connects. It is the change in total product (entry 4), it is the slope of the total product curve (entry 6), it is drawn halfway between quantities (entry 7), it is the output of the variable input (entry 2), it is compared with average product (entry 9), it pulls the average along with it (entry 11), and when it falls we have diminishing marginal returns (entry 8).

Check yourself

5.1 The farm produces 64 bushels with 4 workers and 75 with 5. What is the marginal product of the 5th worker?

Show answer

75 − 64 = 11 bushels.

5.2 A firm's output is 500 units with 20 workers and 560 with 30 workers. What is the marginal product of labor?

Show answer

(560 − 500) ÷ (30 − 20) = 6 units per worker.

06

The slope of the total product curve

★ Exam
Definition

The slope of the total product curve is its rise over its run — the change in output divided by the change in labor — and that is the marginal product.

Textbook page 544.

Pick any two neighbouring points on the curve. The “rise” between them is the extra output; the “run” is one more worker. Rise over run is ΔQ / ΔL — the marginal product. In the first diagram, choose a worker and the green segment shows the rise: its length is that worker's marginal product.

So the curve flattens because marginal product falls. The slope from 0 to 1 worker is 19; from 4 to 5 workers it is 11; from 7 to 8 it is 5.

Watch out. A flattening curve is still sloping upward. “Flatter” means a smaller positive slope, not a negative one: output is still rising.

1

From 0 to 1 worker. The curve rises 19 over a run of 1: a slope of 19.

2

From 4 to 5 workers. The curve rises 75 − 64 = 11: a slope of 11.

3

A total product curve that is a straight line. Its slope never changes, so each worker adds the same: constant marginal product.

How it connects. That slope is marginal product (entry 5), the curve is total product (entry 4), its flattening is diminishing marginal returns (entry 8), and a curve that turned down would need a marginal product below zero.

Check yourself

6.1 Between 6 and 7 workers the farm's curve rises from 84 to 91. What is its slope there?

Show answer

7 bushels per worker: the rise is 7 over a run of 1, the 7th worker's marginal product.

6.2 A total product curve gets flatter as workers are added but never turns down. Is its slope still positive?

Show answer

Yes. Flatter means a smaller positive slope: each worker adds less, but still adds something.

07

Plotting marginal product between quantities

○ Background
Definition

Marginal product is a change between two numbers of workers, so it is plotted halfway between them.

Textbook pages 544–545, Figure 54.2.

The 11 bushels you get when the farm goes from 4 workers to 5 does not belong to 4 workers or to 5. Your textbook puts the point halfway, at 4.5. So the first worker's 19 sits at 0.5, the second's 17 at 1.5, and so on — and the marginal product curve in the second diagram is drawn that way.

Average product has no such rule. It belongs to a number of workers, so the average of 5 workers is plotted at 5.

Watch out. If you plot marginal product at the whole-worker numbers you shift the whole curve half a worker to the right. Read the half-way points when you read a graph.

1

The first worker's marginal product. Plotted at 0.5 workers.

2

The fifth worker's 11 bushels. Plotted at 4.5 workers, halfway between 4 and 5.

3

The average product of 5 workers. Plotted at 5: an average belongs to a number of workers.

How it connects. It is how we draw marginal product (entry 5), and it is why the two curves in the marginal-and-average diagram (entry 11) cross between whole numbers of workers.

Check yourself

7.1 Where is the third worker's marginal product plotted?

Show answer

At 2.5 workers, halfway between 2 and 3.

7.2 Where is the average product of 6 workers plotted?

Show answer

At 6 workers.

08

Diminishing marginal returns

★ Exam
Definition

When the quantity of one input is increased and all other inputs are held fixed, the marginal product of that input falls.

Textbook page 545.

On George and Martha's farm each new worker has a smaller share of the same 10 acres, so each adds less than the one before: 19, 17, 15, 13 … That falling marginal product is diminishing marginal returns.

It is an “other things equal” statement. It holds as long as the other inputs — here the land, and the technology — stay fixed. Change them, and you are asking a different question (see entry 12 (entry 12) and entry 14 (entry 14)).

Watch out. Diminishing returns do not mean output falls. The farm's total product keeps rising; each worker simply adds less. Total product would fall only if marginal product turned negative.

1

The farm's second worker. She adds 17 bushels, fewer than the first worker's 19.

2

A kitchen with one oven. The sixth cook adds fewer meals than the fifth: they share the same oven.

3

A farm that doubles its land and its workers together. Not diminishing returns: all inputs changed. That is a long-run question.

How it connects. It is a fall in marginal product (entry 5), it shows as a flattening of the total product curve (entry 6), it may follow a rising phase (entry 10), it is why marginal cost rises (entry 13), and Malthus's story (entry 15) shows that technology does not remove it.

Check yourself

8.1 Output is 40 for one worker, 70 for two, 90 for three and 100 for four. Are there diminishing marginal returns? Does total product fall?

Show answer

Yes: marginal product falls 40, 30, 20, 10. No: total product keeps rising, because every marginal product is still positive.

8.2 Why must the land stay the same for the statement to hold?

Show answer

Because the claim is about one input rising while all the others are held fixed. If land grows too, marginal product need not fall.

09

Average product

★ ⚑
Definition

Output per unit of the variable input: average product = Q ÷ L.

Not in Module 54's text. Your textbook uses the words once, as a wrong answer on page 547.

⚑ Not in Module 54 — learn it from here. Your textbook never defines average product, but your exam asks for it — usually as average product of labor. Divide total product by the number of workers.

Total product tells you how much the farm grows; average product tells you how much each worker grows on average. Divide the total by the number of workers:

Workers12345678
Total product1936516475849196
Average product1918171615141312

Five workers produce 75 bushels, so the average product is 75 ÷ 5 = 15 bushels per worker. Notice that it is also the average of the marginal products so far: (19 + 17 + 15 + 13 + 11) ÷ 5 = 15.

Watch out. Divide total product by the number of workers. Dividing the marginal product by the number of workers is a different number, and the wrong one.

1

5 workers on the farm. 75 ÷ 5 = 15 bushels per worker.

2

4 bakers at Maple Street Bakery. 92 ÷ 4 = 23 loaves per baker.

3

1 worker. With one worker, average product equals total product and marginal product: 19 bushels on the farm.

How it connects. It comes from total product (entry 4), it is not the same as marginal product (entry 5), and this rule (entry 11) says how the two move together.

Check yourself

9.1 A firm's 6 workers produce 132 units. What is the average product of labor?

Show answer

132 ÷ 6 = 22 units per worker.

9.2 Why is the average product of one worker the same as that worker's marginal product?

Show answer

Both are the same number: one worker's output, divided by one worker, and the change from zero workers.

10

Increasing marginal returns

★ ⚑
Definition

Marginal product that rises as the variable input increases, usually because the first workers can divide the tasks. Diminishing returns begin where marginal product stops rising.

Not in Module 54. Your textbook meets the idea on page 556, as “increasing returns” from specialization.

⚑ Not in Module 54 — learn it from here. The book's farm has no rising phase, so it never shows diminishing returns setting in. Your exam does: it asks what happens “when diminishing returns set in”. They begin at the worker where marginal product peaks and turns down.

George and Martha's farm starts at its highest marginal product and falls. Many firms do not. With one baker and one oven, everything is done by one person. Add a second and they can split the work; the third and fourth specialize further, and each adds more than the one before. Maple Street Bakery, in the second diagram, looks like this:

Baker12345678
Loaves added1220283223171513

Marginal product rises from 12 to 32 loaves with the fourth baker, and then falls. Diminishing returns set in with the fifth baker, who adds 23: fewer than the fourth's 32.

Watch out. Rising marginal product within one firm is not the same thing as increasing returns to scale. Here only the workers are increasing and the oven stays fixed.

1

The bakery's second baker. He adds 20 loaves, more than the first baker's 12.

2

The bakery's fourth baker. She adds 32, the most of any baker.

3

The bakery's fifth baker. He adds 23, less than the fourth: diminishing returns have set in.

How it connects. It is the opposite phase to diminishing marginal returns (entry 8), it is why the marginal product curve (entry 11) starts low, and the point where it ends is where rising marginal cost (entry 13) begins.

Check yourself

10.1 A firm's marginal products are 8, 14, 18, 16 and 10 as it hires its first five workers. With which worker do diminishing returns set in?

Show answer

The fourth: 16 is the first marginal product below its predecessor's 18.

10.2 The farm's marginal product is highest for the first worker. When do diminishing returns begin there?

Show answer

From the second worker. There is no rising phase, so they set in at once.

11

Marginal product pulls average product

★ ⚑
Definition

Average product rises while marginal product is above it, falls while marginal product is below it, and is unchanged where the two are equal.

Not in Module 54. Your textbook teaches the same logic for cost on pages 555–556, with a grade-point average.

⚑ Not in Module 54 — learn it from here. Your exam tests this with a table or a graph: a sample question asks what happens to average product “when diminishing returns set in”. The answer is not that it begins to fall. Your textbook teaches the rule only for costs.

Think of your grade-point average. A new grade above your average pulls it up; one below pulls it down. Marginal product is the “new grade” and average product is the average. Compare each baker's marginal product with the average before that baker joined:

Baker2345678
Marginal product20283223171513
Average before12162023232221
Average now16202323222120
Averagerisesrisesrisessamefallsfallsfalls

The marginal products of the second to fourth baker (20, 28, 32) are above the average before them, so the average rises. The fifth baker's 23 equals the average of 23, so it does not move. From the sixth baker the marginal product is below the average, so the average falls.

Watch out. Diminishing returns set in with the fifth baker, but average product does not start to fall until the sixth. Marginal product has to fall all the way down to the average before it drags the average down.

1

The 3rd baker adds 28 loaves; the first two averaged 16. 28 is above 16, so the average rises, to (32 + 28) ÷ 3 = 20.

2

The 5th baker adds 23; the first four averaged 23. Equal, so the average does not change: it stays at 23.

3

The 6th baker adds 17; the first five averaged 23. 17 is below 23, so the average falls, to (115 + 17) ÷ 6 = 22.

How it connects. It links marginal product (entry 5) to average product (entry 9), it is why average product peaks where the two meet, the two curves are drawn as in plotting between quantities (entry 7), and it is the moment diminishing returns set in (entry 10) that the exam asks about.

Check yourself

11.1 Five workers produce an average of 18 units. The sixth adds 12. Does the average rise, fall or stay the same, and to what?

Show answer

It falls: 12 is below 18. The new average is (5 × 18 + 12) ÷ 6 = 102 ÷ 6 = 17.

11.2 When marginal product is above average product, what is happening to average product?

Show answer

It is rising: a new unit above the average pulls the average up.

12

A bigger fixed input shifts the curves

★ Exam
Definition

More of the fixed input — or better technology — raises output at every quantity of the variable input: total product shifts up, and so does marginal product.

Textbook pages 545–546, Figure 54.3; Bernie's ice, page 546.

Bernie makes ice with a 10-ton machine and electricity. A bigger, 15-ton machine lifts his output at every quantity of electricity — in your textbook's exercise, it doubles it:

Kilowatt-hours01234
Ice, 10-ton machine (pounds)01,0001,8002,4002,800
Ice, 15-ton machine (pounds)02,0003,6004,8005,600

The marginal products shift up as well, from 1,000, 800, 600, 400 to 2,000, 1,600, 1,200, 800 — and they still fall. The new curve has the same shape, higher.

Watch out. Hiring another worker is a movement along a total product curve. Changing the fixed input is a shift of the whole curve.

1

Bernie's bigger machine. With 2 kilowatt-hours he makes 3,600 pounds instead of 1,800.

2

The farm buys 10 more acres. Every number of workers now produces more wheat.

3

A better recipe at the bakery. Technology improves: the same bakers and oven make more loaves, whatever the number of bakers.

How it connects. It changes the position of total product (entry 4), not its shape, so diminishing marginal returns (entry 8) still hold; it moves the whole of the production function (entry 1), it changes the input that the short run (entry 2) kept fixed, its cost side is marginal cost (entry 13), and technology (entry 15) is the long view of the same idea.

Check yourself

12.1 Bernie switches from the 10-ton to the 15-ton machine. Is this a movement along his total product curve or a shift of it?

Show answer

A shift: the fixed input changed. His output rises at every quantity of electricity.

12.2 After the shift, does electricity still show diminishing returns?

Show answer

Yes. The marginal products, 2,000, 1,600, 1,200 and 800, still fall.

13

Diminishing returns raise marginal cost

★ Exam
Definition

If each extra worker costs the same, the cost of one more unit of output is the worker's pay divided by the marginal product — so it rises as marginal product falls.

Not in Module 54. Your textbook makes the link in Module 55, pages 552 and 555.

Suppose a worker costs $60 a day. When that worker adds 12 units, each extra unit costs $60 ÷ 12 = $5. When the next adds only 10, each extra unit costs $60 ÷ 10 = $6. When a later one adds only 6, it costs $60 ÷ 6 = $10.

The falling marginal product is the reason the cost of each extra unit rises. This is a preview of the next module: it is where the upward-sloping marginal cost curve comes from.

Watch out. This is only a preview. It needs the price of the variable input, which Module 54 does not have, and it ignores the fixed cost, which does not affect the cost of one more unit.

1

A $60 worker with a marginal product of 12. $60 ÷ 12 = $5 for each extra unit.

2

The same worker, once marginal product has fallen to 6. $60 ÷ 6 = $10 for each extra unit.

3

A $90 worker with a marginal product of 9. $90 ÷ 9 = $10 for each extra unit.

How it connects. It is the cost side of diminishing marginal returns (entry 8), it runs the other way during increasing marginal returns (entry 10), and it is shifted by a change in the fixed input (entry 12).

Check yourself

13.1 A worker costs $80 a day and adds 16 units. What does each extra unit cost?

Show answer

$80 ÷ 16 = $5.

13.2 What happens to the cost of each extra unit while marginal product is rising?

Show answer

It falls: the same pay buys more output, so the divisor is growing.

14

Diminishing returns are not diseconomies of scale

★ Exam
Definition

Diminishing returns: one input rises, the others are fixed, and its marginal product falls. Diseconomies of scale: every input rises together, in the long run, and average cost rises.

Not in Module 54. Your textbook teaches scale in Module 56.

The two ideas sound alike and answer different questions.

Diminishing returnsReturns to scale
What changesOne input; the others are fixedAll inputs, together
PeriodShort runLong run
The questionDoes marginal product fall?Does output rise by more, less or the same percentage as the inputs?

On the farm, adding workers to 10 acres is a diminishing-returns question. Doubling the land and the workers is a returns-to-scale question, and diminishing returns say nothing about it.

Watch out. If every input changes, it is not a diminishing-returns question — whatever happens to output. The phrase needs one input that rises and the rest that stay fixed.

1

A farm adds workers to its 10 acres. Diminishing returns: one input varies.

2

A farm doubles its land, its barns and its workers. Returns to scale: every input varies.

3

A bakery adds a sixth baker to its one oven. Diminishing returns, if the sixth baker adds less than the fifth.

How it connects. Diminishing returns are a short-run statement (entry 8) with one input (entry 2) fixed, and scale is the long run of entry 3 (entry 3).

Check yourself

14.1 A firm doubles its workers and its machines together. Output rises by only 80%. Is that diminishing marginal returns?

Show answer

No. Every input changed together: it is a question of returns to scale, and the firm shows decreasing returns to scale.

14.2 A farm hires a ninth worker on its fixed acreage and total product rises by less than for the eighth. Which idea is this?

Show answer

Diminishing marginal returns: one input rose and the land stayed fixed.

15

Malthus and technology

○ Background
Definition

Technology raises the whole production function. It does not remove the fall in marginal product at a given level of technology.

Textbook page 546, the FYI “Was Malthus Right?”.

In 1798 Thomas Malthus argued that a growing population on a fixed amount of land would face falling marginal products and, in the end, hunger. He was wrong about the outcome: farming technology advanced so fast that food output rose faster than population.

But he was not wrong about the shape. At any one level of technology, an extra worker on fixed land still adds less than the last. Better technology shifts the whole production function up (see entry 12 (entry 12)); it does not flatten the curve at the new level. Your textbook adds that technology relaxes the limits only over the very long term: in 2008 farmers ran into limits in land and fertilizer.

Watch out. Malthus's prediction failed; the principle of diminishing returns did not. The curve moved up; its shape stayed.

1

A new wheat variety. The farm's total product shifts up: the same workers and land grow more.

2

An extra worker on the same land and technology. Still adds less than the last.

3

The 2008 grain price spike. Farmers scrambling to plant more acreage ran into limits in land and fertilizer.

How it connects. It is the long view of diminishing marginal returns (entry 8), and its relief of the limits is the shift described in entry 12 (entry 12).

Check yourself

15.1 Did Malthus's wrong prediction show that diminishing returns do not exist?

Show answer

No. Technology shifted the production function up. At any given technology, the marginal product of labor on fixed land still falls.

15.2 Does better technology cancel diminishing returns?

Show answer

No. It raises the curve, and the curve at the new level still flattens.

(d) 1 point — the third worker's marginal product, 30, is above the average of the first two workers, 15, so the average rises, to 60 ÷ 3 = 20.

(e) 1 point — the total product curve shifts up: more posters at every number of workers, because the fixed input has increased.

Things to get right

  • Name the fixed input. Diminishing returns are about one input rising while the rest stay fixed.
  • Marginal means the change. Marginal product is the difference between neighbouring totals.
  • Average means divide. Average product is total product over the number of workers.
  • Total product keeps rising while marginal product is positive, even as marginal product falls.
  • Diminishing returns set in where marginal product peaks, which may be after the first worker.
  • Compare marginal with average. Above it, the average rises; below it, the average falls; equal, it stays.
  • A new fixed input shifts the curves; a new worker moves along them.
  • The short run is a condition, not a length of time.
