Profit Maximization

Sixteen terms for one rule. How much should a firm produce? Keep going while the next unit brings in at least what it costs — and stop where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.

Krugman pp. 536–541· 16 terms·Topic 3.5·1½ periods

How to use this guide

Entries 1 to 3 are the goal and the two ways to reach it; entries 4 to 8 are the numbers; entries 9 to 14 are the graph and who the rule applies to; entries 15 and 16 are the decision that comes before the rule.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 53 does not teach it. Entry 14 carries the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the module but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. A unit is worth producing if it adds at least as much to revenue as to cost. Produce every such unit and stop before the first that does not: that is where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and where profit is largest.

⚑ One thing to translate. Your textbook calls the rule the optimal output rule. Your exam calls it the profit-maximizing rule and writes it MR = MC. Learn both names, and always write the comparison itself in your answers.

The sixteen terms

01

Profit maximization

★ Exam
Definition

The assumption that a firm chooses its output to make its economic profit as large as possible.

Textbook page 536.

Your textbook opens Module 53 with one question: what quantity of output would maximize the producer's profit? Everything in this module is an answer to it.

The profit in question is economic profit, from Module 52 — total revenue minus all costs, implicit as well as explicit. Your textbook says so on page 539: the costs in its tables include every opportunity cost, so its profits are economic profits.

Watch out. A firm maximizes profit, not revenue, not sales and not profit per unit. Each of those can peak at a different quantity.

1

Jennifer and Jason at $18 a bushel. Their profit is largest, $18, at 5 bushels — not at 7, where revenue is largest.

2

A cafe choosing how many cakes to bake. It bakes the number that leaves the most profit after every cost, including the owner's time.

3

A firm that can only lose money. Maximizing profit then means making the loss as small as possible.

How it connects. It is measured by total revenue minus total cost (entry 2), it is reached in two steps, whether and how much (entry 15), and the profit that counts is the one in economic profit decides whether (entry 16).

Check yourself

1.1 At $18 a bushel, Jennifer and Jason's revenue is $126 at 7 bushels and $90 at 5. Why do they choose 5?

Show answer

Because profit, not revenue, is the goal: $90 − $72 = $18 at 5 bushels against $126 − $116 = $10 at 7.

1.2 Why does the textbook insist the costs in its tables include implicit costs?

Show answer

Because the firm maximizes economic profit. Leave out the owner's time or capital and the profit you maximize is the wrong number.

Check yourself

2.1 At $18, what is Jennifer and Jason's profit at 3 bushels?

Show answer

TR = $18 × 3 = $54 and TC = $44, so profit = $10.

2.2 Name one weakness of the total route.

Show answer

You have to compute profit at every quantity. The marginal route only asks whether each next unit is worth it.

03

The principle of marginal analysis

★ Exam
Definition

Keep doing something as long as the extra benefit of doing it once more is at least the extra cost. Stop where marginal benefit equals marginal cost.

Textbook page 537.

You met this rule in Unit 1, for a consumer choosing how much to buy and a student choosing how long to study. Your textbook's margin definition: “every activity should continue until marginal benefit equals marginal cost.”

Module 53 applies it to a firm. The benefit of one more unit of output is the revenue it brings in — marginal revenue. The cost is what it adds to total cost — marginal cost.

Watch out. Marginal analysis compares the next unit's benefit and cost. It does not compare totals, and it does not care what was spent before.

1

A student studying for a test. Another hour is worth it while the extra marks are worth more to them than the hour.

2

A consumer buying pizza slices. One more slice while its marginal benefit is at least its price.

3

Jennifer and Jason's fifth bushel. It brings in $18 and costs $16 more to grow, so it is worth growing.

How it connects. For a firm, its benefit side is marginal revenue (entry 4) and its cost side marginal cost (entry 5); the difference is the net gain of a unit (entry 6), the firm's version of the rule is the profit-maximizing rule (entry 7), and with whole units it becomes the last unit worth producing (entry 8).

Check yourself

3.1 What are the marginal benefit and the marginal cost of a firm's next unit of output?

Show answer

The marginal benefit is the extra revenue it brings in, marginal revenue. The marginal cost is the extra cost of producing it.

3.2 Why does the rule say “at least” rather than “more than”?

Show answer

A unit whose benefit exactly equals its cost leaves you no worse off. Doing it or not makes no difference, and the rule's convention is to do it.

04

Marginal revenue

★ Exam
Definition

The change in total revenue from selling one more unit: MR = ΔTR / ΔQ.

Textbook page 537.

For Jennifer and Jason, every extra bushel sells for the market price of $18, so total revenue rises by $18 each time: $0, $18, $36, $54 … Their marginal revenue is $18 for every bushel.

That is a special case. A seller who has to cut its price to sell more gains less than the price on the extra unit, because the lower price applies to every unit it sells. Entry 14 returns to this.

Watch out. Marginal revenue is a change in total revenue, not total revenue itself. The marginal revenue of Jennifer and Jason's third bushel is $18, not $54.

1

The third bushel at $18. TR rises from $36 to $54: MR = $18.

2

Any bushel at $22. MR = $22 — for a firm that takes the market price, MR is the price.

3

A bookshop that must cut its price to sell more. Selling 10 at $20 brings in $200; selling 11 at $19 brings in $209. The eleventh book's MR is $9.

How it connects. It is the change in total revenue (entry 2), it is the benefit side of marginal analysis (entry 3), it is drawn as the marginal revenue curve (entry 9), and it equals the price only for a price taker (entry 13).

Check yourself

4.1 A firm's total revenue is $150 at 10 units and $165 at 11 units. What is the marginal revenue of the eleventh unit?

Show answer

$165 − $150 = $15.

4.2 Why is Jennifer and Jason's marginal revenue the same for every bushel?

Show answer

Because they can sell as many bushels as they like at the market price of $18, so each extra bushel adds exactly $18 to revenue.

05

Marginal cost

★ Exam
Definition

The change in total cost from producing one more unit: MC = ΔTC / ΔQ.

1

The first bushel. $30 − $14 = $16.

2

The sixth bushel. $92 − $72 = $20.

3

A rent rise of $10 a year. Every total cost rises by $10; every marginal cost, and the best output, stays the same.

How it connects. It is the cost side of marginal analysis (entry 3), its row against marginal revenue gives the net gain of each unit (entry 6), it is drawn as the marginal cost curve (entry 10), and the curve's points are placed by plotting between quantities (entry 12).

Check yourself

5.1 Total cost is $56 at 4 bushels and $72 at 5. What is the marginal cost of the fifth bushel?

Show answer

$72 − $56 = $16.

5.2 Jennifer and Jason's fixed cost rises from $14 to $24. What happens to their profit-maximizing output at $18?

Show answer

Nothing. Every marginal cost is unchanged, so they still grow 5 bushels. Their profit falls by $10, from $18 to $8.

The net gain of a unit

★ Exam

Marginal revenue minus marginal cost: how much one more unit adds to profit. Produce a unit if its net gain is not negative.

1

The second bushel at $18. $18 − $6 = +$12.

2

The sixth bushel at $18. $18 − $20 = −$2: growing it would cut profit from $18 to $16.

3

The sixth bushel at $22. $22 − $20 = +$2: at the higher price it is worth growing.

Check yourself

6.1 At $18, what is the net gain of the seventh bushel?

Show answer

$18 − $24 = −$6. It would reduce profit by $6.

6.2 Add the net gains of bushels 1 to 5 at $18 and compare with the profit at 5 bushels. Why do they differ?

Show answer

$2 + $12 + $10 + $6 + $2 = $32, while profit is $18. The $14 fixed cost is paid whatever the output, so it is not part of any unit's gain.

07

The profit-maximizing rule

★ Exam
Definition

Profit is maximized at the quantity where marginal revenue equals marginal cost: MR = MC.

Textbook page 537. Your textbook calls it the optimal output rule.

Your textbook's margin definition: profit is maximized “by producing the quantity of output at which the marginal revenue of the last unit produced is equal to its marginal cost.” It names this the optimal output rule. Your exam calls it the profit-maximizing rule, and writes it MR = MC. They are the same rule.

Below that quantity, one more unit adds more revenue than cost, so profit can still rise. Above it, the last unit adds more cost than revenue, so profit has already started to fall.

Watch out. On your exam, state the comparison, not the name: “produce where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.” An answer that just says “use the optimal output rule” may not earn the point.

1

Jennifer and Jason at $18. MR = MC at 5 bushels, where profit is $18.

2

The same farm at $22. MR = MC at 6 bushels, where profit is $40.

3

A firm whose MR is $30 and MC is $24 at its current output. MR > MC: it should produce more.

How it connects. It is the principle of marginal analysis (entry 3) restated for a firm, it is the shortcut to the answer of the total route (entry 2), it is read off a graph at the profit-maximizing quantity (entry 11), and it holds for every firm (entry 14).

Check yourself

7.1 A firm is producing where marginal revenue is $12 and marginal cost is $9. What should it do, and why?

Show answer

Produce more. The next unit adds $12 to revenue and less than that to cost, so profit rises.

7.2 Write the profit-maximizing rule in words a marker will accept.

Show answer

Produce the quantity at which marginal revenue equals marginal cost.

08

The last unit worth producing

★ Exam
Definition

With whole units, MR may never exactly equal MC. Then produce the last unit whose marginal revenue is at least its marginal cost, and stop before the first one that costs more than it brings in.

Textbook page 537, and the free-response rubric on page 541.

Your textbook says it in one sentence: when no quantity makes the two exactly equal, “the producer should produce until one more unit would cause marginal benefit to fall below marginal cost.”

Its own free-response answer puts it the way to write it on an exam: produce “the largest quantity at which MR exceeds MC.” In that question the fifth unit's MC is $16 against MR of $18, and the sixth unit's MC is $22, so the answer is 5.

Watch out. Do not stop at the first unit where MC is greater than MR — that unit should not be produced at all. The answer is the unit before it.

1

Price $15, marginal costs $8, $10, $13, $17, $21. The third unit adds $2; the fourth would lose $2. Produce 3.

2

Jennifer and Jason at $18. The fifth bushel adds $2; the sixth would lose $2. Produce 5.

3

Price $12, marginal costs $9, $7, $10, $12, $15. The fourth unit's MR equals its MC, so produce it; the fifth would lose $3. Produce 4.

How it connects. It applies the net gain (entry 6) unit by unit, it is the principle of marginal analysis (entry 3) when units are whole, and on a graph it becomes the crossing of the two curves (entry 11).

Check yourself

8.1 A firm sells at $20. The marginal costs of its first five units are $11, $14, $18, $23 and $29. How many units maximize profit?

Show answer

Three. The third adds $20 − $18 = $2; the fourth would add $20 of revenue for $23 of cost.

8.2 A student says the answer to the last question is 4, “the first unit where MC is above MR”. What is wrong?

Show answer

The fourth unit loses $3, so producing it lowers profit. The answer is the last unit that pays its way, not the first that does not.

09

The marginal revenue curve

★ Exam
Definition

A curve showing marginal revenue at each quantity. For a firm that sells at a given market price, it is a horizontal line at the price.

Textbook page 538, and Figure 53.1 on page 539.

Your textbook's definition: the marginal revenue curve “shows how marginal revenue varies as output varies.” For Jennifer and Jason it does not vary — it is a flat line at $18, labelled MR = P in Figure 53.1.

Change the price and the whole line moves. Press the chips in the diagram above: at $14 and $22 the line sits lower and higher, and the profit-maximizing quantity moves with it.

Watch out. A horizontal MR line belongs to a firm that can sell any quantity at the market price. A firm that must cut its price to sell more has a downward-sloping MR curve.

1

Jennifer and Jason at $18. A horizontal line at $18.

2

The market price rises to $22. The line shifts up to $22.

3

A firm with a downward-sloping demand curve. Its MR curve slopes down, below its demand curve.

How it connects. It plots marginal revenue (entry 4), it meets the marginal cost curve at the profit-maximizing quantity (entry 11), and it is horizontal because of price taking (entry 13).

Check yourself

9.1 Draw, in words, the marginal revenue curve of a firm that can sell any quantity at $5.

Show answer

A horizontal line at $5 across the whole range of quantities.

9.2 The market price of tomatoes falls from $18 to $14. What happens to Jennifer and Jason's marginal revenue curve?

Show answer

It shifts down to a horizontal line at $14.

10

The marginal cost curve

★ Exam
Definition

A curve showing the cost of one more unit at each quantity. In your textbook it falls, then rises — a swoosh.

Textbook page 538, and Figure 53.1 on page 539.

Your textbook's definition: the marginal cost curve “shows how the cost of producing one more unit depends on the quantity that has already been produced.” Jennifer and Jason's marginal cost falls from $16 to $6, then climbs: $8, $12, $16, $20, $24.

Your textbook promises the reason later. It is the production function in the next modules: the first few workers make each other more productive, and then each extra worker adds less.

Watch out. Because of the swoosh, a low price line can cross marginal cost twice. The crossing on the falling part is not the answer; profit is maximized where MC crosses MR from below, on the rising part.

1

From the first bushel to the second. MC falls from $16 to $6.

2

From the fifth bushel to the seventh. MC rises from $16 to $20 to $24.

3

A price of $14. The line crosses the falling MC near 0.7 bushels and the rising MC at 4. Only 4 maximizes profit.

How it connects. It plots marginal cost (entry 5), its points sit halfway between quantities (entry 12), and where it meets marginal revenue from below is the profit-maximizing quantity (entry 11).

Check yourself

10.1 At $14, why is the crossing near 0.7 bushels not the profit-maximizing quantity?

Show answer

Because after it, marginal cost is below $14: the second, third and fourth bushels each add more to revenue than to cost. Profit keeps rising until the crossing at 4.

10.2 What shape is Jennifer and Jason's marginal cost curve, and when does your course explain it?

Show answer

A swoosh: it falls, then rises. The reason comes with the production function and short-run costs.

11

The profit-maximizing quantity

★ Exam
Definition

On a graph, the quantity directly below the point where the marginal cost curve crosses the marginal revenue curve from below.

Textbook page 539, Figure 53.1.

Figure 53.1 draws Table 53.2: the swoosh-shaped MC curve, the flat MR line at $18, and their crossing at point E. Drop a line from E to the quantity axis and you read 5 bushels.

The graph and the table give the same answer because they are the same comparison. To the left of E, MR is above MC — the green bars in the diagram. To the right, MC is above MR — the red bars.

Watch out. Read the quantity from the crossing, not from the lowest point of the MC curve and not from where MC meets the price axis.

1

Price $18. MC crosses MR at 5 bushels: Figure 53.1's point E.

2

Price $22. The crossing moves up the MC curve to 6 bushels.

3

Price $14. The crossing on the rising MC is at 4 bushels, where profit is $0.

How it connects. It is the profit-maximizing rule (entry 7) drawn, it is the graph of the last unit worth producing (entry 8), and it needs both the marginal revenue curve (entry 9) and the marginal cost curve (entry 10).

Check yourself

11.1 The price of tomatoes rises from $18 to $22. What happens to the profit-maximizing quantity, and why?

Show answer

It rises from 5 to 6 bushels. The MR line moves up, so it crosses the rising MC curve further to the right.

11.2 On a graph, how do you find the profit-maximizing quantity?

Show answer

Find where the MC curve crosses the MR curve from below, and read the quantity directly beneath that point.

12

Plotting marginal cost between quantities

○ Background
Definition

Each unit's marginal cost is the change from one quantity to the next, so your textbook plots it halfway between the two.

Textbook page 538.

The sixth bushel's marginal cost, $20, is the change in total cost from 5 bushels to 6. Your textbook draws it at 5.5, between the two. That is why the diagram's MC points sit at the half-units.

Plotted this way, the MC line crosses $18 at exactly 5 bushels, $14 at exactly 4 and $22 at exactly 6 — the same answers as the table.

Watch out. This is a drawing convention. On your exam you will usually be given a smooth curve, or asked to sketch one; nobody will mark where you put the half-units.

The fifth bushel's $16. Plotted at 4.5 bushels.

2

The sixth bushel's $20. Plotted at 5.5 bushels.

3

The line between them. It passes $18 at exactly 5 bushels.

How it connects. It places the points of the marginal cost curve (entry 10), and it is why the curve agrees with the marginal cost row (entry 5).

Check yourself

12.1 Where does your textbook plot the marginal cost of the third bushel?

13

A price taker: marginal revenue equals price

★ Exam
Definition

A firm that can sell any quantity it likes at the market price is a price taker. Each extra unit brings in exactly the price, so MR = P.

Textbook page 538. The term itself is in Module 58, page 585.

Your textbook says it assumes Jennifer and Jason's marginal revenue is constant at $18 “just to make the calculations easier”, and that this is true of perfectly competitive markets, which come later in the course. That is why Figure 53.1 labels the line MR = P.

For a price taker, the profit-maximizing rule can be written P = MC, because P and MR are the same number. Keep the general version too: entry 14.

Watch out. MR = P is true only for a price taker. Do not write it for a firm that sets its own price.

1

Jennifer and Jason. They sell any number of bushels at $18, so MR = P = $18.

2

A wheat farmer. One of thousands; nothing they do moves the market price.

3

A firm that sets its own price. Not a price taker; its MR is below its price.

How it connects. It explains why marginal revenue (entry 4) equals the price, why the marginal revenue curve (entry 9) is flat, and why the general case is MR = MC (entry 14).

Check yourself

13.1 Why can a price-taking firm write the profit-maximizing rule as P = MC?

Show answer

Because each extra unit sells at the market price, so its marginal revenue is the price.

13.2 A firm sells 100 units at $8 each, and could sell 101 at $8 each. What is its marginal revenue?

Show answer

$808 − $800 = $8, the price.

14

MR = MC for every firm

★ ⚑
Definition

The profit-maximizing rule compares marginal revenue with marginal cost for every firm. Price equals marginal revenue only for a price taker.

Not in Module 53 — textbook page 584, Module 58, and page 611, Module 61.

⚑ Not in Module 53 — learn it from here. Module 53's firm always sells at the same price, so its marginal revenue never differs from price, and it never says the rule holds for every firm. Your textbook says that first in Module 58: MR = MC “will be true for any profit-maximizing firm in any market structure.” The rule's exam name, the profit-maximizing rule, appears nowhere in your textbook.

Take a firm that must lower its price to sell more: demand is P = 32 − 2Q, and the marginal cost of the qth unit is $6 + $2q.

Unit123456
Price$30$28$26$24$22$20
MR$30$26$22$18$14$10

The fourth unit's MR, $18, is above its MC, $14; the fifth unit's MR, $14, is below its MC, $16. So the firm makes 4 units and charges $24. If it went on to where price equals marginal cost, between 6 and 7 units, it would sell units whose MR is below their MC, and profit would fall.

Watch out. Never write “P = MC” as the profit-maximizing rule for a firm that sets its own price. Write MR = MC, then read the price from the demand curve.

1

Jennifer and Jason. MR = P = $18, so MR = MC and P = MC give the same answer: 5 bushels.

2

The firm in the table. MR = MC at 4 units and a price of $24. P = MC would wrongly say 6 or more.

3

A firm with a patent on a medicine. It sets its price, so its MR is below the price; it still produces where MR = MC.

How it connects. It is the profit-maximizing rule (entry 7) in its general form, and a price taker (entry 13) is its special case.

Check yourself

14.1 For the firm in the table, what are total revenue and total cost at 4 units if its cost at zero output is $10?

Show answer

TR = 4 × $24 = $96. TC = $10 + $8 + $10 + $12 + $14 = $54. Profit is $42.

14.2 Why is price not equal to marginal revenue for this firm?

15

Whether to produce, then how much

★ Exam
Definition

A producer's decision has two steps: an either–or question, whether to produce at all; and, if yes, a how-much question answered by MR = MC.

Textbook pages 538–539.

Your textbook is careful here. Before applying the marginal rule, “a potential producer must, as a first step, answer an ‘either–or’ question: Should I produce at all?” Only then does it make the “how much” decision.

The marginal rule answers only the second question. It always finds the best quantity, even when the best is a loss. Whether to produce that quantity, or nothing, needs a different comparison.

Watch out. MR = MC tells a firm how much to produce if it produces. It does not tell the firm that producing is worth it.

1

Jennifer and Jason at $18. Producing beats not producing, and the best quantity is 5.

2

The farm at $14. The best quantity, 4, makes exactly normal profit.

3

The farm at $12. The best it can do, at 4 bushels, is a loss of $8. Whether to produce is now the real question.

How it connects. The how-much step is profit maximization (entry 1); the whether step depends on economic profit (entry 16).

Check yourself

15.1 Which question does the profit-maximizing rule answer: whether to produce, or how much?

Show answer

How much. It finds the best quantity; whether producing it beats producing nothing is a separate question.

15.2 At $12 Jennifer and Jason's best output loses $8. Does MR = MC still tell them how much to grow if they grow anything?

Show answer

Yes — 4 bushels, where the fourth bushel's MC of $12 equals the price. It is the quantity that makes the loss smallest.

16

Economic profit decides whether

★ Exam
Definition

Whether to stay in a business depends on economic profit, not accounting profit. A firm making at least zero economic profit — a normal profit — is doing as well as it could anywhere else.

Textbook page 539.

Your textbook links back to Module 52: accounting profit ignores implicit costs, so a firm can show an accounting profit while its economic profit is zero or negative. “A firm's decision to produce or not, to stay in business or to close down permanently, should be based on economic profit, not accounting profit.”

At $14, Jennifer and Jason's best economic profit is exactly $0: a normal profit. They cover every cost, including what their time and land could earn elsewhere, and have no reason to leave.

Watch out. That is the long-run rule. In the short run a firm can keep producing at a loss: at $12 Jennifer and Jason lose $8 at 4 bushels, but would lose the whole $14 they owe whatever they grow if they stopped. When to stop producing is the next topic in your course.

1

The farm at $14. Economic profit $0 at 4 bushels: a normal profit.

2

The farm at $22. Economic profit $40 at 6 bushels: better than any alternative.

3

A shop with an accounting profit of $20,000 whose owner could earn $30,000 elsewhere. Economic profit is −$10,000. In the long run it should close.

How it connects. It is the second of the two decisions (entry 15), and it uses the profit that profit maximization (entry 1) maximizes.

Check yourself

16.1 A firm's best output gives total revenue equal to total cost, implicit costs included. Should it stay in business?

Show answer

Yes. Its economic profit is zero — a normal profit — so no alternative use of its resources would do better.

16.2 At $12 a bushel Jennifer and Jason lose $8 at their best output. Why might they keep growing tomatoes this season?

Show answer

Because if they grow nothing they still pay the $14 they owe, and lose $14. Producing loses less.

A free-response question

1. (6 points) A candle maker can sell as many candles as it likes at $12 each. Its total cost, implicit costs included, is $5 at zero output, then $13, $19, $27, $37, $50 and $66 for 1 to 6 candles.

(a) What is the marginal revenue of the third candle? (b) Calculate the profit at 2 candles. (c) Identify the profit-maximizing number of candles, and explain using marginal revenue and marginal cost. (d) The price rises to $14. Identify the new profit-maximizing number of candles. (e) At the original price of $12, the candle maker's rent rises by $10. What happens to its profit-maximizing output? Explain.

Show the mark scheme

(a) 1 point — $12, the price: each candle sells for $12.

(b) 1 point — $24 − $19 = $5.

(c) 2 points — 4 candles. The fourth candle's marginal cost is $37 − $27 = $10, below its $12 marginal revenue; the fifth's is $13, above it. Profit is $48 − $37 = $11.

(d) 1 point — 5 candles: the fifth's MC of $13 is now below $14, the sixth's $16 is above it.

(e) 1 point — no change, still 4 candles. Rent is a fixed cost, so every marginal cost is unchanged; only profit falls, to $1.

Things to get right

  • Maximize profit, not revenue, not sales, not profit per unit.
  • Marginal means the change. MR and MC are differences between neighbouring totals.
  • The first unit's marginal cost is its total cost minus the cost at zero output.
  • Write MR = MC, and say it in words: produce where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.
  • With whole units, produce the last unit whose MR is at least its MC — not the first one where MC is higher.
  • Read the graph where MC crosses MR from below.
  • MR = P only for a price taker. The rule is MR = MC for every firm.
  • Fixed cost does not change the best output. It changes the profit.
