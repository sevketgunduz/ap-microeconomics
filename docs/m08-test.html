Section A — examinable content

1. A city imposes rent control on apartments, capping rents below the market rent. On a supply-and-demand diagram of the apartment market, which statement is correct?

  • (A) The supply curve shifts left, because landlords offer fewer apartments.
  • (B) The demand curve shifts right, because more people want apartments.
  • (C) Neither curve shifts; the cap is a horizontal line below the equilibrium, and the market moves along both curves to the capped price.
  • (D) Both curves shift, so the new equilibrium is at the capped price.
  • (E) The demand curve shifts left, because rent control reduces what people will pay.

2. The equilibrium price of a good is $50. Which of the following is a binding price ceiling?

  • (A) A ceiling of $40.
  • (B) A ceiling of $50.
  • (C) A ceiling of $60.
  • (D) A ceiling of $75.
  • (E) None — a ceiling can never bind in a competitive market.

3. The market rent for apartments is $1,000 a month. The city sets a maximum rent of $1,200. What is the effect?

  • (A) A shortage of apartments, because there is now a legal limit on rent.
  • (B) A surplus of apartments, because the limit is above the market rent.
  • (C) Rents rise to $1,200, the new legal level.
  • (D) Fewer apartments are rented, because landlords fear future controls.
  • (E) No effect; the ceiling is above the equilibrium rent, so it is not binding.

4. A city sets a rent ceiling of $1,100 when the equilibrium rent is $1,000. A year later a large employer opens in the city, and the equilibrium rent the market would now set rises to $1,200. What happens?

  • (A) Nothing; the ceiling was not binding when it was set, so it never binds.
  • (B) Rents rise to $1,200 and the ceiling is ignored.
  • (C) A surplus of apartments appears at $1,100.
  • (D) The ceiling now binds: rent is held at $1,100 and a shortage appears.
  • (E) The supply of apartments increases, so the shortage is avoided.

5. In the textbook's apartment market (Qd = 3.0 − P/1,000, Qs = 1.0 + P/1,000, in millions), the city sets a rent ceiling of $700. What is the shortage?

  • (A) 0.4 million apartments
  • (B) 0.6 million apartments
  • (C) 0.3 million apartments
  • (D) 2.3 million apartments
  • (E) 1.7 million apartments

6. Under the textbook's $800 rent ceiling, 2.2 million people want apartments and landlords offer 1.8 million. How many apartments are actually rented?

  • (A) 1.8 million
  • (B) 2.2 million
  • (C) 2.0 million
  • (D) 0.4 million
  • (E) 4.0 million

7. The equilibrium price of wheat is $5 a bushel. Which of the following is a binding price floor?

  • (A) A floor of $3.
  • (B) A floor of $4.
  • (C) A floor of $5.
  • (D) A floor of $6.
  • (E) None — a floor can only bind in the labor market.

8. In the textbook's butter market (Qd = 15 − 5P, Qs = 10P, in millions of pounds), the government sets a floor of $1.40 a pound. What is the surplus?

  • (A) 3 million pounds
  • (B) 14 million pounds
  • (C) 4 million pounds
  • (D) 8 million pounds
  • (E) 6 million pounds

9. Under the textbook's $1.20 butter floor, producers offer 12 million pounds and buyers want 9 million. If the government does not buy the surplus, how much butter is sold?

  • (A) 12 million pounds
  • (B) 10 million pounds
  • (C) 9 million pounds
  • (D) 3 million pounds
  • (E) 21 million pounds

10. In a labor market the equilibrium wage is $7 and 80 workers are hired. A minimum wage of $10 is set; at $10 firms want to hire 50 workers and 110 people want to work. Which pair is correct?

  • (A) 80 hired; surplus of labor 30.
  • (B) 50 hired; surplus of labor 60.
  • (C) 110 hired; no surplus.
  • (D) 50 hired; surplus of labor 30.
  • (E) 80 hired; surplus of labor 110.

11. With the same $10 minimum wage, how many workers who had jobs at the equilibrium wage lose them, and how many people are newly looking for work who were not at $7?

  • (A) 60 lose jobs; 30 newly looking.
  • (B) 50 lose jobs; 110 newly looking.
  • (C) 30 lose jobs; 60 newly looking.
  • (D) 0 lose jobs; 60 newly looking.
  • (E) 30 lose jobs; 30 newly looking.

12. A student says: "A price ceiling and a price floor have opposite effects, so one raises the quantity bought and sold and the other lowers it." What is the best response?

  • (A) Both reduce the quantity bought and sold below the equilibrium quantity, because the quantity traded is the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied.
  • (B) The student is right: a ceiling raises the quantity traded and a floor lowers it.
  • (C) The student is right: a floor raises the quantity traded and a ceiling lowers it.
  • (D) Neither changes the quantity traded; they change only the price.
  • (E) Both raise the quantity traded, because both create an imbalance that has to be filled.

13. After a $1.20 floor on butter, farmers produce 12 million pounds instead of 10 million. A student concludes: "The floor increased the amount of butter in the market, so it increased the quantity of butter bought and sold." What is wrong?

  • (A) Nothing; production rose, so sales rose.
  • (B) Production did not rise; supply curves cannot move in response to a floor.
  • (C) Supplied is not sold. Buyers take only 9 million pounds at $1.20, so sales fell from 10 million to 9 million; the extra 3 million is a surplus.
  • (D) Sales rose to 12 million, but consumer surplus fell.
  • (E) Sales stayed at 10 million, because the floor only redistributes revenue.

14. Under the textbook's $800 rent ceiling, 1.8 million apartments are rented. At that quantity, renters would pay $1,200 a month for one more apartment and landlords would supply one more for $800. What does this show?

  • (A) The ceiling has raised allocative efficiency, because rent is lower.
  • (B) The market is efficient, because quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at 1.8 million.
  • (C) Landlords are making excess profits on the 1.8 millionth apartment.
  • (D) The market is not allocatively efficient: an extra apartment is worth more to a renter than it costs a landlord to supply, so mutually beneficial trades are not happening.
  • (E) Renters value apartments less than landlords do.

15. For the textbook's $800 rent ceiling, what is the deadweight loss? (At 1.8 million apartments, renters would pay $1,200 and landlords need $800; the equilibrium is 2.0 million.)

  • (A) $80 million a month
  • (B) $40 million a month
  • (C) $160 million a month
  • (D) $400 million a month
  • (E) $20 million a month

16. For the textbook's $1.20 butter floor, what is the deadweight loss? (At 9 million pounds, buyers would pay $1.20 and sellers need $0.90; the equilibrium is 10 million pounds.)

  • (A) $300,000
  • (B) $450,000
  • (C) $1.35 million
  • (D) $900,000
  • (E) $150,000

17. A student computes the deadweight loss of the $800 rent ceiling as ½ × 0.4 million × $400 = $80 million a month. What is the error?

  • (A) The base of the triangle is the drop in the quantity traded, 2.0 − 1.8 = 0.2 million, not the shortage of 0.4 million.
  • (B) There is no error; the shortage is the base of the deadweight-loss triangle.
  • (C) The height should be $1,000, the equilibrium rent.
  • (D) Deadweight loss under a ceiling is a rectangle, not a triangle.
  • (E) Deadweight loss should be calculated at the equilibrium price, where it is zero.

18. The city is deciding between a rent ceiling of $800 and one of $700. With the textbook's apartment schedules, how do the deadweight losses compare?

  • (A) They are equal, because both ceilings are below equilibrium.
  • (B) The $800 ceiling has the larger deadweight loss, because it is closer to equilibrium.
  • (C) The $700 ceiling has the larger deadweight loss: $90 million a month against $40 million.
  • (D) The $700 ceiling has a deadweight loss of $60 million a month against $40 million.
  • (E) Neither has a deadweight loss, because the apartments still exist.

19. Under the textbook's $800 rent ceiling, who gains?

  • (A) All renters, because rent is lower.
  • (B) The renters who still get apartments, at $800 instead of $1,000.
  • (C) Landlords, because demand for their apartments rises.
  • (D) The 400,000 people in the shortage, because they will be housed later.
  • (E) Nobody; every participant loses.

20. Under the $800 ceiling, the 1.8 million renters who still have apartments each pay $200 a month less than at equilibrium, and landlords receive $200 a month less on each. A student calls that $360 million a month "the deadweight loss of rent control." What is wrong?

  • (A) Nothing; the money landlords lose is the deadweight loss.
  • (B) The figure is right but should be called consumer surplus.
  • (C) The deadweight loss is $360 million plus $40 million.
  • (D) That $360 million is a transfer from landlords to renters: one side's loss is the other's gain. The deadweight loss is the $40 million lost to both sides from the 0.2 million apartments no longer rented.
  • (E) There is no deadweight loss because the transfer cancels out.

21. In the textbook's butter market, consumer surplus is $10 million and producer surplus $5 million at the $1.00 equilibrium. After the $1.20 floor, with 9 million pounds sold, which statement is correct?

  • (A) Consumer surplus and producer surplus both fall, by $0.15 million in total.
  • (B) Consumer surplus rises and producer surplus falls, because buyers get butter more reliably.
  • (C) Both rise, because the price is higher and farmers produce more.
  • (D) Consumer surplus falls by $3 million and producer surplus is unchanged.
  • (E) Consumer surplus falls to $8.1 million and producer surplus rises to $6.75 million, so total surplus falls by $0.15 million — the deadweight loss.

Solutions

Question 1 — Price controls · Analysis

Answer: (C)

A price control is a law about the price, not a change in what buyers want or sellers can offer. The curves stay put; the market moves along each curve to the capped price, which is why quantity supplied falls and quantity demanded rises.

  • (A) ✗ Landlords offer fewer apartments by moving down their unchanged supply curve.
  • (B) ✗ More people want apartments by moving down the unchanged demand curve.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Nothing shifts, and there is no new equilibrium — there is a shortage.
  • (E) ✗ Willingness to pay is unchanged; only the price allowed has changed.

Question 2 — Price ceiling · Application

Answer: (A)

A ceiling binds only when it is set below the equilibrium price.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ At the equilibrium price the market already clears; nothing is constrained.
  • (C) ✗ Above equilibrium, so not binding.
  • (D) ✗ Above equilibrium, so not binding.
  • (E) ✗ Ceilings bind in competitive markets whenever they are set below equilibrium; that is the whole of the module.

Question 3 — Binding and non-binding controls · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Krugman's own example (p. 80): a ceiling of $1,200 on a $1,000 market "won't be binding … and it will have no effect."

  • (A) ✗ A legal limit only creates a shortage when it is below the market price.
  • (B) ✗ A ceiling cannot create a surplus; above the market price it does nothing.
  • (C) ✗ A ceiling is a maximum, not a target. Nobody has to charge it.
  • (D) ✗ Invents an expectations effect the scenario does not give.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 4 — Binding and non-binding controls · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Two modules at once. The demand increase (Module 7) raises the price the market would set to $1,200; the ceiling of $1,100 is now below that, so it binds and a shortage appears at $1,100.

  • (A) ✗ Whether a ceiling binds depends on today's equilibrium, not on the day it was set.
  • (B) ✗ The law holds rent at $1,100.
  • (C) ✗ A binding ceiling creates a shortage, not a surplus.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ A new employer raises demand, not supply.

Question 5 — The shortage from a price ceiling · Application

Answer: (B)

At $700: Qd = 3.0 − 0.7 = 2.3 million and Qs = 1.0 + 0.7 = 1.7 million. Shortage = 2.3 − 1.7 = 0.6 million.

  • (A) ✗ The shortage at $800, not $700.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The drop in quantity supplied from equilibrium (2.0 − 1.7), not the shortage.
  • (D) ✗ Quantity demanded, not the shortage.
  • (E) ✗ Quantity supplied, not the shortage.

Question 6 — The shortage from a price ceiling · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The quantity traded is the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied. Renters cannot rent apartments landlords do not offer.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ That many want apartments; only 1.8 million exist at $800.
  • (C) ✗ The equilibrium quantity, which the ceiling prevents.
  • (D) ✗ The shortage.
  • (E) ✗ Adds demanded and supplied, which measures nothing.

Question 7 — Price floor · Application

Answer: (D)

A floor binds only when it is set above the equilibrium price.

  • (A) ✗ Below equilibrium, so not binding.
  • (B) ✗ Below equilibrium, so not binding.
  • (C) ✗ At equilibrium the market already clears.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Krugman's floors include butter, milk, wheat and airfares.

Question 8 — The surplus from a price floor · Application

Answer: (E)

At $1.40: Qs = 10 × 1.40 = 14 million and Qd = 15 − 7 = 8 million. Surplus = 14 − 8 = 6 million pounds.

  • (A) ✗ The surplus at the textbook's $1.20 floor.
  • (B) ✗ Quantity supplied.
  • (C) ✗ The rise in quantity supplied from equilibrium (14 − 10), not the surplus.
  • (D) ✗ Quantity demanded.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 9 — The surplus from a price floor · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Sellers cannot sell what buyers will not buy. At $1.20 buyers take 9 million pounds; the other 3 million are unsold.

  • (A) ✗ The quantity offered, not sold.
  • (B) ✗ The equilibrium quantity, which the floor prevents.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The surplus.
  • (E) ✗ Adds supplied and demanded.

Question 10 — Minimum wage · Application

Answer: (B)

At $10 firms hire 50 (quantity demanded of labor), and 110 − 50 = 60 want to work but cannot find a job: the surplus of labor. These are Krugman's multiple-choice 4 and 5.

  • (A) ✗ 80 is the equilibrium employment; 30 is the number of jobs lost.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Firms hire only 50 at $10.
  • (D) ✗ Right hires, but 30 is the jobs lost, not the surplus.
  • (E) ✗ 110 is the number willing to work.

Question 11 — Minimum wage · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Jobs lost = 80 − 50 = 30. Newly looking = 110 − 80 = 30. Together they make the surplus of 60.

  • (A) ✗ 60 is the whole surplus, not the jobs lost.
  • (B) ✗ 50 is the number still hired; 110 is everyone willing to work.
  • (C) ✗ Right jobs lost; 60 is the whole surplus, not the newcomers.
  • (D) ✗ Firms cut hiring from 80 to 50, so 30 lose jobs.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 12 — Inefficiently low quantity · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Krugman (p. 84): "You might be tempted to think that a price floor and a price ceiling have opposite effects, but both have the effect of reducing the quantity of a good bought and sold."

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A ceiling lowers quantity supplied, so less is traded.
  • (C) ✗ A floor lowers quantity demanded, so less is traded.
  • (D) ✗ Both cut the quantity traded below equilibrium.
  • (E) ✗ An imbalance is not "filled"; it is the reason trades do not happen.

Question 13 — Inefficiently low quantity · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Two ideas combined: the floor raises quantity supplied (a movement up the supply curve), but the quantity sold is the smaller of the two quantities, which is now quantity demanded — 9 million.

  • (A) ✗ Confuses production with sales.
  • (B) ✗ Production did rise — by moving along the supply curve, not by shifting it.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Sales fell to 9 million.
  • (E) ✗ The floor changes the quantity sold as well as the price.

Question 14 — Allocative efficiency · Analysis

Answer: (D)

At the efficient quantity, the last unit is worth exactly what it costs to supply. Here the next apartment is worth $1,200 to a renter and costs $800 to supply — a $400 gain left on the table. That is the departure from allocative efficiency POL-1.A.4 says a control on an efficient market must cause.

  • (A) ✗ A lower price is not efficiency; fewer valuable trades happen.
  • (B) ✗ Quantity demanded is 2.2 million, not 1.8. Only the quantity traded is 1.8.
  • (C) ✗ Landlords receive $800, exactly their supply price for that unit.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Exactly reversed: the renter values it more.

Question 15 — Deadweight loss · Application

Answer: (B)

Deadweight loss = ½ × (2.0 − 1.8) million × ($1,200 − $800) = ½ × 0.2 million × $400 = $40 million a month.

  • (A) ✗ Forgets the ½: a rectangle, not a triangle.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Uses the shortage (0.4 million) and drops the ½.
  • (D) ✗ Multiplies $400 by a million apartments.
  • (E) ✗ Halves twice.

Question 16 — Deadweight loss · Application

Answer: (E)

Deadweight loss = ½ × (10 − 9) million × ($1.20 − $0.90) = ½ × 1 million × $0.30 = $150,000.

  • (A) ✗ Forgets the ½.
  • (B) ✗ Uses the surplus (3 million) as the base and halves: ½ × 3 million × $0.30.
  • (C) ✗ Uses the surplus as the base and the full $0.90 supply price.
  • (D) ✗ Uses the surplus as the base and drops the ½.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 17 — Deadweight loss · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The triangle runs from the quantity actually traded (1.8 million) to the equilibrium quantity (2.0 million). The shortage is a gap at the controlled price between demand and supply — a different measurement.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The shortage includes people who would not have rented at the equilibrium rent either.
  • (C) ✗ The height is the gap between demand price and supply price at the quantity traded: $400.
  • (D) ✗ It is a triangle, with its point at the equilibrium.
  • (E) ✗ The loss is measured over the trades that do not happen, not at the equilibrium point.

Question 18 — Deadweight loss · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

At $700: Qs = 1.7 million, demand price at 1.7 million = $1,300, supply price = $700. Deadweight loss = ½ × (2.0 − 1.7) million × ($1,300 − $700) = ½ × 0.3 million × $600 = $90 million. At $800 it is $40 million. The further from equilibrium, the more lost trades, and each is worth more.

  • (A) ✗ The size depends on how far below equilibrium the ceiling is.
  • (B) ✗ Reversed.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Uses the shortage at $700 (0.6 million) with a $200 height; neither is the triangle.
  • (E) ✗ The loss is the trades that do not happen, not the apartments that exist.

Question 19 — Who gains and who loses · Analysis

Answer: (B)

A ceiling helps the buyers who still buy, at the lower price.

  • (A) ✗ The 400,000 who cannot find an apartment are worse off.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Landlords receive less and rent out fewer apartments.
  • (D) ✗ The shortage is persistent; nothing houses them later.
  • (E) ✗ The renters who keep apartments gain.

Question 20 — Who gains and who loses · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

1.8 million × $200 = $360 million a month moves from landlords to renters — a transfer, gained by one side and lost by the other. The deadweight loss is the surplus that neither side gets: the $40 million from the 0.2 million apartments no longer rented.

  • (A) ✗ What landlords lose to renters is not lost to the economy.
  • (B) ✗ It is a change in surplus shared between the two sides, not total consumer surplus.
  • (C) ✗ Adds a transfer to a loss.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The transfer cancels; the deadweight loss does not.

Question 21 — Deadweight loss and who gains · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The pattern of the CED's sample question 5, with numbers. Demand meets the price axis at $3.00 and supply at $0. After the floor, consumer surplus = ½ × 9 million × ($3.00 − $1.20) = $8.1 million. Producer surplus = 9 million × $1.20 − ½ × 9 million × $0.90 = $6.75 million. Total surplus falls from $15 million to $14.85 million — by $0.15 million, the deadweight loss of question 16.

  • (A) ✗ Producer surplus rises: sellers who sell get $1.20 instead of $1.00.
  • (B) ✗ Buyers pay more and buy less, so consumer surplus falls.
  • (C) ✗ Consumer surplus falls; producing more does not help if it is not sold.
  • (D) ✗ Producer surplus changes, and consumer surplus falls by $1.9 million, not $3 million.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 22 — Inefficient allocation · Analysis · background

Answer: (A)

Krugman's own example (p. 80). The apartment goes to George, who values it under $850, not to the Lees, who would pay $1,500.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A ceiling causes a shortage.
  • (C) ✗ Rent control lowers quality.
  • (D) ✗ It decides who gets apartments by luck and tenure rather than willingness to pay.
  • (E) ✗ The ceiling binds; otherwise there would be no shortage.

Question 23 — Wasted resources and inefficient quality · Analysis · background

Answer: (C)

Queuing time is a wasted resource; neglected repairs are inefficiently low quality (Krugman, pp. 80–81).

  • (A) ✗ Neither scenario is a black market, and ceilings lower quality.
  • (B) ✗ Both are ceilings, which create shortages.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ A ceiling creates a shortage, not a surplus.
  • (E) ✗ Both controls bind.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 16 concept entries appear across the 25 items. The weighting follows the exam, not the textbook: Krugman gives most of his pages to the four named inefficiencies, which carry three background items here; the released papers in this project name deadweight loss 34 times and none of those four once, so deadweight loss and allocative efficiency carry six examinable items (14–18 and 21). Every figure is computed from Krugman's own schedules, which are exactly linear.

Bloom levels. Application 7 · Analysis 13 · Synthesis 5, a ratio of 1 : 1.86 : 0.71 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The calculation verbs of LO POL-1.A(c) earn seven genuine application items; the five synthesis items each combine two results — a demand shift with a ceiling (4), supplied against sold (13), two triangles compared (18), a transfer against a loss (20), and the surplus accounting under a floor (21).

True premise, false conclusion. 13B starts from something true — a floor moves no curve — and concludes that production did not rise; 18D compares the right two ceilings using the wrong triangle.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: reading a control as a curve shift (1A, 1B, 1E); placing a binding control on the wrong side of equilibrium (2C, 7B, 3A); taking quantity demanded or supplied for the quantity traded (6B, 9A, 13A); using the shortage or surplus as the base of the triangle (15C, 16B, 17B, 18D); dropping the ½ (15A, 16A); and calling a transfer a deadweight loss (20A, 20C).

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 5 · D 5 · E 5. No two consecutive items share a key: the longest run is one.
