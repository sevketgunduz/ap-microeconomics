Section I — Detailed Solutions

Question 1 — Scarcity · Skill 1.A

Answer: (B) · Learning objective MKT-1.A · Essential knowledge MKT-1.A.1, MKT-1.A.2 · Topic 1.1

The CED says that most factors of production are scarce, "but some factors of production (such as established knowledge) may not be scarce due to their non-rival nature" (MKT-1.A.2). A formula used by one person is still fully available to everyone else, so using it gives nothing up. Every other option is a scarce resource, and scarcity is what forces the trade-offs of MKT-1.A.1.

  • (A) ✗ Land is scarce, and more so as the population grows.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. Established knowledge is non-rival.
  • (C) ✗ Skilled labor is scarce: an engineer's hour spent on one project cannot be spent on another.
  • (D) ✗ Scarce, especially in a dry climate.
  • (E) ✗ Capital is scarce; being usable for several products means each use has an opportunity cost.

Question 2 — Resource allocation and economic systems · Skill 1.B

Answer: (D) · Learning objective MKT-1.B · Essential knowledge MKT-1.B.2 · Topic 1.2

MKT-1.B.2 names three systems: command, market and mixed. Veloria allocates most goods through markets and others — schooling, rail, safety rules — through government. A system in which both mechanisms operate is a mixed economy, which describes almost every real economy.

  • (A) ✗ One government-run industry does not make a command economy; most prices are set by markets.
  • (B) ✗ Ignores the large role of government.
  • (C) ✗ Safety standards are set by government, not custom; and traditional is not one of the CED's three systems.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Markets, not the government, decide who consumes most goods in Veloria.

Question 3 — The production possibilities curve · Skill 1.C

Answer: (A) · Learning objective MKT-1.C · Essential knowledge MKT-1.C.1, MKT-1.C.3 · Topic 1.3

Moving from C (20 robots, 75 tons) to D (30 robots, 50 tons) gains 10 robots and gives up 25 tons of wheat, so each robot costs 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5 tons of wheat. The cost per robot rises along the curve — 1, 1.5, 2.5 and then 5 tons — so the curve shows increasing opportunity cost and is bowed outward (MKT-1.C.3).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The cost of all ten robots, not of one.
  • (C) ✗ The inverse: robots per ton of wheat.
  • (D) ✗ The cost per robot between B and C.
  • (E) ✗ The cost per robot between D and E.

Question 4 — Shifts of the production possibilities curve · Skill 3.A

Answer: (E) · Learning objective MKT-1.C · Essential knowledge MKT-1.C.4 · Topic 1.3

A change in productivity shifts the curve (MKT-1.C.4). Because only wheat production improves, the wheat intercept moves out and the robot intercept stays at 40: the curve rotates outward along the wheat axis. Every robot now uses resources that could have grown more wheat than before, so the opportunity cost of a robot, measured in wheat, rises.

  • (A) ✗ A parallel shift needs both industries to improve.
  • (B) ✗ Robot production did not change.
  • (C) ✗ Right rotation, wrong direction for the cost: the wheat given up per robot rises.
  • (D) ✗ A technology gain enlarges the set of possibilities; it does not leave resources idle.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — Comparative advantage · Skill 1.C

Answer: (C) · Learning objective MKT-2.A · Essential knowledge MKT-2.A.2 · Topic 1.4

Opportunity cost of one hat: Alpha gives up 8 ÷ 4 = 2 shirts; Beta gives up 6 ÷ 1 = 6 shirts. Alpha has the lower opportunity cost of hats. Opportunity cost of one shirt: Alpha 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5 hat; Beta 1 ÷ 6 ≈ 0.17 hat. Beta has the lower opportunity cost of shirts. Comparative advantage is the lower opportunity cost (MKT-2.A.2), so Alpha's is in hats and Beta's in shirts — even though Alpha has an absolute advantage in both.

  • (A) ✗ One country cannot have the lower opportunity cost in both goods: the two costs are reciprocals.
  • (B) ✗ Beta's opportunity cost of a hat, 6 shirts, is the higher.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Producing more shirts is absolute advantage, not comparative advantage.
  • (E) ✗ An absolute advantage in both goods does not remove comparative advantage.

Question 6 — Terms of trade · Skill 2.C

Answer: (B) · Learning objective MKT-2.B · Essential knowledge MKT-2.B.2 · Topic 1.4

Alpha exports hats and will not accept fewer than its opportunity cost, 2 shirts per hat. Beta exports shirts and will not pay more than its opportunity cost, 6 shirts per hat. Both gain at any price strictly between 2 and 6 shirts per hat (MKT-2.B.2); 4 shirts is the only option in that range.

  • (A) ✗ Alpha would get 1 shirt for a hat that costs it 2 to make.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Beta would pay 7 shirts for a hat it can make itself for 6.
  • (D) ✗ Exactly Alpha's opportunity cost: Alpha gains nothing.
  • (E) ✗ Exactly Beta's opportunity cost: Beta gains nothing.

Question 7 — Absolute and comparative advantage · Skill 1.D

Answer: (A) · Learning objective MKT-2.B · Essential knowledge MKT-2.B.1 · Topic 1.4

MKT-2.B.1: "Production specialization according to comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, results in exchange opportunities that lead to consumption possibilities beyond the PPC." Gains from trade depend on differences in opportunity cost, not on who is more productive. Only if the two countries' opportunity costs were identical would there be nothing to gain.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.

Distractor design

Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones:

  • absolute advantage taken for comparative advantage (5D, 5E, 7B)
  • a total reported where a per-unit or marginal figure is asked (3B, 9E, 12C, 12E, 23D)
  • an elasticity ratio inverted, or slope taken for elasticity (16A, 16D, 17A)
  • a movement along a curve confused with a shift of it (13B–E, 25C, 25D)
  • a sunk cost allowed to drive a marginal decision (11A, 11C, 11D)
  • the surplus areas of a policy misassigned, especially transfers counted as losses (27B, 27E, 30A, 30B)
  • a subsidy treated as revenue, or a tax as costless (24B, 28C, 28D)

Two pairs are worth discussing aloud. Question 4's two "rotates along the wheat axis" options differ only in the direction of the opportunity cost, and question 21's two "complements" options differ only in the direction of the shift. Students who pick the wrong one of each pair have the classification right and the mechanism wrong.

MKT-1.A.234Most factors of production (such as land, labor, and capital) are scarce, but some factors of production (such as established knowledge) may not be scarce due to their non-rival nature.
MKT-1.A.134Economic trade-offs arise from the lack of sufficient resources (scarcity) to meet society's wants and needs.
MKT-1.B.235Resource allocation is significantly influenced by the economic system adopted by society, such as command economy, market economy, or mixed economy. Each system involves a particular set of institutional arrangements and a coordinating mechanism for allocating scarce resources and distributing output.
MKT-1.C.136The PPC is a model used to show the trade-offs associated with allocating resources.
MKT-1.C.336The shape of the PPC depends on whether opportunity costs are constant, increasing, or decreasing.
MKT-1.C.436The PPC can shift due to changes in factors of production as well as changes in productivity/technology.
MKT-2.A.137Absolute advantage describes a situation in which an individual, business, or country can produce more of a good or service than any other producer with the same quantity of resources.
MKT-2.A.237Comparative advantage describes a situation in which an individual, business, or country can produce a good or service at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.
MKT-2.B.137Production specialization according to comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, results in exchange opportunities that lead to consumption possibilities beyond the PPC.
MKT-2.B.237Comparative advantage and opportunity costs determine the terms of trade for exchange under which mutually beneficial trade can occur.
CBA-1.A.138Rational agents consider opportunity costs, whether implicit or explicit, when calculating the total economic costs of any decision.
CBA-1.B.138Total net benefits, the difference between total benefits and total costs, are maximized at the optimal choice.
CBA-2.A.239In a model of rational consumer choice, consumers are assumed to make choices so as to maximize their total utility.
CBA-2.A.339Consumers experience diminishing marginal utility in the consumption of goods and services.
CBA-2.A.439Consumers allocate their limited income to purchase the combination of goods that maximizes their utility by equating/comparing the marginal utility of the last dollar spent on each good.
CBA-2.B.140Marginal analysis involves comparing the additional benefit of increasing a given activity with the additional cost. Comparing marginal benefit (MB) with marginal cost (MC) helps individuals (firms) decide whether to increase, decrease, or maintain their consumption (production) levels.
CBA-2.B.240The optimal quantity at any point in time does not depend on fixed costs (sunk costs) or fixed benefits that have already been determined by past choices.
CBA-2.B.340The optimal quantity is achieved when marginal benefit is equal to marginal cost or where total benefit is maximized.
MKT-3.A.446The law of demand suggests that a change in the own-price causes a change in quantity demanded in the opposite direction and a movement along a demand (marginal benefit) curve.
MKT-3.A.546The conceptual relationship between price and quantity stated by the law of demand leads to downward-sloping demand curves explained by the income effect and substitution effect and/or by diminishing marginal utility.
MKT-3.A.646The market demand curve (schedule) is derived from the summation of individual demand curves (schedules).
MKT-3.B.147Changes in the determinants of consumer demand can cause the demand curve to shift.
MKT-3.D.148Changes in the determinants of supply can cause the supply curve to shift.
MKT-3.E.249Price elasticity of demand is measured by the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price or the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to changes in price. Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve, meaning slope is not elasticity.
MKT-3.E.450The price elasticity of demand depends on certain factors such as the availability of substitutes.
MKT-3.E.550The impact of a given price change on total revenue or total expenditure will depend on whether demand is elastic, inelastic, or unit elastic.
MKT-3.E.751Ranges of values of elasticity of supply are described as elastic or inelastic with the separating benchmark being a magnitude of 1, where the change in the price and the change in the quantity supplied are proportional.
MKT-3.E.1052Income elasticity of demand is measured by the percentage change in the quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in consumers' income. Economists use the income elasticity of demand to determine whether a good is normal or inferior.
MKT-3.E.1152Cross-price elasticity of demand is measured by the percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another good. Economists use the cross-price elasticity of demand to determine whether goods are substitutes, complements, or not related.
MKT-4.A.253In a perfectly competitive market, equilibrium is achieved (and markets clear with no shortages or surpluses) when the price of a good or service brings quantity supplied and quantity demanded into balance, in the sense that buyers wish to purchase the same quantity that sellers wish to provide.
MKT-4.A.453Economists use consumer surplus and producer surplus to measure the benefits markets create to buyers and sellers and understand market efficiency.
MKT-4.A.553Market equilibrium maximizes total economic surplus in the absence of market failures, meaning that perfectly competitive markets are efficient.
MKT-4.B.154Whenever markets experience imbalances—creating disequilibrium prices and quantities, surpluses, and shortages—market forces drive price and quantity toward equilibrium.
MKT-4.B.254Factors that shift the market demand and market supply curves cause price, quantity, consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total economic surplus (within that market) to change. The impact of the change depends on the price elasticities of demand and supply.
POL-1.A.155Some government policies, such as price floors, price ceilings, and other forms of price and quantity regulation, affect incentives and outcomes in all market structures.
POL-1.A.255Governments use taxes and subsidies to change incentives in ways that influence consumer and producer behavior, shifting the supply and demand curves accordingly.
POL-1.A.355Taxes and subsidies affect government revenues or costs.
POL-1.A.455Government intervention in a market producing the efficient quantity through taxes, subsidies, price controls, or quantity controls can only decrease allocative efficiency.
POL-1.A.555Deadweight loss represents the losses to buyers and sellers as a result of government intervention in an efficient market.
POL-1.A.655The incidence of taxes and subsidies imposed on goods traded in perfectly competitive markets depends on the elasticity of supply and demand.
POL-1.B.156Equilibria in competitive markets may be altered by the decision to open an economy to trade with other countries; equilibrium price can be higher or lower than under autarky, and the gap between domestic supply and demand is filled by trade. Opening an economy to trade with other countries affects consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total economic surplus.
POL-1.B.256Tariffs, which governments sometimes use to influence international trade, affect domestic price, quantity, government revenue, and consumer surplus and total economic surplus.
