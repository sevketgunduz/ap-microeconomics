1. Which of the following changes can Lakeside Kayaks make only in the long run?

  • (A) Hiring two more workers for its existing plant.
  • (B) Buying more fiberglass each week.
  • (C) Moving from its small plant to a large one.
  • (D) Running an extra shift on Saturdays.
  • (E) Cutting the price of its kayaks.

2. Lakeside's accountant says: "For our firm, the long run is any period longer than one year."

Which evaluation is correct?

  • (A) Correct, because the firm's leases run for a year.
  • (B) Correct, because for most firms a year is long enough to change everything.
  • (C) Incorrect, because the long run is any period longer than five years.
  • (D) Incorrect, because in the long run all inputs are fixed.
  • (E) Incorrect, because the long run is whatever time it takes to vary every input, which differs from firm to firm.

Solutions — every option explained

Lakeside's plants, average total cost = total fixed cost ÷ output + average variable cost: at 200 kayaks small $320, medium $350, large $450; at 400 kayaks small $290, medium $275, large $300; at 800 kayaks small $275, medium $237.50, large $225. The industry's long-run average total cost falls from $500 to $300 between 100 and 400 kayaks, is $300 from 400 to 600, and rises to $360 at 800.

Question 1 — Long run · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The plant is the input that is fixed in the short run. Changing it is what defines the long run; everything else on the list can be changed with the plant as it is.

  • (A) ✗ Workers are a variable input: a short-run change.
  • (B) ✗ Materials are a variable input.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ An extra shift uses the same plant: a short-run change.
  • (E) ✗ A price is not an input at all.

Question 2 — Long run · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The long run is defined by inputs, not by time: it is the period in which every input can be varied. For a food truck that is days; for a steel mill, years. No fixed length is right for all firms.

  • (A) ✗ The length of a lease does not define the long run for every input the firm uses.
  • (B) ✗ A rule of thumb, not the definition; some plants take far longer than a year to change.
  • (C) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: the verdict "incorrect" is right, but five years is just another arbitrary length of time.
  • (D) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: in the long run all inputs are variable, not fixed.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 3 — Choosing the fixed cost · Application

Answer: (A)

At 200 kayaks: small $12,000 ÷ 200 + $260 = $320; medium $30,000 ÷ 200 + $200 = $350; large $60,000 ÷ 200 + $150 = $450. The small plant is cheapest: there are too few kayaks to spread a larger fixed cost over.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The medium plant costs $350 a kayak at this output, more than the small one.
  • (C) ✗ The biggest plant is the dearest at low output.
  • (D) ✗ $260 is the small plant's average variable cost; it leaves out the fixed cost.
  • (E) ✗ $150 is the large plant's average variable cost only.

Question 4 — Choosing the fixed cost · Application

Answer: (D)

At 800 kayaks: small $12,000 ÷ 800 + $260 = $275; medium $30,000 ÷ 800 + $200 = $237.50; large $60,000 ÷ 800 + $150 = $225. With so many kayaks, the large fixed cost is spread thin and the low variable cost wins.

  • (A) ✗ The small plant's cost at this output, but not the lowest.
  • (B) ✗ The medium plant's cost, but not the lowest.
  • (C) ✗ $75 is the large plant's average fixed cost only.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ $200 is the medium plant's average variable cost only.

Question 5 — Short-run average total cost · Analysis

Answer: (B)

With the plant unchanged, Lakeside moves along the medium plant's short-run curve: $30,000 ÷ 800 + $200 = $237.50. A different plant would be a long-run change.

  • (A) ✗ $225 is the large plant's cost; in one month the firm cannot change its plant.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ $275 is the medium plant's cost at 400 kayaks; at 800 the fixed cost is spread over more kayaks.
  • (D) ✗ $200 is average variable cost only.
  • (E) ✗ $300 is the industry's long-run figure at 400 kayaks, not this plant's cost at 800.

Question 6 — Long-run average total cost · Application

Answer: (D)

At 400 kayaks: small $12,000 ÷ 400 + $260 = $290; medium $30,000 ÷ 400 + $200 = $275; large $60,000 ÷ 400 + $150 = $300. Long-run average total cost is the lowest of these, because in the long run the firm can choose its plant.

  • (A) ✗ The small plant's cost, not the lowest.
  • (B) ✗ The large plant's cost, not the lowest.
  • (C) ✗ The medium plant's average variable cost, without its fixed cost.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The average of the three plants' costs; the long run takes the lowest, not the average.

Question 7 — Long-run average total cost · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Long-run average total cost is defined as the lowest average total cost of any plant at each output. A short-run curve is one of those plants, so it can touch LRATC but never lie below it.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The cost of changing a plant is already part of the long-run comparison; no plant beats the lowest of all plants.
  • (C) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: in the long run all costs are variable, not fixed.
  • (D) ✗ The definition holds at every output, on the falling part of LRATC as elsewhere.
  • (E) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: each short-run curve touches LRATC at one output, so it is not above it everywhere by its fixed cost.

Question 8 — Moving from the short run to the long run · Analysis

Answer: (C)

At first the plant is fixed, so Lakeside moves along the small plant's curve: $12,000 ÷ 800 + $260 = $275. Given time it builds the cheapest plant for 800 kayaks, the large one, at $225.

  • (A) ✗ The large plant cannot be built at once; the short run comes first.
  • (B) ✗ A lasting rise in output makes a change of plant worthwhile: $225 is below $275.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ $320 is the small plant's cost at 200 kayaks; at 800 its fixed cost is spread further.
  • (E) ✗ $237.50 is the medium plant's cost, which Lakeside does not have.

Question 9 — Moving from the short run to the long run · Analysis

Answer: (E)

A temporary change in output is not a reason to change the plant. Once the order is filled Lakeside returns to 200 kayaks, where the small plant, at $320, is cheaper than the medium ($350) or the large ($450).

  • (A) ✗ The comparison at 800 kayaks matters for one month only; at the usual 200 kayaks the large plant costs $450.
  • (B) ✗ The medium plant is dearer at the usual output.
  • (C) ✗ Selling the plant would leave it unable to produce its normal output.
  • (D) ✗ In the short run it can make more kayaks in the same plant, at a cost on its short-run curve.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 10 — Economies of scale · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Economies of scale are defined on the long-run curve: long-run average total cost falling as output rises. Every other option is a short-run curve from the previous module.

  • (A) ✗ Short-run average variable cost can fall with any fixed plant; it says nothing about scale.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Falling marginal cost is a short-run effect of specialization in a fixed plant.
  • (D) ✗ Average fixed cost falls for every firm in the short run as it spreads its fixed cost.
  • (E) ✗ Marginal cost above average total cost makes short-run ATC rise; it is not about scale.

Question 11 — Economies of scale · Application

Answer: (E)

Long-run average total cost falls from $500 to $400 to $340 to $300 as output rises from 100 to 400 kayaks. That falling range is economies of scale.

  • (A) ✗ From 400 to 600 the cost is flat at $300: efficient scale.
  • (B) ✗ From 600 to 800 the cost rises: diseconomies of scale.
  • (C) ✗ The cost does not fall over the whole range.
  • (D) ✗ The cost stops falling at 400 and is flat from 400 to 500.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 12 — Diseconomies of scale · Application

Answer: (B)

From 600 to 800 kayaks long-run average total cost rises from $300 to $360. A rising LRATC is diseconomies of scale.

  • (A) ✗ The numbers are read in the wrong direction: cost rises as output rises.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The cost is flat only from 400 to 600.
  • (D) ✗ Diminishing marginal returns are a short-run idea; this is the long-run curve.
  • (E) ✗ Efficient scale is the flat range, not the rising one.

Question 13 — Diseconomies of scale · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The plant is fixed and only labor changes: that is the short run. Each extra worker has less of the plant to work with, so marginal product falls and short-run average total cost eventually rises. Diseconomies of scale are about the long-run curve.

  • (A) ✗ A rising short-run ATC is not diseconomies of scale, which need every input to change.
  • (B) ✗ Average total cost is rising, not falling, and this is not about scale.
  • (C) ✗ Returns to scale change all inputs in proportion; here only labor changes.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Constant returns to scale need all inputs to change in proportion.

Question 14 — Sources of economies and diseconomies of scale · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Diseconomies of scale usually come from the difficulty of coordinating and communicating in a very large organization.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Bulk discounts lower cost per unit: a source of economies of scale.
  • (C) ✗ Specialized machinery is a source of economies of scale.
  • (D) ✗ A wage rise across the economy shifts every firm's costs up; it is not caused by the firm's size.
  • (E) ✗ Falling average fixed cost is a short-run effect, and it lowers cost.

Question 15 — Increasing returns to scale · Application

Answer: (C)

All inputs rise by 40 percent and output by 70 percent: output grows more than in proportion to the inputs.

  • (A) ✗ Decreasing returns would need output to rise by less than 40 percent.
  • (B) ✗ Constant returns would need output to rise by exactly 40 percent.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Diminishing marginal returns involve one input with the others fixed; here all inputs change.
  • (E) ✗ Output rising faster than inputs lowers cost per unit: economies, not diseconomies.

Question 16 — Increasing returns to scale · Analysis

Answer: (A)

If every input rises in proportion and output rises by more, cost rises less than output, so cost per unit falls: increasing returns to scale bring economies of scale.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ That would be decreasing returns to scale.
  • (C) ✗ Returns to scale describe the long run; they say nothing about a short-run curve being flat.
  • (D) ✗ A short-run effect of specialization, not a consequence of returns to scale.
  • (E) ✗ Total cost still rises with output; only cost per unit falls.

Question 17 — Decreasing returns to scale · Application

Answer: (D)

Inputs double, so cost doubles, but output rises only 1.6 times: decreasing returns to scale. Cost per loaf rises by 2 ÷ 1.6 = 1.25, that is, by 25 percent.

  • (A) ✗ Output rose by less than the inputs, not more.
  • (B) ✗ Output would have to double for constant returns.
  • (C) ✗ The kind of returns is right, but with output rising less than cost, cost per loaf rises.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ All inputs changed, so this is about scale; and 60 percent is the rise in output, not in cost per loaf.

Question 18 — Decreasing returns to scale · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

The two ideas differ in which inputs change. Decreasing returns to scale: all inputs rise together, in the long run. Diminishing marginal returns: one input rises with the others fixed, in the short run.

  • (A) ✗ Output rising less than one input is not the same as output rising less than all inputs together.
  • (B) ✗ Both can raise a cost, but different costs for different reasons.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: decreasing returns to scale make long-run average total cost rise, not fall.
  • (E) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: diminishing marginal returns occur in the short run, when an input is fixed.

Question 19 — Constant returns to scale · Application

Answer: (E)

Inputs and output both rise by 30 percent. Cost per unit is $100,000 ÷ 2,000 = $50 before and $130,000 ÷ 2,600 = $50 after: constant returns to scale.

  • (A) ✗ $100,000 ÷ 2,600 = $38.46 uses the old cost with the new output.
  • (B) ✗ $130,000 ÷ 2,000 = $65 uses the new cost with the old output.
  • (C) ✗ The kind of returns is right but the cost per unit uses the old output.
  • (D) ✗ Cost per unit is unchanged, so returns are constant, not increasing.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 20 — Efficient scale · Application

Answer: (B)

Efficient scale is where long-run average total cost is at its lowest and flat: $300 from 400 to 600 kayaks a month.

  • (A) ✗ From 100 to 400 the cost is falling: economies of scale.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ From 600 to 800 the cost is rising.
  • (D) ✗ At 300 kayaks the cost is $340, above its lowest.
  • (E) ✗ At 800 kayaks the cost, $360, is rising: diseconomies of scale.

Question 21 — Minimum efficient scale · Application

Answer: (A)

The minimum efficient scale is the smallest output at which long-run average total cost reaches its minimum: $300 is first reached at 400 kayaks.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ 600 is the end of the flat range, not its start.
  • (C) ✗ 500 is the middle of the flat range.
  • (D) ✗ At 100 kayaks the cost is at its highest, $500.
  • (E) ✗ At 800 kayaks the firm is past efficient scale, into diseconomies.

Question 22 — Minimum efficient scale · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

The minimum efficient scale is a cost idea: the smallest output with the lowest long-run cost per unit. Profit also depends on revenue; a firm maximizes it where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, which need not be at the minimum efficient scale.

  • (A) ✗ The lowest cost per unit is not the same as the largest profit; profit depends on price and quantity too.
  • (B) ✗ A price above $300 means a profit at that output, not the largest profit.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: the minimum efficient scale is where long-run average total cost first reaches its lowest point.
  • (E) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: the minimum efficient scale is a long-run idea.

Question 23 — Minimum efficient scale and market structure · Application

Answer: (E)

12,000 ÷ 400 = 30: about thirty firms can each produce at the minimum efficient scale. A minimum efficient scale this small relative to the market leaves room for many competitors.

  • (A) ✗ A natural monopoly needs the market to be smaller than one firm's minimum efficient scale.
  • (B) ✗ 12,000 ÷ 4,000 = 3 misreads the minimum efficient scale as 4,000.
  • (C) ✗ 12,000 ÷ 1,000 = 12 uses neither number from the question.
  • (D) ✗ 12,000 ÷ 40 = 300 misplaces a zero.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 24 — Minimum efficient scale and market structure · Analysis

Answer: (B)

300 kayaks is less than the minimum efficient scale of 400. Long-run average total cost is still falling there ($340), so one firm making all 300 has a lower cost than any two firms sharing them: two firms at 150 kayaks each would be further up the falling curve, between $400 and $500 a kayak. That is a natural monopoly.

  • (A) ✗ The market is too small for even one firm to reach the minimum efficient scale.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Three firms at 100 kayaks would each pay $500 a kayak, against $340 for one firm.
  • (D) ✗ Two smaller firms would be further up the falling cost curve, not lower.
  • (E) ✗ A firm below the minimum efficient scale can still produce, just at a higher cost per unit.

Question 25 — Sunk cost · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The $50,000 cannot be recovered whatever Lakeside does, so it cannot change the decision. Going on earns $80,000 − $70,000 = $10,000 more than stopping.

  • (A) ✗ Adds a sunk cost to the comparison; stopping would lose the $10,000 and still not bring back the $50,000.
  • (B) ✗ A sunk cost cannot be "recovered" by producing or by stopping; it is gone either way.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the sunk $50,000 to the future costs.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The price should be set from demand and future costs, not from money already spent.

Question 10 is the CED's sample question 8, word for word (Exam Information p. 129, key B). All four distractors are Module 55's short-run curves, which is why it belongs at the start of the scale items.
