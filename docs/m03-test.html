Part 1 — Topic 1.3 (Questions 1–18)

1. A country's transport ministry can send its fixed engineering budget and workforce either to rail maintenance or to road resurfacing. A journalist writes: "The fact that the ministry cannot do both fully proves it has been badly managed."

What is wrong with the journalist's claim?

  • (A) Nothing — a well-run ministry would find savings that let it do both.
  • (B) The ministry could do both if it borrowed, so the trade-off is avoidable.
  • (C) A trade-off follows from having limited resources, not from mismanagement; every allocation

of a fixed budget involves giving something up.

  • (D) The claim is wrong because rail and road are not substitutes for each other.
  • (E) The claim is wrong because engineering budgets are not scarce in the economic sense.

2. An island can produce at most 40 tonnes of fish or at most 30 tonnes of coconuts in a week, and the trade-off between them is constant.

A student says: "So the island produces 30 tonnes of coconuts, because that is the top of the curve."

Which response is best?

  • (A) Correct — the highest point on the curve is what the economy produces.
  • (B) Incorrect — the curve shows minimum rather than maximum quantities.
  • (C) Incorrect — the island will produce at the midpoint, because that balances both goods.
  • (D) Correct, provided the island wants to be efficient.
  • (E) Incorrect — the curve shows what the island can produce for each quantity of the other

good. Every point on it is equally producible; which one is chosen is a separate question.

3. The same island's curve is: coconuts = 30 − ¾ × fish. Four production bundles are proposed.

Which is not feasible?

  • (A) 24 tonnes of fish and 12 of coconuts
  • (B) 12 tonnes of fish and 18 of coconuts
  • (C) 32 tonnes of fish and 6 of coconuts
  • (D) 36 tonnes of fish and 5 of coconuts
  • (E) 8 tonnes of fish and 20 of coconuts

4. An economy is producing at a point that lies exactly on its production possibilities curve. Its finance minister announces a plan that will raise output of both consumer goods and capital goods next year, with no change in the workforce, the capital stock or technology.

What should an economist conclude?

  • (A) The plan is achievable, because efficient economies can always expand output.
  • (B) The plan is achievable only if the economy switches to producing more of whichever good it is

better at.

  • (C) The plan is impossible as described: on the curve, more of one good requires less of the

other unless resources or technology change.

  • (D) The plan is achievable because the economy will move to a different point on the same curve.
  • (E) The plan is impossible because production possibilities curves never shift.

5. Your textbook says the production possibilities curve "shows the amount that can possibly be produced if all resources are fully employed."

An economy is producing 30 tonnes of steel and 40 of grain. Its curve allows 52 tonnes of grain at 30 tonnes of steel. Which statement follows most directly?

  • (A) The economy's resources are underutilized: 12 tonnes of grain are available with no

steel given up at all, which can only happen inside the curve.

  • (B) The economy is producing the ideal mix of goods, since it lies below its maximum.
  • (C) The economy's curve will shift outward once the idle resources are put to work.
  • (D) The economy is producing efficiently, because 30 tonnes of steel is attainable.
  • (E) The situation is impossible: a curve is only drawn for economies using every resource.

6. Two countries each report producing less this year than last year.

Alanda had a severe hurricane that destroyed a fifth of its factories and port facilities. Belora had no physical damage at all, but a fall in orders left a tenth of its workforce idle and many factories running one shift instead of two.

How should each be shown on a production possibilities curve?

  • (A) Both by a point moving inside the curve, since both produce less.
  • (B) Both by the curve shifting inward, since both have lower output.
  • (C) Alanda by a point moving inside the curve; Belora by the curve shifting inward.
  • (D) Alanda by the curve shifting inward; Belora by a point moving inside the curve.
  • (E) Neither can be shown on a PPC, because the model has only two goods.

7. A bakery's resources allow it to produce, in a day, at most 200 loaves or at most 50 cakes, with a constant opportunity cost between them.

What is the opportunity cost of one cake?

  • (A) 0.25 loaves
  • (B) 4 loaves
  • (C) 50 loaves
  • (D) 150 loaves
  • (E) 200 loaves

8. Using the same bakery, a student writes on an exam: "The opportunity cost is 0.25."

Why would this answer lose marks even though the number is one of the two correct ratios?

  • (A) Because 0.25 is the wrong ratio; the correct figure is 4.
  • (B) Because opportunity cost must always be expressed as a whole number.
  • (C) Because the answer gives no units and does not say the cost of what, measured in what —

0.25 is the cost of one loaf in cakes, and the question may have asked about cakes.

  • (D) Because opportunity cost cannot be calculated from a straight-line PPC.
  • (E) Because the student should have used the midpoint of the curve instead.

9. A country's production possibilities are given in a table rather than a graph.

Machines0102030
Tonnes of grain9060300

What is the opportunity cost of one tonne of grain?

  • (A) 3 machines
  • (B) 30 machines
  • (C) 90 machines
  • (D) ⅓ of a machine
  • (E) It cannot be found without a graph.

10. An economy's production possibilities curve is a straight line. An analyst claims: "As this country shifts more of its resources into textiles, each additional tonne of textiles will cost it progressively more wheat."

Why is the claim inconsistent with the diagram described?

  • (A) Because a straight line means the country cannot produce textiles at all.
  • (B) Because the claim confuses a shift of the curve with a movement along it.
  • (C) Because opportunity cost never changes in any economy.
  • (D) Because a straight-line curve has the same slope everywhere, so each extra tonne of textiles

always costs the same amount of wheat.

  • (E) Because straight-line curves apply only to economies without trade.

11. A country's production possibilities curve between corn and wheat is bowed outward. An economist explains this by pointing out that some of the country's land is excellent for corn and poor for wheat, while other land is the reverse.

How does that fact produce the bowed shape?

  • (A) It does not; the shape is a modelling convention with no economic content.
  • (B) Because the first corn is grown on land best suited to corn, so little wheat is given up;

producing much more corn forces the use of land that was excellent for wheat, so each extra tonne of corn costs more.

  • (C) Because land suited to corn becomes more productive the more corn is grown on it.
  • (D) Because the country will always specialise completely in one crop.
  • (E) Because the total quantity of land falls as more corn is planted.

12. On a bowed-outward curve, moving from zero fish to 20 fish costs 5 coconuts. Moving from 20 fish to 40 fish costs a further 25 coconuts.

How much more expensive, per fish, is the second block than the first?

  • (A) The same — 20 fish are gained either way.
  • (B) Twice as expensive
  • (C) Twenty-five times as expensive
  • (D) Twenty times as expensive
  • (E) Five times as expensive

13. Three economies each produce steel and grain, with steel on the horizontal axis. Their curves all run between the same two intercepts, but:

Economy X's curve is a straight line. Economy Y's curve gets steeper as you move right. Economy Z's curve gets flatter as you move right.

Which describes the opportunity cost of steel in each?

  • (A) X constant · Y increasing · Z decreasing
  • (B) X constant · Y decreasing · Z increasing
  • (C) X increasing · Y constant · Z decreasing
  • (D) All three are constant, since the intercepts are the same.
  • (E) Only X has a meaningful opportunity cost; Y and Z are drawn incorrectly.

14. Two economies each raise total output this year.

Norland was producing inside its curve because a tenth of its plant and workforce stood idle; it put those resources back into production, and its curve did not move. Sudland was already producing on its curve; a large investment in new machinery moved the whole curve outward.

Which economy experienced economic growth?

  • (A) Sudland only, because economic growth means an expansion of what the economy is able to

produce, shown as an outward shift of the curve.

  • (B) Norland only, because it put more of its resources to work.
  • (C) Both, because both produced more.
  • (D) Neither, because growth requires a rise in population.
  • (E) Both, but only Norland's growth is sustainable.

15. A government wants to shift its production possibilities curve outward over the next decade.

Which pair of measures is most directly aimed at the two recognised causes of economic growth?

  • (A) Putting idle factories back to work, and cutting the sales tax
  • (B) Raising the school leaving age and funding research into new production methods
  • (C) Redistributing income, and lowering interest rates
  • (D) Encouraging consumers to spend more, and reducing imports
  • (E) Moving production to a different point on the existing curve, and improving efficiency

16. A country produces wheat and cars. A new fertiliser doubles wheat yields per hectare and has no effect whatever on car production.

What happens to its production possibilities curve?

  • (A) The whole curve shifts outward, both intercepts moving by the same proportion.
  • (B) The wheat intercept moves outward while the car intercept stays where it is, so the curve

pivots and the opportunity cost of a car, measured in wheat, changes.

  • (C) The car intercept moves outward while the wheat intercept stays put.
  • (D) The curve does not move, because fertiliser is not a factor of production.
  • (E) The curve shifts inward, because resources are diverted into wheat.

17. An island's curve runs between 40 tonnes of fish and 30 tonnes of coconuts. It has been producing 20 tonnes of fish and 15 of coconuts — exactly on its curve. A storm then destroys 30% of its productive resources, and the new curve runs between 28 tonnes of fish and 21 of coconuts.

What is now true of the island's old production bundle of 20 fish and 15 coconuts?

  • (A) It is still attainable, because the island still has some resources.
  • (B) It is now inside the new curve, so resources are underutilized.
  • (C) It cannot be determined without knowing the shape of the curve.
  • (D) It is still exactly on the curve, because both intercepts fell by the same proportion.
  • (E) It is now outside the new curve and therefore unattainable; at 20 tonnes of fish the island

can now manage only 6 tonnes of coconuts.

18. Two countries have identical production possibilities curves between capital goods and consumer goods, and both produce efficiently. Ithra chooses a point with many consumer goods and few capital goods. Oshan chooses a point with far more capital goods and far fewer consumer goods.

Assuming nothing else differs, what should be expected after ten years, and why?

  • (A) Ithra's curve will be further out, because higher consumption stimulates production.
  • (B) Both curves will be identical, because both countries produced efficiently throughout.
  • (C) Oshan's curve will be further out, because capital goods are themselves resources, so

producing more of them adds to future productive capacity — at the cost of consumption along the way.

  • (D) Oshan's curve will be further out, which shows that Oshan made the better choice for its

citizens.

  • (E) Neither curve will move, because a curve shifts only when technology improves.

Part 2 — Topic 3.7, examinable in a later unit (Questions 19–20)

19. An economy produces at a point on its production possibilities curve, running its factories at full capacity. Surveys show, however, that the goods being produced are not the ones its citizens most want; a different mix on the same curve would leave many people better off and nobody worse off.

How is this situation best described?

  • (A) The economy is inefficient in production, because people are dissatisfied.
  • (B) The situation is impossible: any point on the curve satisfies consumers by definition.
  • (C) The economy is producing outside its curve.
  • (D) The economy has experienced a contraction.
  • (E) The economy is efficient in production but not efficient in allocation: it produces the

maximum, but not the right mix.

20. On an AP Microeconomics exam, a question shows a production possibilities curve with a labelled point on it and asks whether that point is "efficient."

What is the question asking?

  • (A) Whether the point lies on the curve rather than inside it.
  • (B) Whether the mix of goods at that point is the one consumers most prefer.
  • (C) Whether the point is both on the curve and allocatively efficient, since this course

assesses both.

  • (D) Whether the point maximises the total value of output at market prices.
  • (E) Whether the point lies outside the curve.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Trade-off · Analysis

Answer: (C)

A trade-off is what having limited resources looks like when you have to act. It is not evidence of incompetence, and no amount of good management removes it.

  • (A) ✗ Savings would add resources; they would not abolish the trade-off, only move it. A larger

budget still has to be divided.

  • (B) ✗ Borrowing shifts resources from the future to the present. Something is still given up —

later spending — so the trade-off remains.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. Trade-offs follow from scarcity, not from mismanagement.
  • (D) ✗ Irrelevant. Rail and road need not be substitutes for one another; they compete for the

same budget and engineers, which is all a trade-off requires.

  • (E) ✗ An engineering budget and workforce with competing uses is the textbook definition of

scarce.

Question 2 — Production possibilities curve · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The curve reports the maximum of one good for each quantity of the other. Every point on it is equally producible; the diagram does not say which one gets chosen.

  • (A) ✗ This treats a vertical intercept as a prediction. It is one attainable point among many.
  • (B) ✗ The curve reports maxima. Points below it are feasible precisely because they are less

than the maximum.

  • (C) ✗ Nothing in the model favours the midpoint. Balance is a preference, not a property of the

curve.

  • (D) ✗ Efficiency means being on the curve, which every point on it satisfies. It does not

select the top.

  • (E) ✓ Correct. It distinguishes what is possible from what is chosen.

Question 3 — Feasible / not feasible · Application (numeric)

Answer: (D)

Test each bundle against coconuts = 30 − ¾ × fish.

  • (A) ✗ Exactly on the curve — feasible and efficient.
  • (B) ✗ Inside the curve — feasible, though three coconuts are being wasted.
  • (C) ✗ Exactly on the curve — feasible and efficient.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. At 36 tonnes of fish the curve allows only 3 tonnes of coconuts. Asking for 5

puts the bundle outside.

  • (E) ✗ Inside the curve by a wide margin — feasible, and wasteful.

Question 4 — Efficient in production · Analysis

Answer: (C)

On the curve there is no slack. More of both goods at once requires either idle resources to draw on — there are none — or a shift of the curve, which the question rules out.

  • (A) ✗ Backwards. It is precisely because the economy is efficient that free gains are

unavailable.

  • (B) ✗ Switching toward a good the country is better at is a movement along the curve: more of one

good, less of the other.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. It names the constraint and the two ways out, both of which the question

excludes.

  • (D) ✗ Moving to a different point on the same curve cannot raise both goods. That is what "on the

curve" means.

  • (E) ✗ Curves shift routinely — that is growth. The plan fails because the question holds

resources and technology fixed, not because shifts are impossible.

Question 5 — Underutilized resources · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The curve allows 52 tonnes of grain at this level of steel; the economy is producing 40. That is a gain of 12 tonnes of grain for no steel at all — a free gain, which is the one thing that can never happen on the curve. MKT-1.C.2 names the condition: underutilized resources.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. 52 − 40 = 12 tonnes available at no cost, so the point is inside the curve.
  • (B) ✗ Two separate errors. The curve carries no information about what people want, so it cannot

identify an ideal mix; and being below the maximum is the opposite of a recommendation.

  • (C) ✗ Putting idle resources to work moves the point to the curve. The curve itself moves only

when resources or technology change — that is growth, entry 10.

  • (D) ✗ 30 tonnes of steel being attainable says nothing about efficiency. Efficiency is about

whether more of the other good could be had for free, and here 12 tonnes could.

  • (E) ✗ The curve is drawn for any economy. It shows what that economy could produce with every

resource in use, whatever it is actually doing.

Question 6 — Underutilized against lost resources · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

The distinction turns on whether the resources still exist.

What happenedOn the diagram
AlandaFactories destroyed — resources goneThe curve shifts inward
BeloraWorkers idle, factories on short time — resources intactThe point moves inside the curve

The vocabulary matters on an AP Microeconomics paper: Belora's resources are underutilized. Write unemployment and you have used a word this framework never uses.

  • (A) ✗ Correct for Belora, wrong for Alanda. Destroyed capital is not idle capital; it no longer

exists to be put back to work.

  • (B) ✗ Correct for Alanda, wrong for Belora. Belora's resources are all still there.
  • (C) ✗ Exactly reversed.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Lost resources move the curve; idle resources move the point.
  • (E) ✗ The two-good simplification does not prevent the model representing either event; that is

the whole use of it.

Question 7 — Opportunity cost · Application (numeric)

Answer: (B) 4 loaves

Giving up all 50 cakes buys 200 loaves, so one cake costs 200 ÷ 50 = 4 loaves.

  • (A) ✗ 0.25 is the reciprocal — the cost of one loaf, measured in cakes. This is the most

common error on this question type.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. 200 ÷ 50 = 4 loaves per cake.
  • (C) ✗ 50 is the maximum number of cakes, not a rate of exchange.
  • (D) ✗ 150 is 200 − 50, a subtraction with no meaning here. Opportunity cost is a ratio.
  • (E) ✗ 200 is the maximum number of loaves, not the cost of one cake.

Question 8 — Opportunity cost · Application (numeric)

Answer: (C)

0.25 is a genuine ratio for this bakery — it is the cost of one loaf in cakes. It fails as an answer because it is unlabelled: the reader cannot tell which good is being costed, or in what.

  • (A) ✗ 0.25 is not wrong as a number; it is the loaf-in-cakes ratio. The problem is that it is

unidentified.

  • (B) ✗ There is no such rule. Opportunity costs are frequently fractions — ¾ of a coconut, ⅓ of a

machine.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. It names the actual fault: no units and no statement of what is being costed

in what.

  • (D) ✗ A straight-line PPC is the easiest case to calculate from — the slope is the answer and it

is the same everywhere.

  • (E) ✗ On a straight line every point gives the same ratio, so the midpoint offers nothing extra.

Question 9 — Opportunity cost from a table · Application (numeric)

Answer: (D) ⅓ of a machine

Each block of 10 machines costs 30 tonnes of grain, so the trade-off is constant. Across the whole table, 30 machines cost 90 tonnes of grain.

  • One machine costs 90 ÷ 30 = 3 tonnes of grain.
  • One tonne of grain costs 30 ÷ 90 = ⅓ of a machine.
  • (A) ✗ 3 is the cost of one machine in grain — the reciprocal of what was asked. Reading the

question's direction is the skill being tested.

  • (B) ✗ 30 is a column difference, not a rate.
  • (C) ✗ 90 is the maximum grain output, not a cost.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. ⅓ of a machine per tonne of grain.
  • (E) ✗ The learning objective explicitly allows data "from PPCs or tables." A table is enough.

Question 10 — Constant opportunity cost · Analysis

Answer: (D)

A straight line has one slope. Since the slope is the opportunity cost, the cost cannot rise as the country moves along the curve.

  • (A) ✗ A straight line runs from one intercept to the other, so every quantity of textiles up to

the maximum is producible.

  • (B) ✗ The analyst has not confused a shift with a movement; the claim is about cost changing

along the curve, which is a movement. The error is about the shape.

  • (C) ✗ Far too strong. Opportunity cost changes along any curved PPC — which is the usual real

case. It is constant only because this particular curve is straight.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. Same slope everywhere means same opportunity cost everywhere.
  • (E) ✗ Nothing about trade is involved, and straight-line curves carry no such restriction.

Question 11 — Increasing opportunity cost · Analysis

Answer: (B)

The bow comes from resources being unequally suited to the two goods. The best-suited land is used first, so early units are cheap; later units force the use of land that was valuable for the other crop.

  • (A) ✗ The shape has real content — it records how the sacrifice changes as output changes.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. It gives the mechanism, in the right order: best-suited land first, so cost

rises afterwards.

  • (C) ✗ Land does not become more productive through use. If it did, costs would fall and the

curve would bow inward.

  • (D) ✗ Increasing costs are the reason economies avoid complete specialisation — the last units

are the expensive ones.

  • (E) ✗ The quantity of land is fixed. What changes is which land is being used for what.

Question 12 — Increasing opportunity cost · Application (numeric)

Answer: (E) Five times as expensive

  • (C) ✗ 25 is the coconuts given up in the second block. Dividing by the 5 of the first block gives

  • (D) ✗ 20 is the number of fish in each block, not a ratio of costs.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. 1.25 ÷ 0.25 = 5 times.

Question 13 — Decreasing opportunity cost · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Steepness is opportunity cost. Getting steeper means each extra unit of the horizontal good costs more; getting flatter means it costs less; staying straight means it costs the same.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. X constant, Y increasing, Z decreasing.
  • (B) ✗ Reverses Y and Z. A steeper curve means a rising cost.
  • (C) ✗ X is the straight line, so X is the constant case, not the increasing one.
  • (D) ✗ Shared intercepts say nothing about what happens between them. That is precisely what the

shape records.

  • (E) ✗ All three shapes are legitimate, and the framework names all three by name.

Question 14 — Economic growth · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Growth means an expansion of what the economy is able to produce — the frontier moving. Norland raised output by ending waste; its capacity never changed.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. It names the criterion — an outward shift — and applies it to the right country.
  • (B) ✗ Putting idle resources to work moved Norland toward its existing curve. That is the end

of waste, not growth.

  • (C) ✗ Producing more is not the definition. Moving from inside the curve onto it raises output

with no growth at all.

  • (D) ✗ Population growth is one possible source of growth, not a requirement. New machinery is

another.

  • (E) ✗ Norland did not grow at all, so there is nothing to call sustainable. Its gain also stops

the moment the last idle resource is back in use.

Question 15 — Economic growth · Analysis

Answer: (B)

The two recognised causes are more resources and better technology. Raising the school leaving age adds to human capital — a resource; funding research develops new production methods — technology.

  • (A) ✗ Putting idle resources back to work moves the economy toward its curve rather than moving

the curve, and a tax cut changes what is bought rather than what can be produced. Neither touches capacity.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. One measure per cause, and both act on capacity rather than on its use.
  • (C) ✗ Redistribution changes who receives output; lower interest rates can raise investment, but

the pair is not aimed at the two causes as such.

  • (D) ✗ Both act on how much is bought, not on how much can be produced.
  • (E) ✗ Moving along the curve and improving efficiency both work within existing capacity. Neither

shifts the frontier.

Question 16 — Technology · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Technology that helps only one good moves only that good's intercept. The curve pivots, and because the slope changes, so does the opportunity cost.

  • (A) ✗ A proportional shift of both intercepts happens when resources grow, or when technology

improves in both sectors — not here.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. It names the pivot and the consequence for opportunity cost.
  • (C) ✗ Reversed. The fertiliser helps wheat, so the wheat intercept is the one that moves.
  • (D) ✗ Fertiliser is a technological improvement in wheat production, exactly what MKT-1.C.4

describes as shifting the curve.

  • (E) ✗ Nothing is diverted. The country can now grow more wheat from the same land without giving

up any cars, which moves the curve outward, not inward.

Question 17 — Contraction · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The new curve is coconuts = 21 − ¾ × fish. At 20 tonnes of fish it allows 21 − 15 = 6 tonnes of coconuts. The old bundle asked for 15, so it now lies outside.

  • (A) ✗ Having some resources is not enough. The question is whether this particular bundle is

within the new frontier, and it is not.

  • (B) ✗ Inside would mean attainable but wasteful. The bundle is beyond the new curve, so it cannot

be produced at all.

  • (C) ✗ Both curves are given by their intercepts and both are stated to be straight lines in the

original setup, so the calculation is determinate.

  • (D) ✗ A proportional contraction keeps the slope unchanged, not the individual bundles. Every

bundle on the old curve is now unattainable.

  • (E) ✓ Correct. It states the conclusion and the number that supports it.

Question 18 — Capital goods · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Capital goods are themselves resources. Producing more of them today adds to the stock of resources, which shifts tomorrow's curve outward. Consumer goods are used up and add nothing to future capacity.

  • (A) ✗ Consumption does not build productive capacity. This inverts the mechanism.
  • (B) ✗ Efficiency means being on the curve; it says nothing about which point. The two countries

chose different points, and that choice is what differs.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. It gives the mechanism and names the cost — consumption forgone along the way.
  • (D) ✗ The first half is right and the conclusion is not. A bigger future curve bought with a

decade of low consumption may or may not be the better choice; the diagram cannot settle that.

  • (E) ✗ Accumulating capital is an increase in resources, which shifts the curve. Technology is the

other cause, not the only one.

Question 19 — Allocative efficiency · Analysis (Topic 3.7)

Answer: (E)

Producing the maximum and producing the right things are two different achievements. The economy has the first and lacks the second. PRD-3.A.9 is where this course formalises the pair: a perfectly competitive market in long-run equilibrium is "allocatively and productively efficient" — two conditions, and this economy meets only one.

  • (A) ✗ Efficiency in production is about whether anything is wasted, and nothing is. Dissatisfaction

is about the mix.

  • (B) ✗ A point on the curve guarantees no waste, not satisfaction. That is exactly the distinction

the question is drawing.

  • (C) ✗ The question states the economy is on its curve. Outside is unattainable.
  • (D) ✗ A contraction is the curve moving inward. Nothing here has reduced the economy's capacity.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. It separates the two ideas precisely.

Question 20 — Allocative efficiency · Analysis (Topic 3.7)

Answer: (A)

This is the item most likely to be got wrong for a good reason. Allocative efficiency is assessed in AP Microeconomics — but not from a PPC. The diagram is drawn in physical quantities and contains no information about what anyone wants, so the only efficiency it can settle is efficiency in production: is the point on the curve? Deciding whether the mix is right needs marginal benefit and marginal cost, which arrive in Unit 3.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. On the curve, not inside it.
  • (B) ✗ That is allocative efficiency. It is examinable in this course, at Topic 3.7 — just not

answerable from this diagram.

  • (C) ✓ Right that this course assesses both — but wrong about the question. A PPC cannot show

whether the mix is the preferred one, so no exam item about a labelled point on a curve is asking that. Students who pick this have the syllabus right and the diagram wrong.

  • (D) ✗ Market prices play no part in the PPC model, which is drawn in physical quantities.
  • (E) ✗ Points outside the curve are unattainable, so they cannot be efficient — or anything else.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 14 concept entries appear, weighted by their share of MKT-1.C: opportunity cost three items, the three cost shapes four between them, growth two. Every entry in this module is examinable in AP Microeconomics, so unlike the Module 1 test there is no genuinely off-syllabus section here — Part 2 is later-unit content, not background.

Bloom levels. No item is answerable from a definition.

  • Application (5: Q3, 7, 8, 9, 12) — a procedure applied to a new case; all five require

arithmetic, and Q9 deliberately supplies a table rather than a graph, which the learning objective permits and Krugman never does in this module.

  • Analysis (10) — finding the operative principle in a described situation. Q2, 8 and 10 present a

plausible-sounding claim and ask what is wrong with it.

  • Synthesis (5: Q6, 14, 16, 17, 18) — two or more ideas combined. Q6 is the module's central

discrimination, and Q18 requires the capital-goods mechanism and the restraint not to turn it into a value judgement.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error: the reciprocal trap (7A, 9A), treating any output rise as growth (14C, 14B), idle resources confused with lost resources (6A, 6B), the steeper/flatter inversion (13B), and — in 18D — a distractor that reaches the right conclusion by sliding from economics into a value judgement, which the solution calls out.

Answer distribution. A: 4 · B: 4 · C: 4 · D: 4 · E: 4 — exactly even. The Macro edition of this paper keys A 2 · B 7 · C 7 · D 2 · E 2, which a test-wise student can exploit, so six items (9, 10, 12, 14, 19 and 20) have two of their options exchanged here to level it. Only the labels moved — every stem, distractor and explanation is the one the Macro paper uses — which does mean the two answer keys are not interchangeable.

Part 2. Questions 19–20 cover allocative efficiency, which this framework assesses at Topic 3.7 (PRD-3.A.5, PRD-3.A.9). Keep them. If you are marking this as a Topic 1.3 check, report Part 1 out of 18 and Part 2 separately; if you are revising for the exam, the mark out of 20 is the honest one.

Question 20 is the one to discuss aloud. Its option C is true about the syllabus and false about the diagram, which is exactly the confusion a student carries out of this module: allocative efficiency is on my exam, so I should be able to find it on this graph. The PPC contains no preferences, so it cannot be found there. Saying this once in Unit 1 saves an argument in Topic 3.7.
