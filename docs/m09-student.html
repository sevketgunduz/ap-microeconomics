Quantity Controls

Sixteen terms from Module 9 of Krugman’s Economics for AP and two your course adds. What happens when the law limits how much can be sold — who pays, who collects, and what is lost.

Textbook pages 88–93·16 terms· Topic 2.8·1½ periods

The one idea. A binding quota is set below the equilibrium quantity. Buyers pay the demand price of the quota, sellers receive the supply price, the gap is the quota rent that license holders collect, and the trades the quota forbids are the deadweight loss.

The 16 terms

01

Quantity control, or quota

★ Exam
Definition

An upper limit on the quantity of some good that can be bought or sold. Governments usually enforce it by issuing licenses: only people with a license can legally supply the good.

Textbook page 88.

In the 1930s New York City allowed only taxis with a “medallion” to pick up passengers on the street, and issued 11,787 of them at $10 each. Sixty years later there were still only 11,787 licensed cabs in a city of millions in a hurry, and a medallion sold for several hundred thousand dollars.

That is a quantity control: instead of regulating the price, as the rent ceiling and the butter floor of Module 8 did, the government regulates the quantity and lets the price do what it will.

Watch out. A quota limits the quantity, not the price. Nothing in the medallion law says what a fare must be — the fare becomes whatever riders will pay for the limited number of rides.

Textbook

Taxi medallions. New York, Boston and San Francisco all limit the number of cabs, and medallions trade for six-figure prices.

Textbook

New Jersey clams. A federal quota caps the bushels of clams that may be caught and hands out licenses based on each boat’s past catch.

Illustrative

Import quotas. A limit on how many cars may be imported each year. You meet these again in the topic on international trade.

How it connects. It only matters if it is binding (entry 2). It is the quantity version of the price controls in the comparison (entry 10), it is enforced with a license (entry 12), it creates an incentive to evade (entry 15) it, and why quotas exist (entry 16) is the last entry.

Check yourself

1.1 Is a rule that taxi fares may not exceed $3 a mile a quantity control?

Show answer

No. It limits the price, so it is a price ceiling. A quantity control limits how many rides may be sold.

1.2 A city issues exactly 500 food-truck permits and fines anyone who sells food from a truck without one. What kind of control is this?

Show answer

A quota, enforced by licenses: it caps the number of trucks, not the price of a meal.

02

Binding and non-binding quotas

★ Exam
Definition

A quota is binding when it is set below the equilibrium quantity, so the market cannot reach the quantity it would choose. A quota at or above the equilibrium quantity has no effect.

Textbook page 91.

Without controls, your textbook’s taxi market settles at $5 a ride and 10 million rides a year. A quota of 8 million rides binds: the market wanted 2 million more. Your textbook’s own test case is a quota of 12 million — above the equilibrium quantity, so it is not binding and changes nothing.

So the first step with any quota is the same as with any price control: compare it with the equilibrium. Only then is there anything to draw.

Watch out. Compare quantities, not prices. A binding price ceiling sits below the equilibrium price; a binding quota sits below the equilibrium quantity — on the other axis.

Textbook

A quota of 8 million rides. Below the 10-million equilibrium, so it binds.

Textbook

A quota of 12 million rides. Above the equilibrium quantity: not binding, no effect.

Numeric

Swordfish. Your textbook’s Section 2 Problem 19: a 7-million-pound quota against a 9-million-pound equilibrium binds.

How it connects. It decides whether a quota (entry 1) matters at all. If it does, entry 5 (entry 5) says what happens; whether it stays binding depends on shifts in demand or supply (entry 11).

Check yourself

2.1 The equilibrium quantity of fishing licenses is 50,000 a year. The government caps them at 60,000. What happens?

Show answer

Nothing. A quota above the equilibrium quantity is not binding.

2.2 A quota is set exactly at the equilibrium quantity. Is it binding?

Show answer

No. The market already chooses that quantity, so the quota constrains nobody.

03

Demand price

★ Exam
Definition

The demand price of a given quantity is the price at which consumers will demand exactly that quantity — the height of the demand curve at that quantity.

Textbook page 89, Figure 9.1.

Until now you have read the demand curve one way: “at $5 a ride, how many rides?” The demand price reads it the other way: “how much would riders pay, per ride, to take 10 million rides?” Same curve, different question.

From your textbook’s schedule the demand price of 6 million rides is $7, of 7 million rides $6.50, and of 8 million rides $6. The schedule is a straight line, quantity demanded = 20 − 2 × price, so the demand price of any quantity Q is 10 − Q/2.

Watch out. Read the demand price off the demand curve at the given quantity — not at the equilibrium. Under a quota it is the price buyers actually pay.

Textbook

6 million rides. Demand price $7.

Numeric

8 million rides. 10 − 8/2 = $6.

Numeric

Swordfish. Quantity demanded = 16 − price/2, so the demand price of 7 million pounds is $18.

How it connects. It pairs with the supply price (entry 4). Under a quota it is the price buyers pay (entry 5), and the gap between the two is the quota rent (entry 6), which your textbook calls a wedge (entry 13).

Check yourself

3.1 Demand is quantity demanded = 30 − 3P. What is the demand price of 12 units?

Show answer

30 − 3P = 12, so P = $6.

3.2 Is the demand price of a smaller quantity higher or lower?

Show answer

Higher. The demand curve slopes down, so buyers will pay more per unit for fewer units.

04

Supply price

★ Exam
Definition

The supply price of a given quantity is the price at which producers will supply exactly that quantity — the height of the supply curve at that quantity.

Textbook page 90, Figure 9.1.

The same reversal on the supply side: not “how many rides at $5?” but “what fare would drivers need to offer 10 million rides?”

From your textbook’s schedule the supply price of 6 million rides is $3, of 7 million rides $3.50, and of 8 million rides $4. Quantity supplied = 2 × price, so the supply price of Q rides is Q/2.

Watch out. Under a quota the supply price is what sellers need in order to supply — not what buyers pay. The difference goes to whoever holds the license.

Textbook

6 million rides. Supply price $3.

Numeric

8 million rides. 8/2 = $4.

Numeric

Swordfish. Quantity supplied = price − 5, so the supply price of 7 million pounds is $12.

How it connects. It pairs with the demand price (entry 3). Under a quota it is what sellers receive (entry 5); the gap is the quota rent (entry 6), or in your textbook’s word the wedge (entry 13).

Check yourself

4.1 Supply is quantity supplied = 4P − 8. What is the supply price of 12 units?

Show answer

4P − 8 = 12, so P = $5.

4.2 Is the supply price of a larger quantity higher or lower?

Show answer

Higher. The supply curve slopes up: producers need a higher price to supply more.

05

Price and quantity under a binding quota

★ Exam
Definition

A binding quota cuts the quantity traded to the quota. Buyers pay the demand price of that quantity, which is above the equilibrium price; sellers receive the supply price, which is below it.

Textbook pages 90–91, Figure 9.2.

With 8 million rides allowed, riders are at point A on the demand curve and pay $6 a ride. Drivers are at point B on the supply curve: $4 a ride is enough for them. How can the fare be both? Because there are two markets — one in rides and one in medallions. A driver who rents a medallion collects $6 from the rider and hands $2 to the medallion owner.

Compared with the free market, riders pay more ($6 instead of $5), drivers keep less ($4 instead of $5), and 2 million fewer rides are taken.

Watch out. A quota leaves no shortage and no surplus at the price buyers pay. At $6 riders want exactly 8 million rides — the number allowed. The price rises until it does the rationing.

Textbook

The 8-million quota. Riders pay $6, drivers receive $4, against $5 for both at equilibrium.

Textbook

Check Your Understanding 1. A quota of 6 million rides: riders pay $7 and drivers receive $3.

Numeric

Swordfish. Under the 7-million-pound quota buyers pay $18 and fishers receive $12, against $14 at equilibrium.

How it connects. It needs a binding (entry 2) quota and is read with the demand price (entry 3) and the supply price (entry 4). The gap is the quota rent (entry 6); the lost rides are the deadweight loss (entry 7). Set it beside a price control (entry 10).

Check yourself

5.1 At a quota the demand price is $15, the supply price $9 and the equilibrium price $12. What do buyers pay, and what do sellers receive?

Show answer

Buyers pay $15 and sellers receive $9. The $6 between them goes to the license holder.

5.2 Does a binding quota cause a shortage at the price buyers pay?

Show answer

No. The price rises to the demand price of the quota, where buyers want exactly the quantity allowed.

06

Quota rent

★ Exam
Definition

The quota rent is the difference between the demand price and the supply price at the quota amount: the earnings that go to the license holder from owning the right to sell the good.

Textbook page 91.

Your textbook’s Sunil has a medallion and a sprained wrist; Harriet wants to drive. Harriet can collect $6 a ride and needs only $4 to make driving worth it, so she will pay up to $2 for the medallion — and other drivers will bid until she must pay at least $2. The rent is exactly $2 a ride, the gap between the demand price and the supply price.

Over the whole market the quota rent is $2 × 8 million = $16 million a year. If Sunil drives his own cab he still earns it: the $2 he could have charged Harriet is the opportunity cost of using the medallion himself.

Watch out. The quota rent is a rectangle that someone receives — the license holder. It is not the deadweight loss, which is a triangle nobody receives.

Textbook

Sunil and Harriet. The medallion rents for exactly $2 a ride: $6 − $4.

Numeric

The whole market. $2 a ride × 8 million rides = $16 million a year.

Numeric

A tighter quota. At 6 million rides the rent is $7 − $3 = $4 a ride, and $24 million a year.

How it connects. It is the demand price (entry 3) minus the supply price (entry 4) at the quantity in entry 5 (entry 5); your textbook also calls it the wedge (entry 13), and it is what sets the price of a license (entry 14). Surplus under a quota (entry 9) shows whose surplus it comes from; set it against the deadweight loss (entry 7). It changes when demand or supply shifts (entry 11), and its owners are one reason quotas persist (entry 16).

Check yourself

6.1 At a quota of 2,000 units the demand price is $15 and the supply price is $9. What is the quota rent per unit, and in total?

Show answer

$15 − $9 = $6 a unit; $6 × 2,000 = $12,000 in total.

6.2 A medallion owner drives his own cab. Does he still earn the quota rent?

Show answer

Yes. He gives up the rent he could have earned by renting the medallion out, so part of his fare is really the rent.

07

Deadweight loss

★ Exam
Definition

The lost gains from the trades a market intervention prevents. On the diagram it is the triangle between the demand and supply curves, from the quota to the equilibrium quantity.

Textbook page 92, Figure 9.2.

Starting at 8 million rides, riders would pay at least $5.50 for another million rides and drivers would give them for $4.50; the next million are worth $5 to both. Those 2 million “missed-opportunity rides” would happen without the quota. Their lost value is the deadweight loss — lost to both disappointed riders and frustrated drivers.

Your textbook’s schedule is a straight line, so the triangle can be computed exactly: its base is the 2 million lost rides and its height the $2 gap at the quota. The deadweight loss is ½ × 2 million × $2 = $2 million a year.

Watch out. The triangle’s base runs from the quota to the equilibrium quantity, and its height is the demand price minus the supply price at the quota. Do not include the quota-rent rectangle.

Numeric

Taxi rides. ½ × (10 − 8) million × ($6 − $4) = $2 million a year.

Numeric

Check Your Understanding 1. A 6-million quota: ½ × 4 million × ($7 − $3) = $8 million a year.

Numeric

Swordfish. ½ × (9 − 7) million pounds × ($18 − $12) = $6 million.

How it connects. It is the value of the rides lost in entry 5 (entry 5), and it is not the quota rent (entry 6). It measures how far the market is from allocative efficiency (entry 8), and surplus under a quota (entry 9) shows who bears it. A price control (entry 10) that cuts the quantity as far loses as much; it grows when demand rises (entry 11); and the forbidden trades are the reason for the incentive to evade (entry 15).

Check yourself

7.1 A quota of 300 is set where the equilibrium quantity is 400. At 300 units the demand price is $20 and the supply price $12. What is the deadweight loss?

Show answer

½ × (400 − 300) × ($20 − $12) = ½ × 100 × $8 = $400.

7.2 The quota is tightened. What happens to the deadweight loss?

Show answer

It grows: more trades are lost and the gap between the demand price and the supply price widens.

08

Allocative efficiency

★ ⚑
Definition

A market is allocatively efficient when it produces the quantity at which the last unit is worth to buyers exactly what it costs sellers to supply — where demand meets supply.

Not in this module. Your textbook’s nearest phrase is “efficient in allocation”, in Module 3 (page 18).

⚑ Not in Module 9 — learn it from here. Your course says quantity controls on an efficient market “can only decrease allocative efficiency”. The word allocative does not appear anywhere in your textbook.

At 10 million rides every ride worth more to a rider than it costs a driver is taken, and no ride is taken that costs more than it is worth. A competitive market left alone gets there.

A binding quota stops it short. At 8 million rides a rider would pay $6 for one more ride and a driver would give it for $4: a trade worth $2 to the two of them is forbidden. Your course states it as a rule — quantity controls on a market that produces the efficient quantity can only decrease allocative efficiency.

Watch out. The rule is about a market that was efficient to begin with. Your textbook’s clam quota is the other case: when unlimited fishing would wipe out the resource, the free-market catch was not efficient — a case for later in the course.

Numeric

The 8 millionth ride. Worth $6 to a rider, $4 to supply. The quota forbids the next ride, which is worth a little less than $6 and costs a little more than $4.

Textbook

Missed-opportunity rides. Your textbook counts 2 million rides that buyers and sellers both want and the quota forbids.

Illustrative

Measuring the loss. The value of all the forbidden trades is the deadweight loss of entry 7.

How it connects. The deadweight loss (entry 7) measures how far a quota moves the market from it, and surplus under a quota (entry 9) shows who loses.

Check yourself

8.1 Why can a quota on an efficient market never raise allocative efficiency?

Show answer

The market was already trading exactly the units worth more than they cost. A quota can only forbid some of those trades.

8.2 Under a binding quota, compare the demand price and the supply price at the quota. What does the comparison show?

Show answer

The demand price is higher: buyers value one more unit above what it costs to supply, so too little is being traded.

09

Surplus under a quota

★ ⚑
Definition

A binding quota takes surplus from both sides of the market. Part of it goes to the license holders as quota rent; the rest is the deadweight loss, lost to everyone.

Not in this module. Consumer and producer surplus are in Module 49 of your textbook.

⚑ Not in Module 9 — learn it from here. Your course describes deadweight loss as the losses to buyers and sellers, and expects you to say how a quota changes consumer surplus and total surplus. Module 9 never uses the words consumer surplus or producer surplus.

Riders pay $1 more on each of the 8 million rides still taken — $8 million — and lose the 2 million rides they no longer take, worth ½ × 2 million × $1 = $1 million. Consumer surplus falls by $9 million. Drivers receive $1 less on each of 8 million rides — $8 million — and lose $1 million on the rides they no longer give. Producer surplus falls by $9 million.

The license holders gain the quota rent, $16 million = $8 million from riders + $8 million from drivers. What is left over is $1 million + $1 million = $2 million: the deadweight loss, which nobody receives. Total surplus falls by exactly that.

Watch out. The quota rent is a transfer, not a loss: what riders and drivers give up, license holders receive. Only the triangle is lost. A driver who owns a medallion is on both sides at once — he loses as a driver and gains as a license holder.

Numeric

Taxi rides. Riders −$9 million, drivers −$9 million, license holders +$16 million: total surplus falls by $2 million.

Numeric

Swordfish. Buyers pay $4 more on 7 million pounds and lose $4 million of trades: −$32 million. Fishers get $2 less and lose $2 million: −$16 million. License holders gain $42 million. The difference, $6 million, is the deadweight loss.

Textbook

Both sides lose. Your textbook says the deadweight loss is a loss experienced by both disappointed would-be riders and frustrated would-be drivers.

How it connects. It splits the quota rent (entry 6) between the two sides, and adds up the deadweight loss (entry 7) — the measure of lost allocative efficiency (entry 8).

Check yourself

9.1 A quota raises the price buyers pay from $10 to $13 and cuts sales from 600 to 500. By how much does consumer surplus fall?

Show answer

$3 × 500 + ½ × 100 × $3 = $1,500 + $150 = $1,650.

9.2 Is the quota rent part of the deadweight loss?

Show answer

No. The quota rent is received by the license holders. Only the triangle between the curves is lost to everyone.

10

Quotas compared with price controls

★ Exam
Definition

A price control fixes the price and lets the quantity follow; a quota fixes the quantity and lets the price follow. Both cut the quantity traded, and both create a deadweight loss.

Textbook pages 88, 91 and 92.

Take your textbook’s taxi market and three different laws. A $4 price ceiling: drivers offer 8 million rides, riders want 12 million, a shortage of 4 million. A $6 price floor: riders take 8 million, drivers would give 12 million, a surplus of 4 million. An 8-million quota: riders pay $6, drivers get $4, no shortage and no surplus.

All three cut rides to 8 million, and all three lose the same $2 million of rides. What differs is who keeps the $2 gap: under the ceiling, the riders who still get a cab; under the floor, the drivers who still get a fare; under the quota, the license holders. Taxes and subsidies, in Module 50, drive the same kind of wedge — and there the government collects it.

Watch out. The same deadweight loss assumes the rides go to the riders who value them most. Under a ceiling’s shortage they may not — the misallocation of Module 8 — so the ceiling can cost more than the triangle.

Numeric

A $4 ceiling. Quantity supplied 8 million, quantity demanded 12 million: a shortage of 4 million rides.

Numeric

A $6 floor. Quantity demanded 8 million, quantity supplied 12 million: a surplus of 4 million rides.

Textbook

Not always binding. Your textbook notes that quotas, like price ceilings and price floors, don’t always have a real effect.

How it connects. It sets the quota (entry 1) beside Module 8’s controls: the price and quantity (entry 5) differ, the deadweight loss (entry 7) can be the same.

Check yourself

10.1 Which control leaves neither a shortage nor a surplus: a binding ceiling, a binding floor or a binding quota?

Show answer

The quota. The price rises to the demand price of the quantity allowed, so the market clears at that price.

10.2 A $6 floor and an 8-million quota both cut taxi rides to 8 million. Who receives the $2 gap under each?

Show answer

Under the floor, the drivers who still sell rides, at $6. Under the quota, the license holders.

11

When demand or supply shifts

★ Exam
Definition

A quota fixes the quantity, so a shift in demand or supply changes the prices and the quota rent instead. A fall in demand can make a quota stop binding; a rise in demand makes it bite harder.

Textbook page 93, Check Your Understanding 2.

Your textbook’s Check Your Understanding 2: tourism falls, and demand falls by 4 million rides at every fare. The new equilibrium is $4 and 8 million rides — exactly the quota, which no longer binds. Any smaller fall leaves it binding with a smaller rent.

The other way: if demand rises by 2 million rides at every fare, the demand price of 8 million rides becomes $7, the quota rent $3 a ride, and the market would now choose 11 million rides at $5.50 — so the deadweight loss grows to ½ × 3 million × $3 = $4.5 million.

Watch out. Ask which way the equilibrium quantity moves. Towards the quota (a fall in demand, a fall in supply), the quota bites less; away from it (a rise in demand, a rise in supply), it bites harder.

Textbook

Check Your Understanding 2. Demand falls by 4 million rides at every fare: the 8-million quota no longer binds.

Numeric

Demand rises. Up 2 million at every fare: quota rent $3 a ride, deadweight loss $4.5 million.

Textbook

New York, 2004. The city sold more than 1,000 new medallions; owners expected the value of theirs to fall, and fares were raised by 25% to placate them.

How it connects. It decides whether a quota stays binding (entry 2), and it moves the quota rent (entry 6) and the deadweight loss (entry 7).

Check yourself

11.1 An 8-million quota is in place. Tourism collapses and taxi demand falls by 5 million rides at every fare. Does the quota still bind?

Show answer

No. Quantity demanded is now 15 − 2P, so the market settles at $3.75 and 7.5 million rides — below the quota.

11.2 Which raises the quota rent: an increase in demand or a decrease?

Show answer

An increase. Buyers pay more for the same limited quantity, and the supply price of that quantity has not changed.

12

License

○ Background
Definition

A license gives its owner the right to supply a good or service.

Textbook page 88.

A taxi medallion is a license; so is the right to fish for clams off New Jersey. The quota is the number of licenses issued. A license holder can use it, rent it out, or sell it.

Watch out. The license is how the quota is enforced, not the limit itself. The limit is the number of licenses.

Textbook

Taxi medallions. Required to pick up passengers hailing a cab in the street.

Textbook

Clam licenses. Allocated to boat owners by their past catch; many now rent theirs to others.

Illustrative

Food-truck permits. A city that caps its permits is running a quota through licenses.

How it connects. It enforces the quota (entry 1), and it is worth the rent it earns (entry 14).

Check yourself

12.1 What gives a license a market value?

Show answer

Only license holders may sell, and the quantity is restricted, so a license earns the quota rent.

12.2 If a city issued a taxi license to anyone who asked, would licenses be worth anything?

Show answer

No. The quota would not bind, so there would be no rent for a license to earn.

13

The wedge

○ Background
Definition

Your textbook says a quota drives a wedge between the demand price and the supply price: the price paid by buyers ends up higher than the price received by sellers.

Textbook page 91.

The wedge is the double-headed arrow in your textbook’s Figure 9.2, from $4 up to $6. Its size, per unit, is the quota rent. It is background because it is your textbook’s word for an idea you already have: the gap between the demand price and the supply price.

Watch out. The wedge is a vertical distance — a gap in price at one quantity — not a gap in quantity.

Textbook

Figure 9.2. From $4 to $6 at 8 million rides: a $2 wedge.

Numeric

Swordfish. $18 − $12 = a $6 wedge at 7 million pounds.

Illustrative

A tax. A tax drives the same kind of wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep; you meet it in Module 50.

How it connects. It is the gap between the demand price (entry 3) and the supply price (entry 4) — the quota rent (entry 6) per unit.

Check yourself

13.1 In your textbook’s taxi market, how big is the wedge at a quota of 9 million rides?

Show answer

$5.50 − $4.50 = $1.

13.2 Is the wedge measured along the quantity axis or the price axis?

Show answer

The price axis: it is the vertical gap between the two curves at the quota.

14

The price of a license

○ Background
Definition

When licenses can be traded, a license is worth the quota rent it earns. Renting one for a day costs a day’s rent; buying one outright costs what all its future rent is worth.

Textbook pages 88 and 91.

Sunil rents his medallion to Harriet for $2 a ride, the quota rent. A medallion bought outright in New York cost several hundred thousand dollars, because it brings in rent every year. The 1930s owners paid $10.

Watch out. A high license price is a sign that the quota binds hard — not that a license is costly to make.

Textbook

New York medallions. Issued at $10; later traded for several hundred thousand dollars.

Textbook

New Jersey clams. The license to operate one clam boat is worth more than the boat.

Numeric

Renting for a day. In the textbook’s model, $2 for each ride the medallion allows.

How it connects. It is set by the quota rent (entry 6) a license (entry 12) earns.

Check yourself

14.1 What would happen to the price of a medallion if the city doubled the number issued?

Show answer

It would fall. More rides allowed means a lower demand price at the quota, so a smaller rent.

14.2 Why is a clam license worth more than the boat that uses it?

Show answer

The license earns the quota rent on a restricted catch; a boat can be bought by anyone.

15

The incentive to evade

○ Background
Definition

Because buyers would pay more than sellers need for the trades a quota forbids, both have an incentive to make those trades anyway — around the rule, or against it.

Textbook page 92.

New York’s medallion rule covers only cabs hailed in the street, so car services that make prearranged pickups do much of the work taxis would do. Unlicensed cabs simply break the law; being unregulated, they cause a large share of the city’s taxi accidents.

Watch out. Evasion is a symptom of the deadweight loss: the forbidden trades are worth making, which is why people take risks to make them.

Textbook

Car services. Prearranged pickups need no medallion.

Textbook

Unlicensed cabs. Pick up passengers without a medallion, illegally.

Textbook

Your textbook’s multiple-choice 4. Quotas lead to inefficiency from missed opportunities and to incentives to evade or break the law — not to a surplus.

How it connects. It comes from the trades lost in the deadweight loss (entry 7), and it is the cost of enforcing a quota (entry 1).

Check yourself

15.1 Why do unlicensed cabs appear when the number of medallions is limited?

Show answer

Riders would pay more than drivers need for rides the quota forbids, so some drivers supply them illegally.

15.2 How is this like a black market under a price ceiling?

Show answer

Both are trades the control forbids but that buyers and sellers both want.

16

Why quotas exist and persist

○ Background
Definition

Some quotas have a good reason — protecting a resource that unlimited use would destroy. Others outlive their reason, because the license holders who earn the rent defend them.

Textbook pages 88–89 and 92–93.

The New Jersey clam quota was introduced in the 1980s, when overfishing threatened to wipe out the clam beds. New York’s taxi medallions were meant to guarantee quality in the 1930s and, your textbook says, have long since lost any economic reason — but medallion owners did not want more of them issued.

Watch out. Explaining why a quota exists is not defending it. You can be asked what a quota does without being asked whether it is a good idea.

Textbook

Clams. A quota justified by protecting the clam beds.

Textbook

Swordfish. Your textbook’s Problem 19: a 1991 quota to let depleted stocks recover.

Textbook

Taxis. A 1930s quality rule that survives because medallion owners earn the rent.

How it connects. It explains why governments impose a quota (entry 1), and why the owners of the quota rent (entry 6) keep it in place.

Check yourself

16.1 Give one good reason for a quota.

Show answer

To protect a resource, such as a fishery, that would be used up without a limit.

16.2 Why might a quota with no remaining purpose survive?

Show answer

The license holders earn the quota rent and lobby to keep it; the cost is spread thinly over many buyers.

Question 1 (5 points). Draw a correctly labeled graph illustrating hypothetical supply and demand curves for the U.S. automobile market. Label the equilibrium price and quantity. Suppose the government institutes a quota to limit automobile production. Draw a vertical line labeled “Qineffective” to show the level of a quota that would have no effect on the market. Draw a vertical line labeled “Qeffective” to show the level of a quota that would have an effect on the market. Shade in and label the deadweight loss resulting from the effective quota.

Show the answer and the rubric
  1. A correctly labeled diagram: axes Price and Quantity, upward-sloping S and downward-sloping D, both labeled.
  2. Equilibrium at the crossing, with PE and QE labeled on the axes.
  3. Qineffective: a vertical line to the right of QE.
  4. Qeffective: a vertical line to the left of QE.
  5. The deadweight loss: the triangle to the right of Qeffective, between the curves, shaded and labeled.

A sixth point your course would value: mark the price buyers pay where Qeffective meets the demand curve, the price sellers receive where it meets the supply curve, and the quota-rent rectangle between them.

Question 2. Draw a correctly labeled graph of the market for taxicab rides. On the graph, draw and label a vertical line showing the level of an effective quota. Label the demand price, the supply price, and the quota rent.

Show the answer

Axes labeled Fare and Quantity of rides; downward-sloping D, upward-sloping S, equilibrium E. A vertical quota line to the left of the equilibrium quantity. The demand price where the line meets D, the supply price where it meets S, and the quota rent as the vertical distance between them — in your textbook’s numbers $6, $4 and $2 at 8 million rides.

The five things to get right from Module 9

  1. Check whether it binds. A quota matters only below the equilibrium quantity.
  2. Read both curves at the quota. Buyers pay the demand price; sellers receive the supply price.
  3. The gap is the quota rent, a rectangle, and the license holders collect it.
  4. The deadweight loss is the triangle from the quota to the equilibrium quantity — not the rectangle.
  5. Say whose surplus it was. Buyers and sellers both lose; part of their loss is the rent, the rest is gone.
