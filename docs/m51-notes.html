This module is 480 pages out of sequence, and Unit 1 is not complete without it. Krugman is ordered macro-first, so Utility Maximization sits in Section 9 — after elasticity, surplus and deadweight loss. Topics 1.5 and 1.6 have no other home. Decide before you start Unit 1 whether you will teach it here, out of book order, or leave two topics open until later. Teaching it here is better and needs planning: it assumes nothing from Modules 46–50.

The two Enduring Understandings, quoted verbatim:

CBA-1 (CED p. 38) — "Rational economic decisions require the evaluation of costs and benefits."
CBA-2 (CED p. 39) — "To determine the optimal level at which to pursue an activity whose total benefits exceed total cost, rational economic agents compare marginal benefits and marginal costs."

Krugman's four stated objectives for the module (p. 511):

  1. How consumers make choices about the purchase of goods and services
  2. Why consumers' general goal is to maximize utility
  3. Why the principle of diminishing marginal utility applies to the consumption of most goods and services
  4. How to use marginal analysis to find the optimal consumption bundle

All four are examinable. This is a rare module in which nothing is off-syllabus — but it is also a module where the framework asks for three things the text never names. See section 3.

Verdict on alignment. Topic 1.6 is served extremely well. CBA-2.A.1 through CBA-2.A.4 are the whole of Module 51, and the text's optimal consumption rule is CBA-2.A.4 written out. The module also respects the CED's exclusion without knowing it exists: indifference curves occur 0 times in Module 51. Topic 1.5 is served in substance and not in vocabulary. CBA-1.B asks students to explain and calculate decisions "by comparing total benefits and total costs", and the phrase total benefit occurs 0 times in this module — twice in the whole 927-page book. Krugman says total utility throughout, which is exactly what CBA-1.A.2 means by total benefit; the idea is delivered, the label is not. The real gap is CBA-2.B, on CED p. 40: marginal benefit, marginal cost and sunk costs are required in Topic 1.6 and none of the three appears here. Section 3 documents all of it.

The requirements, in the CED's own words

CBA-1.A.1 — "Rational agents consider opportunity costs, whether implicit or explicit, when calculating the total economic costs of any decision." (CED p. 38)
CBA-1.A.2 — "Total benefits form the metric 'utility' for consumers and total revenue for firms." (CED p. 38)
CBA-1.B.1 — "Total net benefits, the difference between total benefits and total costs, are maximized at the optimal choice." (CED p. 38)
CBA-1.B.2 — "Some decisions permit rational agents to look at only marginal benefit and marginal cost. Other decisions cannot be broken down into increments in this way and must be evaluated by looking at total benefits and total costs." (CED p. 38)
CBA-2.A.1 — "Consumers face constraints and have to make optimal decisions accounting for these constraints." (CED p. 39)
CBA-2.A.2 — "In a model of rational consumer choice, consumers are assumed to make choices so as to maximize their total utility." (CED p. 39)
CBA-2.A.3 — "Consumers experience diminishing marginal utility in the consumption of goods and services." (CED p. 39)
CBA-2.A.4 — "Consumers allocate their limited income to purchase the combination of goods that maximizes their utility by equating/comparing the marginal utility of the last dollar spent on each good." (CED p. 39)
Exclusion (CED p. 39) — "Indifference curves are beyond the scope of the course and the AP Exam, but equating the ratios of marginal utility to price is within the scope."

CBA-1.A.2 is the single most useful sentence in this topic for a teacher, because it licenses the translation the whole module depends on: for a consumer, total benefit is total utility. Krugman never says "total benefit"; the framework tells you they are the same thing.

One caution on the budget line. The word budget occurs 0 times in the entire Micro CED. CBA-2.A.1 says only "constraints" and CBA-2.A.4 says "limited income". The budget line is Krugman's apparatus, not the framework's vocabulary — and it is still worth teaching, because the AP Review's own free-response question asks students to draw one and because it makes the affordable set visible. Just expect the exam to hand students a table and ask for the equal-marginal-utility- per-dollar condition more often than it asks for the diagram.

3. Required elements this module does not contain

All three gaps are in the CBA-2.B block on CED p. 40 and in CBA-1.B, and every count below is a search of the module against a search of the whole book.

CBA-1.B — "total benefits and total costs" as a named comparison. The phrase total benefit occurs 0 times in Module 51 and 2 times in all 927 pages. Krugman works entirely in total utility. Because CBA-1.A.2 states that total benefits are utility for a consumer, the substance is fully delivered — Table 51.2 is a total-benefit calculation — but a student who has only ever seen the word "utility" can be stopped cold by a question that says "total benefit". Supply the label, and say the two words mean the same thing here. Total cost is easier: Sammy's total cost is his $20, spent.

CBA-2.B.1 and CBA-2.B.3 — marginal benefit and marginal cost. Marginal benefit occurs 0 times in Module 51 and 69 times in the book; marginal cost occurs 0 times in Module 51 and 401 times in the book. CBA-2.B.1 defines marginal analysis as "comparing the additional benefit of increasing a given activity with the additional cost", and CBA-2.B.3 states that "the optimal quantity is achieved when marginal benefit is equal to marginal cost or where total benefit is maximized."

That is the same rule the module teaches, in different symbols. Make the translation explicit:

CBA-2.B.2 — sunk costs. Sunk cost occurs 0 times in Module 51 and 22 times in the book, none before book p. 563 — they belong to Modules 56 and 58–60 in Unit 3. But the framework puts the requirement in Topic 1.6: "The optimal quantity at any point in time does not depend on fixed costs (sunk costs) or fixed benefits that have already been determined by past choices." 5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

1. Krugman's Figure 51.4 says the two marginal utilities per dollar are both 2 at the optimum. His own Table 51.3 does not. At bundle C the last pound of clams gives 10 utils for $4 — 2.50 per dollar — and the sixth pound of potatoes gives 4.5 utils for $2 — 2.25 per dollar. The "2" is read off the crossing point of two smoothed curves drawn at midpoints, not off the table.

This matters because students treat exact equality as the test and then cannot find it. State the rule as the framework does — equate or compare the marginal utility of the last dollar spent — and teach the operational version: spend every dollar where it buys the most utility, and stop when no reallocation helps. With lumpy units you get as close to equality as the goods allow. Then show FRQ 1, where the numbers happen to be exactly equal, so students see both cases.

2. Equal marginal utility per dollar is necessary, not sufficient. This is CYU 3 and it is the most instructive item in the module. At 4 pounds of clams and 10 pounds of potatoes the two MU/$ figures are both 0.75 — perfectly equal — and the bundle costs 4 × \$4 + 10 × \$2 = \$36 on a \$20 income. Equality plus affordability is the condition, which is exactly why CBA-2.A.4 begins "Consumers allocate their limited income".

3. Marginal utility can be negative, and a rational consumer stops before it. Cassie's ninth clam has marginal utility −1: eating it lowers total utility from 64 to 63. That is true even though the clams are free, which is CYU 1. Students who have learned "more is always better" need this once, explicitly.

4. Total utility rises while marginal utility falls. These are not in conflict, and the confusion is near-universal. Up to the eighth clam Cassie's total utility is still rising — it is just rising by less each time. Marginal utility is the slope of total utility; total utility peaks exactly where marginal utility hits zero.

5. Utils are hypothetical and are not comparable between people. Krugman is careful about this on p. 511: "we don't need to make comparisons between your feelings and mine." Util occurs 0 times in the Micro CED. Use the unit to make the arithmetic possible and do not let a student conclude that one person's 50 utils exceed another's 40.

6. Divide by the price. Always. MCQ 4 exists to catch students who compare 100 utils with 80 utils and ignore that one costs \$2 and the other \$1. Marginal utility ranks units; marginal utility per dollar ranks purchases, and only the second answers "what should I buy next?"
