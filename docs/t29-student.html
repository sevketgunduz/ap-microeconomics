International Trade and Public Policy

Nineteen terms for the last topic of Unit 2. What happens to a market when a country opens it to the rest of the world — who gains, who loses, and what a tariff or a quota takes back.

Reference book: Hoang, Wray and Chakraborty, chapters 27–28· 19 terms·Topic 2.9·2 periods

How to use this guide

Your usual textbook has no chapter on this topic, so this guide works from a different one: Hoang, Wray and Chakraborty, Economics, chapters 27 and 28. Entries 1 to 9 are opening a market to trade; entries 10 to 18 are tariffs and quotas; entry 19 is the wider debate.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and your reference book does not teach it. Entries 7, 8 and 9 carry the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. Opening to trade moves the domestic price to the world price. Whichever way it moves, one group gains, one group loses, and the winners gain more — a triangle of new surplus. A tariff or a quota pushes the price back part of the way and gives part of that triangle back.

⚑ Two warnings about your reference book. It is written for a different exam, so its words differ: it says welfare loss where your exam says deadweight loss, and social surplus where your exam says total economic surplus. And it never says who gains and who loses when a country first opens to trade. Entries 7, 8 and 9 supply that.

The nineteen terms

01

Autarky

★ Exam
Definition

A country that does not trade with the rest of the world. Its market settles at its own domestic equilibrium.

Reference book, page 428.

Your reference book defines it as self-sufficiency: “a country that does not engage in international trade”. In the olive market drawn above, autarky means domestic demand and domestic supply meet at $7 a kilogram and 50 thousand kilograms.

Autarky is the starting point for everything in this topic. Every question about trade compares something to it: is the world price above or below the autarky price, and what happens to each group when the country opens up?

Watch out. Autarky is a benchmark, not a policy anyone recommends. Exam questions often say “no trade” instead of the word itself — they mean the same thing.

1

The olive market closed to trade. $7 and 50 thousand kilograms, set by domestic demand and supply alone.

2

Japan between 1639 and 1853. Your reference book's historical example of a country closed to trade.

3

A good no one imports or exports. Its market behaves as if it were in autarky, whatever the country's wider policy.

How it connects. It is the benchmark for the world price (entry 2), it is where both a world price below it (entry 4) and a world price above it (entry 5) start from, and it is what the gain from trade (entry 9) is measured against.

Check yourself

1.1 Domestic demand is Qd = 120 − 10P and domestic supply is Qs = 10P − 20. What is the autarky price and quantity?

Show answer

Set them equal: 120 − 10P = 10P − 20, so P = $7 and Q = 50.

1.2 Why does every trade question start from autarky?

Show answer

Because the gains and losses from trade are measured as changes from it. Without the no-trade equilibrium there is nothing to compare free trade with.

02

World price

★ Exam
Definition

The price at which the rest of the world will buy or sell any quantity of a good. A country open to trade faces it in place of its own equilibrium price.

Reference book, page 425, Figures 27.5 and 27.6.

Once a market opens, its price is no longer set by domestic demand and supply alone. Buyers can buy from abroad and sellers can sell abroad, so the domestic price is pulled to the world price, Pw.

Whether that is good or bad news for a given group depends on one comparison only: is the world price below or above the autarky price?

Watch out. The world price is not the price of imports “plus transport” or “plus a margin”. In the model it is simply the price, and the domestic price becomes equal to it.

1

Olives at a world price of $4. Below the autarky price of $7, so the country will import.

2

Olives at a world price of $9. Above $7, so the country will export.

3

Olives at a world price of $7. Exactly the autarky price: opening to trade changes nothing.

How it connects. It replaces the autarky price (entry 1) as the price that matters, it is taken as given because the country is a price taker (entry 3), and its position decides whether the country imports (entry 4).

Check yourself

2.1 A country's autarky price for rice is $30 and the world price is $22. Will it import or export?

Show answer

Import. The world price is below the autarky price, so domestic buyers can buy more cheaply abroad.

2.2 Can a country import a good it is perfectly able to produce?

Show answer

Yes — it imports whenever the world price is below its autarky price, which means other countries can supply it more cheaply. Domestic producers keep supplying the part they can sell at the world price.

03

A small country is a price taker

★ Exam
Definition

The world market is so large that one country's purchases or sales cannot move the world price. It faces a perfectly elastic world supply: a horizontal line at Pw.

Reference book, pages 438 and 445.

Your reference book states the assumption every time it draws the diagram: world supply is “so large that, realistically, no amount of demand … would be able to generate enough scarcity for the price … to increase.” So the world supply curve is horizontal.

You met exactly this line in Module 47: perfectly elastic supply. Here it means the country can buy as much as it likes at Pw and nothing below it.

Watch out. Every diagram in this topic depends on this assumption. If the country could push the world price up by importing more, the tariff analysis would change — and your course does not cover that case.

1

A small country importing wheat. It takes the world price and buys the gap at that price.

2

A small country exporting coffee. It sells any amount abroad at the world price.

3

A very large importer. Could move the world price — beyond your course.

How it connects. It is the reason the world price (entry 2) is a horizontal line, and it is why a tariff (entry 10) raises the domestic price by the full amount of the tax.

Check yourself

3.1 What does the world supply curve look like for a price-taking country, and why?

Show answer

Horizontal at the world price, because the world will supply any quantity the country wants at that price and the country's demand is too small to move it.

3.2 Why does the price-taker assumption matter for a tariff?

Show answer

Because with a horizontal world supply, a $1 tariff raises the domestic price of imports by the full $1. If the country could push the world price down, part of the tariff would fall on foreign sellers.

04

A world price below autarky

★ Exam
Definition

When the world price is below the autarky price, the domestic price falls to it: quantity demanded rises, domestic quantity supplied falls, and the country imports.

Reference book, page 425, Figure 27.6.

Open the olive market at a world price of $4:

  • Domestic buyers now want 80 thousand kilograms (up from 50).
  • Domestic growers now supply only 20 thousand (down from 50).
  • The 60 thousand in between are imported.

This is the case your course's own model graph draws: autarky at P1 and Q1, the world price below it, and the two new quantities labelled QS2 and QD2.

Watch out. Domestic production does not drop to zero. Growers whose cost is below the world price keep selling; only the higher-cost ones stop.

1

World price $4. Qd 80, Qs 20, imports 60.

2

World price $6. Qd 60, Qs 40, imports 20 — a smaller gap the closer the world price is to autarky.

3

World price $2. Qs 0 and Qd 100: every kilogram is imported.

How it connects. It starts from autarky (entry 1) at a world price below it (entry 2), the size of the gap is what trade fills (entry 6), and its winners and losers are entry 7's subject (entry 7).

Check yourself

4.1 At a world price of $5, how much does the olive market import?

Show answer

Qd = 120 − 50 = 70 and Qs = 50 − 20 = 30, so imports are 40 thousand kilograms.

4.2 A world price below autarky lowers the domestic price. Who is hurt?

Show answer

Domestic producers: they sell less, at a lower price. Who gains is entry 7.

05

A world price above autarky

★ Exam
Definition

When the world price is above the autarky price, the domestic price rises to it: domestic quantity supplied rises, quantity demanded falls, and the country exports the difference.

Reference book, page 425, Figure 27.5.

Open the olive market at a world price of $9 instead:

  • Domestic growers now supply 70 thousand kilograms (up from 50).
  • Domestic buyers now want only 30 thousand (down from 50).
  • The 40 thousand left over are exported.

Your course says it in one phrase: the equilibrium price “can be higher or lower than under autarky”. The exporting case is the one students forget, so practise it as often as the importing one.

Watch out. When a country exports, its own consumers pay the higher world price too. Growers will not sell at home for less than they can get abroad.

1

World price $9. Qs 70, Qd 30, exports 40.

2

World price $8. Qs 60, Qd 40, exports 20.

3

World price $12. Qd 0: domestic buyers are priced out entirely, and all 100 thousand kilograms are exported.

How it connects. It is the other side of autarky (entry 1), its gap is filled the other way (entry 6), and its winners and losers are the reverse of the importing case (entry 8).

Check yourself

5.1 A country's autarky price for beef is $10 and the world price is $14. Does it import or export, and what happens to the price domestic buyers pay?

Show answer

It exports, and the domestic price rises to $14 — domestic buyers pay more than under autarky.

5.2 At a world price of $10, how much does the olive market export?

Show answer

Qs = 100 − 20 = 80 and Qd = 120 − 100 = 20, so exports are 60 thousand kilograms.

06

Trade fills the gap

★ Exam
Definition

Imports equal domestic quantity demanded minus domestic quantity supplied; exports equal domestic quantity supplied minus domestic quantity demanded, both at the world price.

Reference book, pages 425 and 442.

imports = Qd − Qs     exports = Qs − Qd    (at the world price)

Your course's wording is that “the gap between domestic supply and demand is filled by trade”. Read it off the diagram as the horizontal distance between the two curves at the world price.

And spending on imports is that gap times the price: at $4, 60 thousand kilograms of imports cost $240 thousand.

Watch out. Do not read the quantity of imports as the whole of quantity demanded. Domestic growers still supply part of it; imports are only the gap.

1

World price $4. 80 − 20 = 60 imported. Spending on imports $4 × 60 = $240 thousand.

2

A $1 tariff, so a price of $5. 70 − 30 = 40 imported.

3

World price $9. 70 − 30 = 40 exported.

How it connects. It measures the importing case (entry 4) and the exporting case (entry 5), and the imports left after a tariff are the base of the revenue rectangle (entry 12).

Check yourself

6.1 A country opens its hat market at a world price of $7, where domestic buyers want 10 thousand hats and domestic firms supply 4 thousand. How much is spent on imported hats?

Show answer

$7 × (10 − 4) thousand = $42 thousand.

6.2 Why is the gap measured at the world price and not at the autarky price?

Show answer

Because once the market is open, the world price is the price everyone faces. At the autarky price there is no gap at all.

07

Who gains when a country imports

★ ⚑
Definition

When a country opens to imports, consumers gain — they pay less and buy more — and domestic producers lose. Consumers gain more than producers lose.

Not in your textbook — your reference book draws the diagram but never measures it.

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your course says that opening an economy to trade “affects consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total economic surplus”. Your reference book's trade chapter draws the importing diagram and never uses the words consumer surplus or producer surplus at all. OpenStax's Figure 20.3 makes the same comparison if you want a second picture.

Compare the olive market at the world price of $4 with autarky at $7:

No tradeWorld price $4Change
Consumer surplus$125$320+$195
Producer surplus$125$20−$105
Total economic surplus$250$340+$90

All figures in thousands of dollars. Part of the consumers' gain is simply a transfer from domestic producers — the lower price on the 20 thousand kilograms they still sell, and the surplus on the units they no longer grow. The rest is new: the $90 thousand triangle that trade creates.

Watch out. “Trade benefits the country” is true of the total and false of every group. Domestic producers are worse off when a country imports, and an answer that ignores them is incomplete.

1

Olive buyers at $4. Consumer surplus rises from $125 to $320 thousand.

2

Olive growers at $4. Producer surplus falls from $125 to $20 thousand.

3

Sugar in the United States, in OpenStax's Figure 20.3. Consumers gain from the lower price; domestic sugar producers are worse off.

How it connects. It is the surplus story of a world price below autarky (entry 4), its mirror image is the exporting case (entry 8), and the two together are the gain from trade (entry 9).

Check yourself

7.1 A country opens to imports of shoes. What happens to consumer surplus, producer surplus and total economic surplus in its shoe market?

Show answer

Consumer surplus rises, producer surplus falls, and total economic surplus rises, because consumers gain more than producers lose.

7.2 Why do consumers gain more than producers lose?

Show answer

Because the lower price is partly a transfer from producers to consumers, and on top of that consumers buy units that were never worth producing at home. That extra triangle is the net gain.

08

Who gains when a country exports

★ ⚑
Definition

When a country opens to exports, producers gain — they sell more at a higher price — and domestic consumers lose, because they now pay the world price too. Producers gain more than consumers lose.

Not in your textbook — the exporting diagram is there, the surplus is not.

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your reference book draws exports in its Figure 27.5 and never says what they do to anyone's surplus. Another popular textbook gets it wrong, claiming a country exporting computers raises consumer surplus too. It does not: home buyers pay the higher world price.

Compare the olive market at a world price of $9 with autarky at $7:

No tradeWorld price $9Change
Consumer surplus$125$45−$80
Producer surplus$125$245+$120
Total economic surplus$250$290+$40

Thousands of dollars again. It is the importing case turned upside down: the group that loses is now the consumers, and the total still rises — by the triangle between the curves, here $40 thousand.

Watch out. Exporting does not make home consumers better off. The domestic price rises to the world price, so they pay more and buy less — even though the country as a whole gains.

1

Olive growers at $9. Producer surplus rises from $125 to $245 thousand.

2

Olive buyers at $9. Consumer surplus falls from $125 to $45 thousand.

3

Sugar in Brazil, in OpenStax's Figure 20.3. Brazil exports: its producers gain, its consumers are worse off, and total surplus rises.

How it connects. It is the surplus story of a world price above autarky (entry 5), it mirrors the importing case (entry 7) exactly, and it adds its own triangle to the gain from trade (entry 9).

Check yourself

8.1 A country starts exporting wheat. What happens to the price its own bakers pay for wheat?

Show answer

It rises to the world price. Domestic buyers lose consumer surplus, even though wheat growers gain more than they lose.

8.2 In the exporting case, which group would lobby against free trade?

Show answer

Domestic consumers — and firms that use the good as an input. In the importing case it is domestic producers.

09

Total economic surplus rises with trade

★ ⚑
Definition

Opening to trade raises total economic surplus in either direction. The gain is the triangle between domestic demand and domestic supply, out to the world price.

Not in your textbook — it calls the total “social surplus” and never measures it for trade.

⚑ Not in your textbook — learn it from here. Your course's wording is that opening to trade affects “total economic surplus”. That phrase appears nowhere in your reference book — it says social surplus or community surplus — and its trade chapter never measures what trade adds to it.

In both cases one group gains, one group loses, and the winners gain more than the losers lose. The net gain is a triangle with its point at the autarky equilibrium:

  • Importing at $4: ½ × 60 × ($7 − $4) = $90 thousand.
  • Exporting at $9: ½ × 40 × ($9 − $7) = $40 thousand.

The base of the triangle is the quantity traded; the height is the gap between the world price and the autarky price. The further the world price is from autarky, the bigger the gain.

Your reference book calls total surplus social or community surplus. Your exam calls it total economic surplus. They are the same.

Watch out. The gain from trade is a net gain. It does not mean nobody loses — entries 7 and 8 say exactly who does. A complete answer names the losers too.

1

Importing at $4. $340 − $250 = $90 thousand: the triangle.

2

Exporting at $9. $290 − $250 = $40 thousand: a smaller triangle, because $9 is closer to $7 than $4 is.

3

A world price of $7. No gap, no trade, no gain.

How it connects. It is measured from autarky (entry 1), it adds up the importing case (entry 7) and the exporting case (entry 8), and it is the thing a tariff's surplus account (entry 13) gives part of back.

Check yourself

9.1 At a world price of $5, what is the gain from trade in the olive market?

Show answer

Imports are 70 − 30 = 40 and the price gap is $2, so the gain is ½ × 40 × $2 = $40 thousand.

9.2 Why is the gain from trade a triangle and not a rectangle?

Show answer

Because each extra unit traded is worth a little less than the one before. The first imported kilogram saves nearly the whole $3 price gap; the last saves almost nothing.

10

Tariff

★ Exam
Definition

A tax on imported goods. In your course it is a per-unit tax: a fixed amount on each imported unit.

Reference book, page 437.

Your reference book's definition is short: “Tariffs are a specific tax on imported goods and services.” “Specific” means a fixed amount per unit — exactly the per-unit tax of Module 50, applied only to the units that cross the border.

Because the world supply is horizontal, a $1 tariff shifts it up by $1, and the domestic price rises by the full $1 — both on imported olives and on domestic ones, since domestic growers will not sell for less than an imported kilogram now costs.

Watch out. A tariff is paid on imports only. Domestic growers pay nothing — they simply get to charge the higher price.

1

$1 a kilogram on imported olives. The domestic price rises from $4 to $5.

2

A 25% tariff on imported steel. A percentage tariff — the same idea, priced differently.

3

A tax on domestically grown olives. Not a tariff. That is a per-unit tax of the Module 50 kind.

How it connects. It is a per-unit tax on the world supply that the price-taker assumption (entry 3) describes, its effects are listed in the next entry (entry 11), and its alternative is a quota (entry 15).

Check yourself

10.1 Why does a $1 tariff raise the domestic price by exactly $1 in this model?

Show answer

Because the world supply is horizontal at the world price. Every imported unit now costs $1 more, and nothing about the world price changes.

10.2 A tariff is collected only on imports. Why does the price of domestic olives rise too?

Show answer

Because domestic and imported olives are the same good. Domestic growers can charge what an imported kilogram costs, so they do.

11

What a tariff does to prices and quantities

★ Exam
Definition

A tariff raises the domestic price by the tariff. Domestic production rises, domestic consumption falls, and imports fall from both ends.

Reference book, pages 437–438, Figure 28.2.

Add a $1 tariff to the olive market at a world price of $4:

Free trade at $4$1 tariff, price $5
Quantity demanded8070
Domestic quantity supplied2030
Imports6040

Imports shrink from both sides: domestic growers supply 10 thousand more, and buyers want 10 thousand fewer.

Watch out. A tariff does not stop trade unless it is high enough to raise the price to the autarky price. In the olive market that is a $3 tariff; anything less leaves some imports.

1

A $1 tariff. Price $5; Qd 70, Qs 30, imports 40.

2

A $2 tariff. Price $6; Qd 60, Qs 40, imports 20.

3

A $3 tariff. Price $7, the autarky price; imports fall to zero and the market is back where it started.

How it connects. It follows from the tariff (entry 10), its remaining imports are the base of the revenue rectangle (entry 12), and a quota (entry 16) reproduces the same price.

Check yourself

11.1 A $1.50 tariff is placed on olives at a world price of $4. What are Qd, Qs and imports?

Show answer

The price is $5.50: Qd = 65, Qs = 35, imports 30 thousand kilograms.

11.2 What is the smallest tariff that stops all olive imports, and why?

Show answer

$3, because it raises the domestic price to $7, the autarky price, where domestic quantity supplied equals quantity demanded.

12

Tariff revenue

★ Exam
Definition

The government collects the tariff on each unit still imported: tariff × (quantity demanded − domestic quantity supplied) at the new price.

Reference book, page 444.

tariff revenue = tariff × imports after the tariff

With a $1 tariff the olive market still imports 40 thousand kilograms, so the government collects $1 × 40 = $40 thousand. On the diagram it is a rectangle between the world price and the tariff-inclusive price, as wide as the remaining imports.

A $2 tariff collects $2 × 20 = $40 thousand as well — twice the rate, half the imports. Raising a tariff can leave revenue flat or even reduce it.

Watch out. Multiply by the imports after the tariff, never by the free-trade imports. Using 60 here instead of 40 is the commonest mistake on the whole topic.

1

$1 tariff. $1 × 40 = $40 thousand.

2

$2 tariff. $2 × 20 = $40 thousand.

3

$3 tariff. $3 × 0 = $0: a prohibitive tariff raises nothing.

How it connects. Its width is the gap that trade still fills (entry 6), it is drawn as part of the surplus account (entry 13), it follows from the new quantities (entry 11), and it is what a quota (entry 17) lacks.

Check yourself

12.1 A $2 tariff leaves 25 thousand units of imports. What is the revenue?

Show answer

$2 × 25 thousand = $50 thousand.

12.2 Why can a higher tariff raise less revenue?

Show answer

Because revenue is the rate times the imports that remain, and imports fall as the rate rises. Push the tariff high enough and imports, and revenue, fall to zero.

13

The tariff's surplus account

★ Exam
Definition

A tariff lowers consumer surplus; part of the loss goes to domestic producers, part to the government, and two triangles go to nobody.

Reference book, pages 438–440 and 444, Figures 28.2 and 28.5.

The $1 tariff, in full — thousands of dollars:

Free trade at $4$1 tariffChange
Consumer surplus$320$245−$75
Producer surplus$20$45+$25
Tariff revenue$0$40+$40
Total economic surplus$340$330−$10

Read it as Module 50's account: consumers lose $75; producers gain $25 and the government gains $40 of it; the missing $10 is the deadweight loss.

Your reference book labels the same pieces c (producers), e (government) and d + f (lost), out of a consumer loss of c + d + e + f.

Watch out. A tariff gives back part of the gain from trade — $10 of the $90 here. It never makes the country better off than free trade, which is why the tariff that maximises total surplus is zero.

1

Consumers. Lose $75 thousand: the higher price on 70, and the 10 they no longer buy.

2

Domestic producers. Gain $25 thousand: the higher price on 20, and surplus on 10 extra.

3

The government. Collects $40 thousand on the 40 still imported.

How it connects. It gives back part of the gain from trade (entry 9), it includes the revenue (entry 12), and what is left over is the deadweight loss (entry 14).

Check yourself

13.1 A tariff lowers consumer surplus by $500, raises producer surplus by $200 and raises $220 in revenue. What is the deadweight loss?

Show answer

$500 − $200 − $220 = $80.

13.2 Which tariff maximises the sum of consumer surplus, producer surplus and tariff revenue for a small importing country?

Show answer

A tariff of zero. Any positive tariff creates a deadweight loss, so free trade gives the largest total.

14

Deadweight loss of a tariff

★ Exam
Definition

Two triangles: one for domestic production that costs more than the world price, and one for consumption that no longer happens. Your reference book calls it welfare loss.

Reference book, pages 440 and 444.

With a $1 tariff the olive market loses two triangles, each ½ × 10 × $1 = $5 thousand:

  • The production triangle: the extra 10 thousand kilograms domestic growers now supply cost more to grow than they would cost to import.
  • The consumption triangle: buyers who valued olives above the world price no longer buy the last 10 thousand kilograms.

Together, $10 thousand. A $2 tariff gives two triangles of ½ × 20 × $2 = $20 thousand each: $40 thousand — four times the loss for twice the tariff.

Watch out. Two triangles, not one. A tariff diagram has a gap on each side of the revenue rectangle, and students who shade only one undercount by half.

1

$1 tariff. $5 + $5 = $10 thousand.

2

$2 tariff. $20 + $20 = $40 thousand.

3

Your reference book's term. “Welfare loss” — on your exam, write deadweight loss.

How it connects. It is the residual of the surplus account (entry 13), and it is the area you compute last in a calculation from a graph (entry 18).

Check yourself

14.1 A $3 tariff on olives is prohibitive. What is the deadweight loss?

Show answer

½ × 30 × $3 for each triangle, $45 + $45 = $90 thousand — the whole gain from trade, because trade stops.

14.2 Why does doubling a tariff roughly quadruple its deadweight loss?

Show answer

Because both the base and the height of each triangle double: twice the price gap, and twice the lost quantity.

15

Quota

★ Exam
Definition

A legal limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported.

Reference book, page 445.

Your reference book: “Quotas are quantitative limits on the importation of a good into a country.” Where a tariff works through the price of imports, a quota works through their quantity directly.

Your course is specific about what you need: you must understand how quotas affect quantities, prices and surplus, but you will not be asked to graph one. Your reference book draws the quota diagram three times; treat those figures as background.

Watch out. A quota is not a ban unless it is set at zero. It allows a fixed amount in and stops the rest.

1

A limit of 40 thousand kilograms of imported olives. Imports cannot exceed 40, whatever the price.

2

A cap on the number of foreign cars sold each year. A quota in your reference book's sense.

3

A zero quota. A ban: the market returns to autarky.

How it connects. It is the alternative to a tariff (entry 10), and what it does is the next entry (entry 16).

Check yourself

15.1 What is the difference in how a tariff and a quota restrict imports?

Show answer

A tariff raises the price of imports and lets the quantity adjust; a quota fixes the quantity and lets the price adjust.

15.2 Your course says you will not be asked to graph a quota. What must you still be able to do?

Show answer

Explain how it changes the quantity imported and produced, the domestic price, consumer surplus and total economic surplus.

16

What a quota does

★ Exam
Definition

A quota raises the domestic price until imports equal the quota. The effects on price, quantities and deadweight loss match the equivalent tariff — but the government collects nothing.

Reference book, pages 445–446.

Limit olive imports to 40 thousand kilograms. At the world price of $4 buyers want 80 and growers supply 20, so the gap is 60 — more than the quota allows. The price rises until the gap is exactly 40:

(120 − 10P) − (10P − 20) = 40  →  P = $5

That is the same price as the $1 tariff, with the same quantities and the same $10 thousand of deadweight loss. The difference is the $40 thousand rectangle: under a tariff it is government revenue; under a quota it is quota rent, kept by whoever holds the import licences — usually importers or foreign exporters.

Watch out. Your reference book says the government “will receive some revenue” from a quota through selling licences. On your exam, a quota raises no government revenue — that is exactly how exam questions tell a quota from a tariff.

1

A 40-thousand-kilogram quota. Price $5, consumption 70, domestic production 30, quota rent $40 thousand.

2

A 20-thousand-kilogram quota. Price $6, like the $2 tariff, with $40 thousand of quota rent.

3

A quota larger than free-trade imports. Non-binding: the price stays at $4 and nothing changes.

How it connects. It reproduces the tariff's price and quantities (entry 11), it is the quota (entry 15)'s quantity-based twin, and how it differs is the comparison (entry 17).

Check yourself

16.1 Find the domestic price under a quota of 60 thousand kilograms.

Show answer

(120 − 10P) − (10P − 20) = 60 gives P = $4: the quota equals free-trade imports, so it does not bind.

16.2 Under a quota, who receives the rectangle that a tariff would have given the government?

Show answer

Whoever holds the right to import — the licence holders. It is called quota rent.

17

Tariff and quota compared

★ Exam
Definition

Set to restrict imports equally, a tariff and a quota give the same price, quantities, consumer-surplus loss, producer-surplus gain and deadweight loss. Only the tariff raises government revenue.

Calculating from a linear graph

★ Exam
Definition

Every number in this topic is an area or a gap on a straight-line graph: quantities from the curves, rectangles for revenue, triangles for surplus and deadweight loss.

Reference book, pages 440–444.

Your course asks you to calculate changes in market outcomes. On a straight-line graph two methods always agree:

  • Triangles from the intercepts. Consumer surplus at $5 = ½ × 70 × ($12 − $5) = $245 thousand.
  • Areas between the prices. The fall from $4 to $5 is a trapezoid: $1 × (80 + 70) ÷ 2 = $75 thousand — and $320 − $245 = $75.

A warning about your reference book: its worked tariff example uses points that do not lie on straight lines, so its two methods give different answers there (a fall in consumer surplus of £140m one way and £170m the other). Practise on straight-line numbers like the ones in this guide; exam graphs are always straight.

Watch out. Use the intercepts for triangles and the new quantities for rectangles. Most lost marks come from using a free-trade quantity after a tariff.

1

Consumer surplus at $4. ½ × 80 × $8 = $320 thousand.

2

Producer surplus at $4. ½ × 20 × $2 = $20 thousand.

3

The change in producer surplus from $4 to $5. $1 × (20 + 30) ÷ 2 = $25 thousand.

How it connects. It is how the deadweight loss (entry 14) is computed.

Show answer

18.2 Why do the two methods disagree on your reference book's tariff example but agree here?

Show answer

Because the triangle method assumes the curve is a straight line to the intercept. Its points are not on one straight line; these are.

Protectionism and other barriers

○ Background
Definition

Protectionism is any policy that shields domestic producers from foreign competition. Besides tariffs and quotas it includes subsidies to domestic firms and administrative barriers such as standards and regulations.

Reference book, pages 436 and 448–454, and Chapter 29.

Your reference book lists four types of trade protection: tariffs, quotas, subsidies, and administrative barriers. Your course examines only the first two in this topic.

The arguments for protection — protecting jobs, infant industries, national security, retaliation — and against it are your reference book's Chapter 29. They make for good discussion, but you will not be asked to evaluate them.

A subsidy to domestic producers is analysed exactly like Module 50's per-unit subsidy; in trade it lets domestic firms supply more at the world price, with a cost to the government and a deadweight loss.

Watch out. Do not bring subsidies or administrative barriers into an answer about tariffs and quotas unless the question asks for them. They are not part of this topic's scope.

1

A subsidy to domestic olive growers. Domestic output rises, imports fall, the government pays.

2

A safety standard only foreign producers struggle to meet. An administrative barrier.

3

Retaliatory tariffs in a trade war. Context — your reference book discusses them on page 439.

How it connects. It is the wider debate around the tariff and the quota (entry 17).

Check yourself

19.1 Name two forms of trade protection that are not tariffs or quotas.

Show answer

Subsidies to domestic producers, and administrative barriers such as standards or regulations.

19.2 Is protectionism ever in a country's total-surplus interest in this model?

Show answer

No. For a small price-taking country, every barrier creates a deadweight loss. The arguments for protection rest on things the model leaves out, such as jobs or security.

A free-response question

1. (6 points) A small country imports olives. Domestic demand is Qd = 120 − 10P and domestic supply is Qs = 10P − 20, in thousands of kilograms; the world price is $4.

(a) Calculate the quantity imported under free trade. (b) The government imposes a tariff of $1 per kilogram. Identify the new domestic price and the new domestic production. (c) Calculate the tariff revenue. (d) Calculate consumer surplus after the tariff. (e) What tariff would maximise the sum of consumer surplus, producer surplus and tariff revenue? Explain.

Show the mark scheme

(a) 1 point — Qd = 80, Qs = 20, so imports are 60 thousand kilograms.

(b) 2 points — the domestic price is $5; domestic production is 30 thousand kilograms.

(c) 1 point — $1 × (70 − 30) = $40 thousand.

(d) 1 point — ½ × 70 × ($12 − $5) = $245 thousand.

(e) 1 point — a tariff of $0. Any positive tariff creates a deadweight loss, so free trade gives the largest total for a small price-taking country.

Things to get right

  • Start from autarky. Is the world price above it or below it? That one comparison decides everything.
  • Both directions. Importing: consumers gain, producers lose. Exporting: producers gain, consumers lose. The total rises either way.
  • Imports and exports are the gap between domestic demand and supply at the world price.
  • A tariff raises the domestic price by the full tariff, because the country is a price taker.
  • Revenue uses the imports that remain, never the free-trade imports.
  • Two deadweight triangles for a tariff, one on each side of the revenue rectangle.
  • A quota raises no government revenue — that is the difference from a tariff. Do not graph a quota on your exam.
  • Translate your reference book. Welfare loss = deadweight loss. Social surplus = total economic surplus.
