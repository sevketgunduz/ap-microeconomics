The headline. After Module 5 this is a relief: Module 6 is well aligned with the Micro framework. It delivers all three statements of Topic 2.2 (MKT-3.C.1, MKT-3.C.2, MKT-3.D.1, CED p. 48), the equilibrium half of Topic 2.6 (MKT-4.A.1, MKT-4.A.2, p. 53) and the first statement of Topic 2.7 (MKT-4.B.1, p. 54). One Micro requirement is missing from the module — MKT-4.A.3, that the equilibrium price "provides information to economic decision-makers to guide resource allocation"; signal and information both occur 0 times in pp. 59–70. The rest of Topic 2.6 — consumer and producer surplus — is Module 49, not a gap in this one.

The two enduring understandings, verbatim:

MKT-3 (CED p. 48) — "Individuals and firms respond to incentives and face constraints."
MKT-4 (CED p. 53) — "Although equilibria are stable, an economy can move from one equilibrium to another if market conditions change."

Krugman's five objectives for the module (p. 59):

  1. What the supply curve is
  2. The difference between movements along the supply curve and changes in supply
  3. The factors that shift the supply curve
  4. How supply and demand curves determine a market's equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity
  5. In the case of a shortage or surplus, how price moves the market back to equilibrium

All five are on-syllabus. Objectives 1–3 are Topic 2.2; 4 is MKT-4.A.2; 5 is MKT-4.B.1.

Verdict on alignment. Good, and plainly so. Topic 2.2 is covered completely — including MKT-3.C.2's horizontal summation, which Figure 6.5 draws. The equilibrium and disequilibrium statements are covered completely. The one supplied element, prices as signals, is a single idea that the class has already met in substance in Module 1 (pp. 2–3). Do not manufacture more. The module's real teaching difficulties are the ones it shares with the Macro reading — in production against in consumption, and the order of the surplus subtraction — plus four claims in the Macro materials that reverse here (section 5).

The knowledge elements, in the CED's own words

Topic 2.2, learning objective MKT-3.C (CED p. 48): "a. Define (using graphs as appropriate) the law of supply. b. Explain (using graphs as appropriate) the relationship between price and quantity supplied."

MKT-3.C.1 — "A change in own-price causes a change in quantity supplied in the same direction and a movement along a supply curve." (CED p. 48)
MKT-3.C.2 — "The market supply curve (schedule) is derived from the summation of individual supply curves (schedules). The market supply curve is upward-sloping." (CED p. 48)

Learning objective MKT-3.D (CED p. 48): "Explain (using graphs as appropriate) producers' (sellers') responses to changes in incentives and technology."

MKT-3.D.1 — "Changes in the determinants of supply can cause the supply curve to shift." (CED p. 48)

Topic 2.6 (CED p. 53), the two statements this module delivers:

MKT-4.A.1 — "The supply-demand model is a tool for understanding what factors influence prices and quantities and why prices and quantities might differ across markets or change over time."
MKT-4.A.2 — "In a perfectly competitive market, equilibrium is achieved (and markets clear with no shortages or surpluses) when the price of a good or service brings the quantity supplied and quantity demanded into balance, in the sense that buyers wish to purchase the same quantity that sellers wish to provide."

and the one it does not:

MKT-4.A.3 — "Equilibrium price provides information to economic decision-makers to guide resource allocation."

Topic 2.7 (CED p. 54): learning objective MKT-4.B — "a. Define a surplus and shortage. b. Explain (using graphs where appropriate) how changes in underlying conditions and shocks to a competitive market can alter price, quantity, consumer surplus, and producer surplus. c. Calculate (using data from a graph or table as appropriate) changes in price, quantity, consumer surplus, and producer surplus in response to changes in market conditions or market disequilibrium."

MKT-4.B.1 — "Whenever markets experience imbalances—creating disequilibrium prices and quantities, surpluses, and shortages—market forces drive price and quantity toward equilibrium."

Three things in that wording change what you say in class.

The Micro framework names no determinant of supply in the essential knowledge, and never says "input prices". Macro's MKT-2.D.1 reads "such as changes in input prices"; the Micro statement names none, and input prices occurs 0 times in the Micro CED against 5 in the Macro one. What the Micro learning objective does name is technology — "responses to changes in incentives and technology". This is the mirror of Module 5, where the Macro statement named income and the Micro one named nothing. Teach the five determinants equally.

MKT-3.C.1 says "same direction", not "positive relationship". It is the exact grammatical twin of MKT-3.A.4 ("in the opposite direction") from Module 5. Krugman's own definition says "positively related" (p. 60); both are right, and the framework's version is the one to drill because it pairs with demand word for word.

Micro asks students to define a surplus and a shortage, and to calculate changes in disequilibrium — not, in so many words, to "calculate the surplus or shortage". Macro's MKT-2.F(c) says exactly that. In practice the subtraction is still examined (it is the "quantity" in LO MKT-4.B(c)), so the drill stays; only the student-guide wording changes.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

"Substitutes in production" is not "substitutes in consumption"

Unchanged from the Macro reading, and still the module's hardest idea. In consumption (Module 5) a rise in the other good's price increases demand; in production a rise in the other good's price decreases supply, because the producer switches; for complements in production (beef and leather) it increases supply. Put the two-by-two on the board. MCQ 2 is built on it.

Higher wages are an input price — unless the good is labour

CYU 1(c) and 1(d): wages are an input price for fast food (shift left), but the price on the axis in the market for construction labour (movement along). Ask what good is on the horizontal axis.

Surplus and shortage are subtractions, and the order matters

Surplus = Qs − Qd above equilibrium; shortage = Qd − Qs below. From Figures 6.7 and 6.8: at $1.50, 11.2 − 8.1 = 3.1; at $0.75, 11.5 − 9.1 = 2.4 billion pounds.

"Surplus" is not "consumer surplus"

This one is Micro-only and it gets worse before it gets better. In this module surplus means excess supply. In Module 49 — the same CED topic — consumer surplus and producer surplus mean gains from trade, and the equilibrium is where their total is largest. Say now that the two words are unrelated, and that a market with a "surplus" in the Module 6 sense has less total surplus in the Module 49 sense, not more.

The price moves, not the curves

In this module the disturbance is a price away from equilibrium; in Module 7 a curve moves. Both are Topic 2.7 in Micro, so students meet them within days of each other — keep them visibly apart.

Four claims in the Macro materials reverse here

  1. "Input prices are the only determinant the framework names" — false in Micro: no determinant is

named in the EK, input prices occurs 0 times, and the learning objective names technology.

  1. "The framework tests the curve, not the table" and "individual supply is background" — false:

MKT-3.C.2 names the schedule and the individual curves.

  1. "AP never asks whether an equilibrium is desirable" — false: MKT-4.A.5 says market

equilibrium maximises total surplus, in the same topic.

  1. "Consumer and producer surplus appear nowhere in the course" — false: 41 occurrences in the Micro

CED.

The Micro student guide is generated from the Macro one with all four reversed.

Teach as a pair, never singly.

  • Demand / supply — "opposite direction" against "same direction"
  • Movement along / shift of — own-price against everything else, for the second time
  • In production / in consumption — the module's hardest distinction
  • Substitutes / complements in production — same mechanism, opposite sign
  • Surplus / shortage — two subtractions in opposite orders
  • Surplus (excess supply) / consumer and producer surplus — the Micro-only vocabulary clash
  • A price away from equilibrium (here) / a curve that moves (Module 7)
