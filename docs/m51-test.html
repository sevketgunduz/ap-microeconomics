1. A student argues: "A £90 concert ticket must give more utility than a £9 cinema ticket, otherwise nobody would pay the difference."

What is wrong with the argument?

  • (A) Nothing — price is the only measurable evidence of utility, so the inference is sound.
  • (B) Price and utility are different things. Price is what you give up; utility is the satisfaction you get. The concert may well be worth more to this buyer, but that has to be argued from their preferences, not read off the price tag — and for someone who dislikes live music it is worth less.
  • (C) The argument is wrong because utility cannot be measured at all, so no comparison between the two tickets is possible.
  • (D) The argument is wrong because expensive goods generally have lower marginal utility than cheap ones.
  • (E) The argument is right for the concert but not for the cinema, because live events have no close substitutes.

2. Two friends each rate the same meal on a utility scale. Ali records 70 utils; Bea records 45.

What may be concluded?

  • (A) Ali enjoyed the meal more than Bea did.
  • (B) Ali would pay more for the meal than Bea would.
  • (C) Bea has a lower income than Ali.
  • (D) The scales must be wrong, since two people eating the same meal should record the same utility.
  • (E) Nothing about which of them enjoyed it more. Utils are a personal scale used to rank one person's own options, and the model deliberately avoids comparing satisfaction between people.

3. A consumer's total utility from cups of tea is 0, 26, 46, 60, 68, 70.

A student says: "This person's utility function is 68."

What is wrong with that?

  • (A) 68 is the marginal utility of the fourth cup rather than the total.
  • (B) The utility function must be written as an equation, never as a list of numbers.
  • (C) 68 is one value of the utility function — the total utility at four cups. The function is the whole relationship between quantity and utility, which is the entire list of six numbers.
  • (D) The student should have given the average, 45.
  • (E) Nothing is wrong, provided the consumer is consuming four cups.

6. Using the same pizza figures — total utility 0, 30, 54, 72, 84, 90 — a student writes: "The marginal utilities are 30, 24, 18, 12, 6, so they are falling. But the total utility is rising. One of these must be wrong."

Which response is correct?

  • (A) The marginal utilities are wrong; if total utility rises, marginal utility must rise too.
  • (B) The total utilities are wrong; a falling marginal utility requires a falling total.
  • (C) Both are right and there is no conflict. Marginal utility is the slope of total utility, so a total that is still climbing but climbing less steeply gives exactly this pattern. Total utility would only fall once marginal utility went below zero, which has not happened here.
  • (D) Both are right, but only because pizza is an unusual good; for most goods the two move together.
  • (E) The student should compare averages instead, since totals and marginals cannot be compared.

7. Which statement describes the principle of diminishing marginal utility correctly?

  • (A) Total utility falls as more of a good is consumed.
  • (B) Each successive unit consumed adds less to total utility than the unit before it, so the marginal utility curve slopes downward.
  • (C) Marginal utility is always negative after the first unit.
  • (D) Consumers eventually stop enjoying a good entirely, so its total utility returns to zero.
  • (E) The principle holds for every good without exception, which is what makes it a principle.

8. At an all-you-can-eat restaurant a diner's total utility from clams runs 0, 15, 28, 39, 48, 55, 60, 63, 64, 63. The clams cost her nothing once she has paid to get in.

How many clams does a rational diner eat, and why?

  • (A) Nine, because the clams are free and free goods carry no opportunity cost.
  • (B) As many as the restaurant will serve, since her budget constraint does not bind.
  • (C) Four, because that is where marginal utility has fallen to half its starting value.
  • (D) It cannot be determined without knowing the entry price.
  • (E) Eight. The ninth clam has a marginal utility of −1, so eating it would lower her total utility from 64 to 63. A negative marginal utility is a reason to refuse a unit even at a price of zero.

10. A consumer has an income of $48. Good X costs $6 and good Y costs $4.

Can she afford the bundle 5 units of X and 5 units of Y?

  • (A) Yes, and it spends all her income exactly.
  • (B) Yes, with $8 left over.
  • (C) Yes, but only if she does not buy anything else.
  • (D) No. The bundle costs 5 × $6 + 5 × $4 = $30 + $20 = $50, which is $2 more than her income, so it lies outside her consumption possibilities.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without knowing how much utility she gets from each good.

11. A consumer's income is unchanged, but the prices of both goods she buys are halved.

What happens to her consumption possibilities?

  • (A) They are unchanged, because her income has not changed.
  • (B) They shrink, because each dollar now represents a smaller share of the market.
  • (C) They are unchanged in size but shift toward whichever good she prefers.
  • (D) They cannot be determined without knowing her utility function.
  • (E) They expand: every bundle she could afford before is still affordable and many more now are. Halving both prices has exactly the same effect on the affordable set as doubling her income.

14. A consumer is deciding where to spend her next dollar. The next unit of good R would give her 45 utils and costs $9. The next unit of good S would give her 24 utils and costs $4.

Which should she buy, and why?

  • (A) Good S, because 24 ÷ $4 = 6 utils per dollar against 45 ÷ $9 = 5 utils per dollar for R. The dollar buys more satisfaction in S even though a unit of R delivers nearly twice as much.
  • (B) Good R, because a unit of R gives 45 utils and a unit of S only 24.
  • (C) Good R, because it costs more and more expensive goods deliver more utility.
  • (D) Good S, because it is the cheaper good and cheaper goods are always better value.
  • (E) Neither — the two are equally good buys, since 45 − 9 = 36 and 24 − 4 = 20 are both positive.

15. A student's answer reads: "At the optimum, the marginal utility of the last unit of each good must be equal."

What is wrong with it?

  • (A) Nothing; this is the optimal consumption rule as usually stated.
  • (B) It should say total utility rather than marginal utility.
  • (C) It should say the marginal utility of the first unit of each good.
  • (D) It leaves out the price. The condition is that the marginal utility per dollar is equal — MU ÷ P, not MU. A good costing twice as much must deliver twice the marginal utility to be an equally good buy, and the student's version would have a consumer spending very differently.
  • (E) It is wrong because marginal utilities can never be equal across two different goods.

16. A consumer is spending all of her income. The last unit of good A gave her 60 utils and A costs $4; the last unit of good B gave her 90 utils and B costs $6.

What should she do?

  • (A) Buy more of B, because 90 utils exceeds 60 utils.
  • (B) Buy more of A, because it is the cheaper good.
  • (C) Buy less of both and save the difference.
  • (D) Buy more of B and less of A, because B has the higher total utility.
  • (E) Nothing — she is already at her optimum. 60 ÷ $4 = 15 utils per dollar and 90 ÷ $6 = 15 utils per dollar, so the marginal utility per dollar is equal across the two goods and all her income is spent.

17. A consumer has an income of $50. At her current bundle the marginal utility per dollar is exactly 4 for both goods she buys — but the bundle costs only $40.

Is she maximising her total utility?

  • (A) No. Equal marginal utility per dollar is necessary but not sufficient: she must also spend all her income. The unspent $10 could buy units with positive marginal utility, so a better bundle exists.
  • (B) Yes. Equal marginal utility per dollar is the optimal consumption rule, and it holds.
  • (C) Yes, and saving the $10 adds further utility, so she is doing better than the rule requires.
  • (D) It cannot be determined without knowing the two prices.
  • (E) No, because marginal utility per dollar should be as high as possible rather than equal.

18. A student is asked to explain, using total benefits and total costs, whether a three-year university degree is worth taking. He objects that his textbook only ever discusses total utility, and that a degree cannot be analysed at the margin anyway.

Which response is correct?

  • (A) He is right on both counts, so the question cannot be answered with the tools of this course.
  • (B) He is wrong on both counts: total utility is a different concept from total benefit, and a degree can be analysed one credit at a time.
  • (C) For a consumer, total benefit is utility — the framework says so — so his total-utility tables are total-benefit tables under another name. And he is right that a degree has no useful margin, which is exactly why the framework asks for a totals comparison here: some decisions cannot be broken into increments and must be judged by comparing total benefits with total costs.
  • (D) He should convert the decision into marginal terms, because all economic decisions are marginal decisions.
  • (E) Total benefit applies only to firms; for consumers the correct term is total revenue.

19. A market stall owner finds that an extra hour's trading brings in $40 and costs $25; the hour after that brings in $30 and costs $28; the next brings $22 and costs $31.

How many extra hours should she trade, and what does the stopping rule mean?

  • (A) Three hours, because all three bring in revenue.
  • (B) Two hours. She keeps going while marginal benefit is at least marginal cost and stops when it is not, so the third hour is dropped. Stopping where marginal benefit meets marginal cost does not mean the activity is barely worth doing — the earlier hours were well ahead, and stopping there is what makes the gap between total benefit and total cost as large as possible.
  • (C) One hour, because only the first has a large margin, and a small margin means the hour is not worth working.
  • (D) None, because at the optimum marginal benefit equals marginal cost, which means the trading adds nothing.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without knowing her fixed costs for the day.

20. A student has paid $45 for a non-refundable ticket to a weekend course. On the morning it starts he realises he would get more out of a day's revision at home. A friend tells him: "You have to go, you have already paid $45 for it."

Which response is correct?

  • (A) The friend is right: not going wastes the $45 entirely.
  • (B) The friend is right, because the $45 is a cost of staying home but not of going.
  • (C) The friend is wrong, but only because $45 is a small sum; for a larger amount the argument would hold.
  • (D) The friend is wrong. The $45 is a sunk cost — it is gone whether he goes or not, so it is identical on both sides of the comparison and cancels out. The only question is whether the course day is worth more to him than the revision day, and he has already answered it.
  • (E) The friend is wrong, because non-refundable payments are never economic costs.

Question 1 — Utility · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Price is what you give up; utility is what you get. The two are connected — people usually pay more for things they value more — but the connection runs through the buyer's own preferences, and a price tag is not evidence about a particular person's satisfaction.

  • (A) ✗ Price is evidence about what someone was willing to pay, not about what this buyer gets.

Plenty of expensive purchases disappoint.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. It separates the two concepts and gives the counter-case.
  • (C) ✗ Overcorrects. Utility is not measurable in practice, but the model still lets one person

rank their own options, which is all the comparison needs.

  • (D) ✗ Invents a relationship between price and marginal utility that does not exist. Marginal

utility depends on how much of the good you already have.

  • (E) ✗ Substitutes are irrelevant to the point at issue, and the argument fails for the same

reason in both cases.

Question 2 — Util · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Your textbook is explicit on page 511: "we don't need to make comparisons between your feelings and mine." Utils exist so that one person's options can be ranked and the arithmetic can be done. Across people they mean nothing.

  • (A) ✗ The most natural reading, and the one the model rules out. There is no shared unit behind

Ali's 70 and Bea's 45.

  • (B) ✗ Willingness to pay depends on income as well as preference, and in any case cannot be

inferred from an unanchored personal scale.

  • (C) ✗ Nothing in the scenario is about income, and utility scales carry no information about it.
  • (D) ✗ Two people genuinely may enjoy the same meal differently. That is not an error.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. It says both what cannot be concluded and why.

Question 3 — Utility function · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The utility function is the whole mapping from quantity to utility. A single figure is one point on it, in the same way that one price is not a demand curve.

  • (A) ✗ 68 is the total utility at four cups. The marginal utility of the fourth cup is

68 − 60 = 8.

  • (B) ✗ In this course a utility function almost always arrives as a table, and that is how the

exam supplies it.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. One value against the whole relationship.
  • (D) ✗ The average has no role in this model. Totals and marginals are the two quantities that

matter.

  • (E) ✓ Right that 68 is the correct total at four cups — but it answers the wrong question.

The student called 68 the utility function, and a function is not a number.

Question 4 — Total utility · Application (numeric)

Answer: (A)

With two goods, total utility is the sum of the utility from each. Three units of M give 54 and two units of N give 33, so the bundle is worth 54 + 33 = 87 utils.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. 54 + 33 = 87.
  • (B) ✗ 73 is 40 + 33 — two units of M rather than three. Read the row you were asked for.
  • (C) ✗ 99 is 54 + 45, which takes three units of each good. The bundle has two of N.
  • (D) ✗ 21 is 54 − 33. The utility from two goods adds; subtracting one column from the

other has no meaning.

  • (E) ✗ Prices decide which bundles are affordable. They play no part in scoring the utility of

a bundle you have been handed.

Question 5 — Marginal utility · Application (numeric)

Answer: (D)

Marginal utility is the change in total utility from one more unit: the fourth slice takes total utility from 72 to 84, so its marginal utility is 84 − 72 = 12 utils.

  • (A) ✗ 84 is the total utility at four slices, not the change.
  • (B) ✗ 21 is 84 ÷ 4, an average. Marginal utility is a difference, never an average.
  • (C) ✗ 6 is the marginal utility of the fifth slice (90 − 84). Off by one row — the commonest

slip on this item type.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. 84 − 72 = 12.
  • (E) ✗ 18 is the marginal utility of the third slice (72 − 54). Off by one the other way.

Question 6 — Total and marginal utility · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Both columns are right, and together they are the normal case. Marginal utility is the slope of total utility, so falling marginal utility with rising total utility just means a hill that is still climbing and getting less steep.

  • (A) ✗ Reverses the relationship. Rising total utility requires marginal utility to be

positive, not rising.

  • (B) ✗ Total utility only falls once marginal utility goes below zero. Here the smallest marginal

utility is 6.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. It names the relationship and the condition under which total would fall.
  • (D) ✗ There is nothing unusual about pizza. This pattern is what diminishing marginal utility

looks like for almost every good.

  • (E) ✗ Averages have no role here, and the two quantities are related in a perfectly definite way.

Question 7 — Diminishing marginal utility · Analysis

Answer: (B)

CBA-2.A.3 in one line: "Consumers experience diminishing marginal utility in the consumption of goods and services." It is a claim about the marginal figure, and it is what gives the marginal utility curve its downward slope.

  • (A) ✗ Confuses marginal with total. Total utility usually keeps rising for a long while after

marginal utility has begun to fall.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. The claim, and its consequence for the curve.
  • (C) ✗ Marginal utility is usually positive and shrinking. It goes negative only at the point of

satiation, if at all.

  • (D) ✗ Total utility flattens toward a peak; it does not return to zero.
  • (E) ✓ Right that it is called a principle — but wrong that it has no exceptions. Your

textbook's own FYI box gives three: skiing, coffee and Marshall's wallpaper. It holds "in the great majority of cases", which is enough to build on.

Question 8 — Diminishing marginal utility · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The clams are free at the margin, so the price is not what stops her. What stops her is the good itself: the ninth clam has negative marginal utility, and consuming it would make her worse off. This is your textbook's Check Your Understanding 1.

  • (A) ✗ A free good still has to be worth having. A unit with negative marginal utility is not.
  • (B) ✗ Correct that the budget does not bind here, and that is exactly why the answer has to come

from marginal utility instead.

  • (C) ✗ Invents a stopping rule. Marginal utility halving is not a reason to stop; going negative

is.

  • (D) ✗ The entry price is a sunk cost — the same whatever she does next — so it cannot affect

how many clams she eats. See question 20.

  • (E) ✓ Correct. The number, the marginal utility that justifies it, and the reason price is

not involved.

Question 9 — Consumption bundle · Application (numeric)

Answer: (A)

Cost of a bundle = quantity × price, summed over the goods: 4 × $7 + 6 × $3 = $28 + $18 = $46.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. $28 + $18 = $46.
  • (B) ✗ $40 comes from 4 × $7 + 6 × $2 or a similar slip. Check which price goes with which good.
  • (C) ✗ $10 is $7 + $3, the cost of one unit of each. The bundle has ten units in it.
  • (D) ✗ $70 is 10 × $7 — every unit priced as though it were good J.
  • (E) ✗ $28 is the spending on J alone. The bundle includes the K as well.

Question 10 — Budget constraint · Application (numeric)

Answer: (D)

The budget constraint is that spending must be no more than income. Here spending would be $50 against an income of $48, so the bundle fails it by $2 and lies outside her consumption possibilities.

  • (A) ✗ $50 ≠ $48. Two dollars short is still short.
  • (B) ✗ Reverses the sign of the gap: the bundle costs $2 more than she has, not $8 less.
  • (C) ✗ There is nothing else in the problem to buy. The bundle is unaffordable on its own.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. The arithmetic and the conclusion.
  • (E) ✓ Right that utility decides which affordable bundle she picks — but affordability comes first

and needs no utility information at all. Whether a bundle costs more than $48 is settled by prices and quantities alone.

Question 11 — Consumption possibilities · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The affordable set depends on exactly two things — income and prices. Halving both prices doubles what every dollar buys, which is arithmetically the same as doubling income.

  • (A) ✗ Income is only half of what determines the set. Prices are the other half.
  • (B) ✗ Cheaper goods make more bundles affordable, not fewer.
  • (C) ✗ Preferences decide which bundle is chosen from the set. They do not decide what is in it.
  • (D) ✗ The utility function is irrelevant to affordability, for the same reason.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. The expansion, and the equivalence with a rise in income.

Question 12 — Budget line · Application (numeric)

Answer: (B)

Each intercept is all the income spent on one good: $72 ÷ $9 = 8 units of A, and $72 ÷ $4 = 18 units of B. Join the two points with a straight line.

  • (A) ✗ Repeats the two prices. Intercepts are income divided by price, not the price itself.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. 8 and 18.
  • (C) ✗ The two figures swapped. The cheaper good must have the larger intercept — $4 goes into

$72 more times than $9 does.

  • (D) ✗ Uses the income as a quantity, which ignores the prices entirely.
  • (E) ✗ 63 and 68 are $72 − $9 and $72 − $4. Subtraction gives money left over, not a quantity.

Question 14 — Marginal utility per dollar · Application (numeric)

Answer: (A)

Divide each marginal utility by its price. R: 45 ÷ 9 = 5 utils per dollar. S: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 utils per dollar. The next dollar does more work in S.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Both divisions, and the point that a bigger marginal utility can still be the

Question 15 — Marginal utility per dollar · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The rule is about marginal utility per dollar, and the student's version drops the division. CBA-2.A.4 is precise: consumers maximise utility "by equating/comparing the marginal utility of the last dollar spent on each good."

  • (A) ✗ It is a near-miss of the rule, and the missing word is the one that matters.
  • (B) ✗ Total utility is what gets maximised; it is not what gets equalised.
  • (C) ✗ The first unit of a good has the highest marginal utility. Equalising those would be a

rule about where to start, not where to stop.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. It names the omission and says what difference it makes.
  • (E) ✗ Marginal utilities certainly can be equal across goods — it just is not the condition for

an optimum unless the prices are also equal.

Question 16 — The optimal consumption rule · Application (numeric)

Answer: (E)

A: 60 ÷ $4 = 15 utils per dollar. B: 90 ÷ $6 = 15 utils per dollar. Equal, and all income is spent, so the optimal consumption rule holds and no reallocation would improve matters.

  • (A) ✗ Compares raw marginal utilities. B's unit gives more utility and costs proportionally

more.

  • (B) ✗ Price alone is not the test. A is cheaper per unit and delivers proportionally less.
  • (C) ✗ Unspent income buys no utility, and the stem says she is spending it all.
  • (D) ✗ "Higher total utility" is not given and would not be the criterion if it were.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. Both divisions, and both halves of the rule.

Question 17 — The optimal consumption rule · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The rule has two parts and only one of them holds. Equal marginal utility per dollar means she cannot improve by reallocating what she spends; the unspent $10 means she can improve by spending more. Your textbook's own Check Your Understanding 3 makes the same point in reverse, with a bundle whose marginal utilities per dollar are perfectly equal and which costs $36 on a $20 income.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Necessary, not sufficient — and why.
  • (B) ✓ Right that the equality is the heart of the rule — but the rule also requires

all income to be spent, which the framework signals by saying consumers allocate their limited income.

  • (C) ✗ Saving yields no utility in this model, so the $10 is simply wasted.
  • (D) ✗ The prices are not needed. Whatever they are, $10 buys something with positive marginal

utility.

  • (E) ✗ Marginal utility per dollar falls as you buy more of a good, so "as high as possible" would

mean buying almost nothing.

Question 18 — Total benefit and total cost · Analysis

Answer: (C)

He has noticed something real on both counts, and the framework answers both. CBA-1.A.2: "Total benefits form the metric 'utility' for consumers." CBA-1.B.2: some decisions "cannot be broken down into increments in this way and must be evaluated by looking at total benefits and total costs."

  • (A) ✗ Both objections have answers, and the framework supplies them.
  • (B) ✗ Wrong on the first half — for a consumer the two really are the same thing — and the

credit-by-credit reading of a degree misses what the objection was about.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. It resolves the vocabulary and endorses the totals approach.
  • (D) ✗ CBA-1.B.2 says the opposite: some decisions cannot be taken at the margin.
  • (E) ✗ Inverts CBA-1.A.2. Total revenue is the firm's metric; utility is the consumer's.

Question 19 — Marginal benefit and marginal cost · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Hour by hour: $40 against $25 — work it. $30 against $28 — work it. $22 against $31 — stop. The optimum is the last hour at which marginal benefit is at least marginal cost, which is two hours.

  • (A) ✗ Revenue is not the test; revenue against cost is. The third hour loses $9.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. The count, the rule, and the point that a thin margin on the last unit does

not mean a thin gain overall.

  • (C) ✗ A small margin on the second hour is still a gain — $2. You stop when the margin turns

negative, not when it gets small.

  • (D) ✗ The standard misreading of MB = MC. It describes the last unit, not the whole activity.

Over the two hours she is $17 ahead.

  • (E) ✗ Fixed costs for the day are the same whether she trades the extra hours or not, so they

cannot change the answer — question 20 again.

Question 20 — Sunk cost · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

CBA-2.B.2: "The optimal quantity at any point in time does not depend on fixed costs (sunk costs) or fixed benefits that have already been determined by past choices." The $45 appears on both sides of the comparison and cancels.

  • (A) ✗ The $45 is spent either way. Attending does not recover it; it only adds a day he would

rather spend otherwise.

  • (B) ✗ Reverses the logic and invents an asymmetry. The payment is identical whichever he chooses.
  • (C) ✗ Size is irrelevant. A sunk cost of $45,000 is just as irrelevant to the next decision as

$45.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. It names the concept, gives the reason it cancels, and identifies the

comparison that does matter.

  • (E) ✗ Overcorrects. The $45 was a real cost when he paid it; what it is not is a cost of the

choice he faces now.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 16 concept entries appear. Weighting follows the learning objectives: marginal utility per dollar and the optimal consumption rule take four items between them, because they are what CBA-2.A(c) asks students to calculate, and the totals approach takes its own item (13) because CBA-1.B asks for that separately.

Bloom levels. Application 8 · Analysis 7 · Synthesis 5, a ratio of 1 : 0.9 : 0.6 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. This is the largest deviation of any paper in the course and it is deliberate: Topics 1.5 and 1.6 carry three calculate verbs and two quantitative suggested skills. Compare Module 1, where opportunity cost was the only calculable concept and application was capped at three items.

The three supplied entries are tested last and tested properly. Questions 18, 19 and 20 cover total benefit and total cost, marginal benefit and marginal cost, and sunk costs — all required by the framework in Topics 1.5 and 1.6, and all absent from Module 51. If your class has worked only from the textbook they will not recognise the vocabulary, which is exactly the diagnosis this paper should deliver.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: comparing raw marginal utilities instead of marginal utilities per dollar (14B, 15A, 16A); confusing total with marginal (3A, 5A, 6A, 7A); reading the wrong row of a utility column (4B, 4C, 5C, 5E); treating an intercept as a price (12A) or a subtraction as a quantity (12E); and letting a sunk cost into the comparison (8D, 19E, 20A).

Four distractors reach a right-looking conclusion by wrong reasoning, flagged in the solutions: 3E, 7E, 10E, 14D and 17B. The most useful of them is 14D — it picks the right good for a rule that would fail on slightly different numbers, and a student who chooses it needs different feedback from one who picked 14B.

Answer distribution. A 4 · B 4 · C 4 · D 4 · E 4 — exactly even, with no letter repeating twice in a row.
