There is no background section. All 13 concepts in this module are examinable in AP Microeconomics, and Module 4 is the best-aligned module in Section 1. Two of the thirteen — terms of trade and consumption possibilities beyond the PPC — are required by the framework and never named in the textbook, so questions 14–18 test material your students can only have got from the teaching notes or the student guide.
Question 20 is the boundary question. It tests what this module does not let you answer. Topic 2.9 (world price, tariffs, surplus analysis) uses supply and demand, not a PPC, and has no coverage anywhere in Krugman.

That is far more application-weighted than the 1 : 3 : 1.75 the course standard sets, and the reason is in the learning objectives rather than in the test design. MKT-2.A(b) asks students to determine absolute and comparative advantage "using data from PPCs or tables," and MKT-2.B(b) asks them to calculate mutually beneficial terms of trade. Two of the topic's four verbs are computational. A paper on this topic that was only one-fifth application would not be testing the topic.

1. Two neighbouring farms agree that for the next month the first will harvest both farms' olives and the second will press both farms' oil, splitting the output between them. No money changes hands and no contract is signed.

Is this trade in the economic sense?

  • (A) No, because trade requires a price, and no price was agreed.
  • (B) No, because trade requires money to change hands.
  • (C) Yes. Each farm provides a good or service to the other and receives one in return, which is what trade is; money makes exchange convenient but is not part of the definition.
  • (D) Yes, but only if both farms end up better off — otherwise it is not trade.
  • (E) No, because the two farms are not specialising in different goods.

2. A bakery owner makes her own bread, does her own accounts and repairs her own delivery van. An accountant points out that she is doing all three jobs competently and wasting nothing: she is working flat out and every hour is productive.

Which statement is correct?

  • (A) She cannot be made better off, because she is already fully employed and wasting nothing.
  • (B) She is producing inefficiently, which is why trade would help her.
  • (C) She must be producing inside her production possibilities curve.
  • (D) She may be perfectly efficient in production and still worse off than she needs to be. Self-sufficiency is a baseline, not a failure of effort — what she is missing is a lower-opportunity-cost supplier of accounts and repairs.
  • (E) The accountant is right that nothing can be improved, since opportunity cost applies only when resources are idle.

3. Without trade, two castaways together catch 34 fish and gather 17 coconuts in a week. After each specialises and they trade, the pair has 40 fish and 20 coconuts. Nobody worked longer hours, no new resources appeared, and neither castaway learned a new skill.

Where did the extra 6 fish and 3 coconuts come from?

  • (A) From the trade itself: exchanging goods creates value even when production does not change.
  • (B) From moving each task to the producer who gave up less to do it. The poorer fisherman stopped fishing, and the fish he was catching at a cost of 2 coconuts each are now caught at a cost of ¾ of a coconut each.
  • (C) From the castaways working closer to the corners of their curves, which is always more productive than the middle.
  • (D) From specialisation raising each castaway's skill, as in Adam Smith's pin factory.
  • (E) They cannot have come from anywhere: total output cannot rise without more resources, so the figures must be wrong.

4. A teacher explains gains from trade using Adam Smith's pin factory, where ten workers each performing one of eighteen operations produce vastly more pins than ten workers each making whole pins. A student concludes that comparative advantage works because specialists get better at their task through repetition.

What is wrong with the conclusion?

  • (A) Nothing — practice is exactly the mechanism comparative advantage describes.
  • (B) The pin factory is about capital rather than labour, so it is not about specialisation at all.
  • (C) Repetition makes workers worse rather than better, which is why the pin factory is a poor example.
  • (D) The conclusion is right about pins but wrong about pin factories, since factories also gain from machinery.
  • (E) They are two different mechanisms. The pin factory gains from splitting a task and from practice; comparative advantage gains from producers having different opportunity costs, and it works even when nobody gets better at anything. Only the second is what this topic assesses.

5. Using all its resources, a country can produce at most 600 tonnes of rice or at most 150 tractors.

What is the opportunity cost of one tractor, and of one tonne of rice?

  • (A) One tractor costs 4 tonnes of rice; one tonne of rice costs ¼ of a tractor.
  • (B) One tractor costs ¼ of a tonne of rice; one tonne of rice costs 4 tractors.
  • (C) One tractor costs 450 tonnes of rice; one tonne of rice costs 450 tractors.
  • (D) One tractor costs 150 tonnes of rice; one tonne of rice costs 600 tractors.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without knowing how many resources the country has.

6. Asked for the opportunity cost of a bushel of wheat for a country that can produce 150 bushels of wheat or 100 units of textiles, a student writes: "0.67."

The number is arithmetically right. Why does the answer still lose the mark?

  • (A) Because opportunity cost must always be given as a fraction rather than a decimal.
  • (B) Because 0.67 is a rounding of ⅔ and rounding is not permitted in economics.
  • (C) Because the country's opportunity cost cannot be found from intercepts alone.
  • (D) Because it says neither what is being costed nor in what. This country's two ratios are ⅔ of a textile unit per bushel and 1½ bushels per textile unit, and a bare number could be either. Opportunity cost is a rate and needs both goods named.
  • (E) Because the student should have given both ratios added together.

7. Two orchards use identical amounts of land, labour and equipment.

Crates of apples1,200900

Which orchard has an absolute advantage in each crop?

  • (A) Northfield in both crops.
  • (B) Northfield in apples; Southgate in pears.
  • (C) Southgate in apples; Northfield in pears.
  • (D) Southgate in both crops.
  • (E) Neither, because the two orchards use the same quantity of resources.

8. A furniture workshop, Alva, can produce at most 1,000 chairs or at most 500 tables. A much smaller workshop, Brix, can produce at most 200 chairs or at most 200 tables. Alva can out-produce Brix in both goods.

If the two specialise, what should each make, and why?

  • (A) Alva should make both, since it produces more of each and Brix has nothing to offer.
  • (B) Brix should make chairs and Alva tables, because the smaller workshop should take the simpler product.
  • (C) Alva should make chairs and Brix tables. A chair costs Alva ½ a table against 1 table for Brix, and a table costs Brix 1 chair against 2 chairs for Alva — so each has one comparative advantage despite Alva's absolute advantage in both.
  • (D) They should not specialise at all, because Alva's absolute advantage in both goods means there are no gains from trade.
  • (E) Alva should make tables and Brix chairs, because Alva's advantage is larger in tables (500 against 200) than in chairs (1,000 against 200).

9. Using the two orchards from question 7 — Northfield 1,200 crates of apples or 400 of pears; Southgate 900 apples or 600 pears — which orchard has a comparative advantage in each crop?

  • (A) Northfield in apples; Southgate in pears.
  • (B) Northfield in both crops.
  • (C) Southgate in apples; Northfield in pears.
  • (D) Southgate in both crops.
  • (E) Northfield in apples; neither in pears.

10. Two regions produce olives and wine, with olives on the horizontal axis. Both PPCs are straight lines. Region K's curve is noticeably flatter than Region L's.

What does that tell you, and why?

  • (A) Region K can produce more of both goods, because a flatter curve lies further out.
  • (B) Region L has the comparative advantage in olives, because a steeper curve means it is better at the horizontal good.
  • (C) Nothing can be concluded without the intercept values.
  • (D) Region K is producing inefficiently, because an efficient PPC is always steep.
  • (E) Region K has the comparative advantage in olives. The slope of a straight-line PPC is the opportunity cost of the horizontal good, so a flatter curve means each extra tonne of olives costs less wine.

11. A student writes: "Country X is much more efficient than Country Y, so Country X has a comparative advantage in both goods and Country Y has none."

Which response is correct?

  • (A) The student is right, provided Country X really is more efficient in both industries.
  • (B) The student is right about comparative advantage but wrong to call it efficiency.
  • (C) The student is wrong because comparative advantage depends on population rather than efficiency.
  • (D) The student is wrong, and it is arithmetic rather than fairness. The two opportunity costs of a producer are reciprocals of each other, so if one is lower than the rival's the other must be higher. No producer can have a comparative advantage in both goods unless the costs are equal, in which case nobody has one.
  • (E) The student is wrong because the more efficient country always has the comparative disadvantage.

12. A furniture maker and an apprentice work the same hours. The table gives the hours each needs per item.

Hours needed per itemMakerApprentice
One chair69
One table1012

Who has the absolute advantage in each item, and who has the comparative advantage in tables?

  • (A) The apprentice has the absolute advantage in both, because the numbers are larger; the maker has the comparative advantage in tables.
  • (B) The maker has the absolute advantage in both; the maker also has the comparative advantage in tables, at 10 hours against 12.
  • (C) The maker has the absolute advantage in both, because fewer hours per item is better. But a table costs the apprentice 12 ÷ 9 = 1⅓ chairs against 10 ÷ 6 = 1⅔ chairs for the maker, so the apprentice has the comparative advantage in tables.
  • (D) Neither has an absolute advantage, since they work the same hours.
  • (E) The maker has the absolute advantage in chairs only, and the apprentice in tables only.

13. Two countries report weekly output per worker:

Weekly output per workerCountry PCountry Q
Tonnes of wheat2012

The two countries produce cloth equally fast. Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Neither country has a comparative advantage in cloth, because their cloth output is identical.
  • (B) Country P has the comparative advantage in both goods, since it matches Q in cloth and beats it in wheat.
  • (C) The comparative advantage in cloth cannot be determined without knowing each country's workforce.
  • (D) Country P has the comparative advantage in cloth, because it is at least as productive in cloth and more productive overall.
  • (E) Country Q has the comparative advantage in cloth. A unit of cloth costs Q only 12 ÷ 80 = 0.15 of a tonne of wheat, against 20 ÷ 80 = 0.25 for P — and the reason lies entirely in the wheat row, not the cloth row.

14. Northfield's opportunity cost of a crate of apples is ⅓ of a crate of pears. Southgate's is ⅔ of a crate of pears.

What is the range of mutually beneficial terms of trade for apples, measured in pears?

  • (A) Any price at or below ⅓ of a crate of pears per crate of apples.
  • (B) Strictly between ⅓ and ⅔ of a crate of pears per crate of apples.
  • (C) Exactly ½ a crate of pears per crate of apples, the midpoint of the two costs.
  • (D) Any price at or above ⅔ of a crate of pears per crate of apples.
  • (E) Any positive price, since both orchards gain from trade whatever the rate.

15. Using the same two orchards, Southgate proposes buying apples from Northfield at ¾ of a crate of pears per crate of apples.

What happens, and why?

  • (A) Both accept, since ¾ is above Northfield's cost of ⅓ and Northfield is the seller.
  • (B) Both accept, because ¾ lies between the two opportunity costs.
  • (C) Northfield refuses, because ¾ of a crate of pears is less than its own cost of producing apples.
  • (D) Southgate refuses its own proposal once it checks the arithmetic. Growing apples itself costs Southgate only ⅔ of a crate of pears per crate, so paying ¾ is worse than self-sufficiency. Northfield would happily accept.
  • (E) The deal is impossible to evaluate without knowing how many crates are traded.

16. The same two orchards agree to trade apples at exactly ⅔ of a crate of pears per crate of apples — precisely Southgate's own opportunity cost.

What is the result?

  • (A) Southgate gains nothing at all: paying ⅔ is exactly what growing the apples itself would have cost, so it is indifferent between trading and not. The entire gain from trade goes to Northfield, which is why the framework describes a range rather than a single price.
  • (B) Both gain equally, because the price sits at one of the two opportunity costs.
  • (C) Northfield gains nothing, because the price equals an opportunity cost.
  • (D) The trade is impossible, because a price equal to an opportunity cost is undefined.
  • (E) Both gain, but Southgate gains more because it is the buyer.

17. After specialising and trading, a castaway consumes 30 fish and 10 coconuts. His own production possibilities curve runs between 40 fish and 30 coconuts, so at 30 fish it allows only 7.5 coconuts.

What does this show, and what is he actually producing?

  • (A) That he must be producing 30 fish and 10 coconuts, which proves his curve was drawn wrongly.
  • (B) That his curve has shifted outward, since he is now consuming more than it allowed.
  • (C) That his consumption point lies outside his own PPC — 10 exceeds 7.5, so he could not have produced that bundle. He is producing 40 fish and 0 coconuts, a point on his curve, and trading 10 fish for 10 coconuts.
  • (D) That he is producing inefficiently, since consumption and production differ.
  • (E) That the 7.5 figure is irrelevant once trade is allowed, because trade removes the constraint on production.

18. In Topic 1.3 a student learned that points outside a production possibilities curve are unattainable. In Topic 1.4 the same student is told that trade lets a country consume beyond its PPC.

How should the two statements be reconciled?

  • (A) The Topic 1.3 rule was a simplification and is abandoned once trade is introduced.
  • (B) They are about different things. The curve still limits what a producer can produce — that rule is unchanged. What trade changes is what a producer can consume, by exchanging some of its own output for output it did not make.
  • (C) Trade shifts the curve outward, so the bundle is no longer outside it.
  • (D) The Topic 1.4 claim applies only to countries; for individuals the Topic 1.3 rule still holds.
  • (E) Both statements are about consumption, and the second is the more accurate of the two.

19. Almost every industry in a low-income country is less productive than the corresponding industry in a high-income country. The low-income country nevertheless exports a large share of the world's clothing.

Which explanation is correct?

  • (A) The productivity gap is much smaller in clothing than in sophisticated goods such as aircraft, so clothing is what the low-income country does comparatively well. An absolute disadvantage in everything is entirely consistent with a comparative advantage in something.
  • (B) The low-income country must in fact have an absolute advantage in clothing, or it could not export it.
  • (C) The high-income country chooses not to make clothing for non-economic reasons; comparative advantage plays no part.
  • (D) The low-income country exports clothing at a loss, which is the only way a less productive country can compete.
  • (E) Trade of this kind produces no gains for the high-income country, which is why such trade is usually restricted.

20. A question gives a domestic supply-and-demand diagram for wheat, states a world price below the domestic equilibrium price, and asks for the quantity imported and the change in consumer surplus.

A student tries to answer it using Module 4's comparative-advantage model. What should you tell them?

  • (A) The model gives both answers, since comparative advantage determines quantities traded.
  • (B) The model gives the quantity but not the surplus change, which requires elasticity.
  • (C) The question is unanswerable as written, because a world price cannot be below a domestic equilibrium price.
  • (D) Module 4 answers it once you convert the supply-and-demand diagram into a PPC.
  • (E) Module 4 gives only the direction of trade — who exports and who imports, from whose opportunity cost is lower. Quantities traded and the surplus changes belong to Topic 2.9, which uses a supply-and-demand diagram with a world price, and which Krugman does not cover anywhere.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Trade · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Trade is an exchange of goods or services in both directions. Tom and Hank trade fish for coconuts and never use money; the two farms here trade harvesting for pressing. Money removes the need to find someone who wants exactly what you have, which makes trade far easier — but it is not what makes an exchange a trade.

  • (A) ✗ A price is the rate at which the goods exchange, and there is one here even if nobody

wrote it down: half the output each. A stated monetary price is not required.

  • (B) ✗ States the confusion the question is built on. Barter is trade.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. Goods or services in both directions is the whole definition.
  • (D) ✗ Gains from trade are the usual result of voluntary trade, not part of what makes an

exchange one. A trade that turned out badly was still a trade.

  • (E) ✗ They are specialising in different tasks — one harvests, the other presses. Even if they

were not, an exchange would still be an exchange.

Question 2 — Self-sufficiency · Analysis

Answer: (D)

This is the surprise the module is built on. Being efficient in production means nothing is wasted; it does not mean you are doing the best you could. The baker's hours produce accounts and van repairs at a high cost in forgone baking, and somebody else produces them at a lower cost.

  • (A) ✗ Full employment of your own resources is exactly the self-sufficient position the module

improves on. Tom and Hank are both on their own curves before they trade.

  • (B) ✗ The stem says she wastes nothing, so she is efficient in production. Trade helps

efficient producers, which is the point.

  • (C) ✗ Inside the curve would mean waste, and the stem rules that out. Her problem is which point

on the curve she is at, and what she could consume instead.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. Efficient in production, and still beatable.
  • (E) ✗ Opportunity cost applies to every use of a scarce resource, idle or not. Her hour of

bookkeeping costs an hour of baking whether or not she is busy.

Question 3 — Gains from trade · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Total output rose because each good is now produced by whoever gives up least to produce it. The 6 extra fish are the 6 Hank used to catch: they cost him 2 coconuts each, and Tom catches them for ¾ of a coconut each. Nothing was created from nothing — a high-cost producer stopped.

  • (A) ✗ Exchange redistributes; it does not raise output. The extra output came from the

specialisation, and trade is what makes specialising worth doing.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. It names the mechanism and prices it.
  • (C) ✗ Every point on a straight-line PPC is equally efficient. Corners are where complete

specialisation happens to put you, not a more productive place to be.

  • (D) ✓ Reaches a true-sounding conclusion by the wrong mechanism. Practice is the pin-factory

gain. The stem explicitly rules it out — "neither castaway learned a new skill" — and the gains still appear. That is the whole content of comparative advantage.

  • (E) ✗ Total output rose with the same resources, which is exactly what reallocating production

to the lower-cost producer does. The figures are your textbook's own Table 4.2.

Question 4 — Specialization · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Two mechanisms, and only one is on the syllabus. Smith's workers gain from splitting an eighteen-step task and from not switching between steps. Tom and Hank gain purely because a fish costs them different amounts of coconuts, with no learning at all.

  • (A) ✗ Practice is not the mechanism. Comparative advantage works in a one-week model where

nobody improves.

  • (B) ✗ The pin factory is entirely about the division of labour among ten workers. Machinery is

not what the passage describes.

  • (C) ✗ Repetition raising output is exactly Smith's claim, and it is correct. The problem is that

it is a different claim.

  • (D) ✗ Invents a distinction between pins and pin factories, and still does not identify the

actual error.

  • (E) ✓ Correct. Different mechanisms; only differing opportunity costs are assessed here.

Question 5 — Opportunity cost as a ratio · Application (numeric)

Answer: (A)

Give up all 600 tonnes of rice to build 150 tractors, so one tractor costs 600 ÷ 150 = 4 tonnes of rice. Inverting, one tonne of rice costs 150 ÷ 600 = ¼ of a tractor. The two are reciprocals.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. 4 and ¼.
  • (B) ✗ Both ratios inverted. This is the commonest error in the topic: dividing the wrong way

round.

  • (C) ✗ 450 is 600 − 150, a subtraction. Opportunity cost is a ratio, never a difference.
  • (D) ✗ Uses each intercept as though it were the cost of one unit of the other good. Those are

the maximum outputs, not rates.

  • (E) ✗ The two intercepts are all that a straight-line PPC needs. The absolute quantity of

resources never enters an opportunity-cost calculation.

Question 6 — Opportunity cost as a ratio · Analysis

Answer: (D)

An opportunity cost is a rate between two goods, so an answer has to say which good is being costed and in what. This country's two true ratios are ⅔ and 1½, and "0.67" identifies neither.

  • (A) ✗ Decimals are perfectly acceptable. ⅔ and 0.67 would both earn the mark if labelled.
  • (B) ✗ There is no such rule, and sensible rounding is normal.
  • (C) ✗ Intercepts are exactly what a straight-line PPC gives you, and the arithmetic in the stem

is right.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. The fault is the missing units, not the number.
  • (E) ✗ Adding the two ratios produces a figure with no meaning. They are alternative expressions

of one trade-off, not components of it.

Question 7 — Absolute advantage · Application (numeric)

Answer: (B)

Absolute advantage compares the same good across the two producers. Apples: 1,200 against 900, so Northfield. Pears: 600 against 400, so Southgate. Nothing else is involved.

  • (A) ✗ Northfield grows fewer pears — 400 against 600.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. One each, read straight off the table.
  • (C) ✗ Exactly reversed. Take the larger of the two numbers in each row.
  • (D) ✗ Southgate grows fewer apples — 900 against 1,200.
  • (E) ✗ Equal resources is what makes the comparison meaningful. It is the premise, not an

obstacle.

Question 8 — Absolute and comparative advantage · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Alva's absolute advantage in both goods is a distraction. Chairs: Alva gives up 500 ÷ 1,000 = ½ a table per chair; Brix gives up 200 ÷ 200 = 1 table. Tables: Alva gives up 1,000 ÷ 500 = 2 chairs; Brix gives up 1 chair. So Alva is the low-cost chair producer and Brix the low-cost table producer.

  • (A) ✗ The error the whole module exists to correct. Producing more of everything does not mean

producing everything.

  • (B) ✗ Right that they should split, wrong which way, and the reason given — "simpler product" —

is not an economic argument.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. Both ratios, both directions, and the conclusion.
  • (D) ✗ States the error explicitly. Tom has an absolute advantage in both goods in your textbook

and gains from trade anyway.

  • (E) ✓ Reaches a specialisation split by faulty reasoning, and gets it backwards as a result.

The size of an absolute advantage is not what determines comparative advantage; the ratio within each producer's own column is. Comparing 500-against-200 with 1,000-against-200 is comparing across producers, which is exactly what the topic warns against.

Question 10 — Comparative advantage · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The slope of a straight-line PPC is the opportunity cost of the horizontal good, measured in the vertical one. Flatter means a smaller sacrifice per unit, which is exactly a lower opportunity cost.

  • (A) ✗ Flatness is a shape, not a position. A flatter curve can lie entirely inside a steeper

one.

  • (B) ✗ Exactly reversed, and the reason given restates absolute advantage. "Better at" is not

the test.

  • (C) ✗ The slope alone settles it. Intercept values would let you compute the number, but the

comparison is already available.

  • (D) ✗ Efficiency is about being on the curve. Shape says nothing about it.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. It gives the conclusion and the Topic 1.3 reason for it.

Question 11 — Comparative disadvantage · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Your textbook puts it in one sentence: "as long as people have different opportunity costs, everyone has a comparative advantage in something, and everyone has a comparative disadvantage in something." It follows from the arithmetic of reciprocals, not from a principle of fairness.

  • (A) ✗ Efficiency in both industries is an absolute advantage in both, which is compatible with a

comparative advantage in only one.

  • (B) ✗ The student's comparative-advantage claim is the part that is wrong. The vocabulary is a

side issue.

  • (C) ✗ Population does not enter. The ratios are computed within each producer's own output.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. The reciprocal argument, and the equal-costs exception.
  • (E) ✗ Overcorrects into a new error. Being more efficient does not hand you the comparative

disadvantage either; productivity and comparative advantage are simply different questions.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. Fewer hours for absolute advantage; the within-column ratio for comparative.
  • (D) ✗ Equal hours worked is the premise that makes the comparison fair, not a reason it cannot

  • (E) ✗ The maker needs fewer hours for both items, so the absolute advantage is not split.

Question 13 — Output data and comparative advantage · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The cleanest possible demonstration that comparative advantage is not productivity. The two countries make cloth at identical speed, and one of them still has the comparative advantage in cloth — because a unit of cloth costs Q less wheat than it costs P.

Country PCountry QLower
One tonne of wheat costs80 ÷ 20 = 4 units of cloth80 ÷ 12 = 6⅔ unitsP
One unit of cloth costs20 ÷ 80 = 0.25 tonne12 ÷ 80 = 0.15 tonneQ
  • (A) ✓ Notices the right fact — identical cloth output — and draws the wrong conclusion from

it. Comparative advantage in cloth is not read off the cloth row at all; it is read off what has to be given up, which is wheat.

  • (B) ✗ Impossible: reciprocal ratios cannot both be lower.
  • (C) ✗ Output per worker already controls for workforce size. That is what the units mean.
  • (D) ✗ "More productive overall" is absolute advantage. It does not transfer.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. The numbers, and the explanation of where they come from.

Question 14 — Terms of trade · Application (numeric)

Answer: (B)

The seller will not accept less than its own opportunity cost; the buyer will not pay more than its own. Northfield sells apples and will not take less than ⅓; Southgate buys and will not pay more than ⅔. So the price must sit strictly between ⅓ and ⅔ of a crate of pears per crate of apples.

  • (A) ✗ At or below ⅓ is where the seller refuses. Northfield gains nothing at ⅓ and loses below

it.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. Strictly between the two opportunity costs.
  • (C) ✗ The midpoint is one workable price among infinitely many. The framework asks for the

range, and any interior price makes both better off.

  • (D) ✗ At or above ⅔ is where the buyer refuses. Southgate can grow its own apples for ⅔.
  • (E) ✗ Your textbook rules out a specific deal for exactly this reason: 15 coconuts for 5 fish is

3 coconuts per fish, above Hank's own cost of 2, so he refuses.

Question 15 — Terms of trade · Application (numeric)

Answer: (D)

The range is ⅓ to ⅔, and ¾ is above the top of it. Southgate's own cost of growing a crate of apples is ⅔ of a crate of pears, so paying ¾ is worse than doing it itself. The proposal is against the proposer's interest.

  • (A) ✗ Correct that Northfield accepts, but "both accept" needs the buyer too — and the buyer is

the one this price hurts.

  • (B) ✗ ¾ is greater than ⅔, so it does not lie between the two costs. Check the arithmetic before

the conclusion.

  • (C) ✗ Reverses who is squeezed. ¾ is comfortably above Northfield's ⅓, so Northfield is

delighted.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. Buyer refuses; seller would accept.
  • (E) ✗ Quantity does not matter. The terms of trade are a rate, and whether the rate beats each

party's own cost is independent of how many crates change hands.

Question 16 — Terms of trade · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The boundaries of the range are exactly the prices at which one party is indifferent. At ⅔ Southgate pays precisely what self-sufficiency would have cost, so it captures none of the gain; every bit of it goes to Northfield, which produces at ⅓ and sells at ⅔.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. It identifies who gains nothing, who gains everything, and why the framework

specifies a range.

  • (B) ✗ Equal division would need a price the two parties are equally advantaged by, not one of the

endpoints.

  • (C) ✗ Reversed. ⅔ is Southgate's cost; Northfield's is ⅓, and selling at ⅔ is its best

possible outcome.

  • (D) ✗ Nothing is undefined. The trade is perfectly possible; it is just not beneficial to one

party.

  • (E) ✗ Being the buyer confers no advantage, and at this price Southgate gains nothing at all.

Question 17 — Consumption beyond the PPC · Analysis

Answer: (C)

10 exceeds 7.5, so the consumption bundle is outside his own curve — a bundle he could not have produced. Production is still on the curve: he produces at the corner, 40 fish and 0 coconuts, and trades 10 fish away for 10 coconuts.

  • (A) ✗ He is not producing that bundle. Production and consumption differ once there is trade,

and the curve is drawn correctly.

  • (B) ✗ A shift needs more resources or better technology. He has neither; he has a trading

partner.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. It separates the consumption point from the production point and names both.
  • (D) ✗ Production and consumption differing is the normal case with trade, not evidence of

waste. He produces at a point on the frontier.

  • (E) ✗ Trade does not relax the constraint on production at all — only on consumption. The 7.5 is

precisely what makes the comparison interesting.

Question 18 — Consumption beyond the PPC · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Both statements are true because they are about different quantities. MKT-1.C limits production; MKT-2.B.1 says specialisation "results in exchange opportunities that lead to consumption possibilities beyond the PPC." Read the noun carefully and the contradiction disappears.

  • (A) ✗ The Topic 1.3 rule is not abandoned. It is exactly as true after trade as before, for

production.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. Production against consumption.
  • (C) ✗ Trade does not shift the curve. Nothing about the producer's resources or technology

changed.

  • (D) ✗ The distinction has nothing to do with scale. Tom and Hank are individuals, and their

consumption goes beyond their curves too.

  • (E) ✗ The Topic 1.3 statement is about production, which is why it survives.

Question 19 — Comparative advantage between countries · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Your textbook's FYI makes exactly this argument. Productivity differences vary by good: very large in aircraft, much smaller in clothing. Clothing is therefore where the low-income country gives up least, and an absolute disadvantage in every industry is no obstacle at all.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. The uneven productivity gap is the mechanism.
  • (B) ✗ The stem states the opposite, and comparative advantage does not require absolute

advantage — which is the module's central point.

  • (C) ✗ Discards the economics for no reason. The pattern of trade follows from the opportunity

costs.

  • (D) ✗ Exporting requires a price above cost, not below it. A comparative advantage means the

cost is genuinely lower.

  • (E) ✗ Both parties gain from a trade inside the terms-of-trade range. That is what "mutually

beneficial" means.

Question 20 — Imports and exports · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Module 4 answers who exports what, from a comparison of opportunity costs. It contains no world price, no import quantity, no tariff and no surplus analysis. Those are Topic 2.9, they use a supply-and-demand diagram, and world price and autarky occur 0 times in Krugman's 927 pages — so this material has to come from OpenStax or AMSCO.

  • (A) ✗ Comparative advantage gives direction, not quantity. The PPC model has no prices in it at

all.

  • (B) ✗ Neither half is available from this model, and the surplus change is read off the diagram

rather than requiring elasticity.

  • (C) ✗ A world price below the domestic equilibrium is the standard importing case. Nothing is

wrong with the question.

  • (D) ✗ The two diagrams are not convertible. A PPC plots two goods in physical quantities; a

  • (E) ✓ Correct. It names what this module gives, what it does not, and where the rest comes

from.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 13 concept entries appear. Weighting follows the learning objectives: comparative advantage carries three items and the terms of trade three, because MKT-2.A(b) asks students to determine advantage and MKT-2.B(b) to calculate terms of trade. Questions 14–18 test the module's two unnamed requirements, so they are the items that reveal whether the gaps in the textbook were actually filled in class.

Bloom levels. Application 6 · Analysis 9 · Synthesis 5, a ratio of 1 : 1.5 : 0.8 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The deviation is deliberate and is argued under the blueprint: two of this topic's four learning-objective verbs are computational, and a paper that was only one-fifth application would not be testing Topic 1.4. Compare the Module 1 paper, where opportunity cost was the only concept supporting calculation and application was capped at three.

Both data formats appear, as MKT-2.A(b) requires: questions 7, 9 and 14–16 work from PPC-style maxima, while 12 and 13 work from tables — question 12 in input per unit and question 13 in output per worker. Your textbook's own review does the same thing on facing pages, and getting one of them backwards is the commonest failure in this topic.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: inverting the ratio (5B); treating a difference as an opportunity cost (5C); reading an intercept as a rate (5D); comparing raw numbers across producers instead of ratios within one (8E, 12B); claiming a comparative advantage in both goods (9B, 9D, 13B); and confusing absolute advantage with comparative advantage (8A, 11A, 13D, 19B).

Four distractors reach a right-looking conclusion by wrong reasoning, flagged in the solutions: 3D, 8E, 13A and 15A. 13A is the most useful wrong answer on the paper — it spots the genuinely important fact that the two countries make cloth equally fast and then infers exactly the wrong thing from it. A student who picks it has read the table properly and misunderstood the concept, which needs different feedback from a student who did neither.

Answer distribution. A 4 · B 4 · C 4 · D 4 · E 4 — exactly even, and no letter repeats more than twice in a row.

Question 20 is worth ten minutes of discussion. It is the only item that tests the boundary of the module, and the honest answer involves telling students that a whole examinable topic is missing from their textbook. Better they hear it in Unit 1 than discover it in Unit 2.
