The analysis weighting comes from the other half of the module: the income and substitution effects carry no arithmetic at all and are tested almost entirely by scenario.

1. The price of butter rises by 15%. Margarine's price does not change. A shopper who has always bought butter switches to margarine. Butter is about 0.4% of the household's weekly spending.

Which effect explains the switch, and why?

  • (A) The income effect, because the shopper can now afford less butter than before.
  • (B) Both effects equally, because every price change produces both.
  • (C) The substitution effect, almost entirely. Butter has become dearer relative to margarine, so the shopper switches — and at 0.4% of spending the loss of purchasing power is far too small to change anything.
  • (D) Neither effect. This is a movement along the demand curve, which is a separate idea.
  • (E) The income effect, because the shopper's real income has fallen by 15%.

2. Over one year the price of tea and the price of coffee both rise by exactly 12%. Nothing else changes. A drinker of both buys less of each.

What can be said about the substitution effect between tea and coffee?

  • (A) It is zero. Neither drink has become cheaper relative to the other, so there is nothing to substitute towards. The fall in both quantities is the income effect, plus substitution towards everything else whose price did not move.
  • (B) It is negative for both, which is why both quantities fall.
  • (C) It is 12% for each, matching the price rise.
  • (D) It cannot be determined without knowing which drink the consumer prefers.
  • (E) It is positive for tea and negative for coffee, because one must always substitute for the other.

3. Two households each face a 20% rise in the price of petrol. The Ahmeds spend 18% of their income on petrol; the Bakers spend 1%.

For which household is the income effect the more important, and what makes it so?

  • (A) The Bakers, because they have less room in their budget to absorb the rise.
  • (B) Both equally, because the percentage price rise is the same for both.
  • (C) Neither, because the income effect depends on income rather than on spending patterns.
  • (D) The Ahmeds. The income effect is proportional to the share of spending the good absorbs, so a 20% rise costs them roughly 3.6% of their purchasing power against 0.2% for the Bakers.
  • (E) The Ahmeds, but only if petrol is an inferior good for them.

4. A student writes: "There is no income effect here, because the family's wages did not change when the rent went up."

What is wrong with the claim?

  • (A) Nothing — the income effect requires a change in income by definition.
  • (B) The income effect does not need money income to change. It happens because the price change alters what the family's unchanged income will buy, which is a fall in real income.
  • (C) It is wrong because rent is an inferior good, and inferior goods have no income effect.
  • (D) It is wrong because the income effect applies only to the whole economy, not to one family.
  • (E) It is wrong because wages always adjust to price changes in the long run.

5. A worker's pay rises by 2% over a year. The prices of the things they buy rise by 5%.

What has happened to the worker's real income, to the nearest tenth of a percent?

  • (A) It has risen by 3.0%.
  • (B) It has fallen by 5.0%.
  • (C) It is unchanged, because pay and prices both rose.
  • (D) It has risen by 2.0%.
  • (E) It has fallen by about 2.9%.

6. Which of the following is an explanation of the law of demand, rather than a restatement of it?

  • (A) When a good's price rises it becomes relatively more expensive than its alternatives, so buyers switch away from it.
  • (B) When price rises, quantity demanded falls.
  • (C) The demand curve has a negative slope.
  • (D) Higher prices mean people buy less because things cost more.
  • (E) There is an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

7. A question asks for a complete explanation of why an individual's demand curve for rice slopes downward, for a household that spends a large share of its income on rice and treats rice as a normal good.

Which answer earns full marks?

  • (A) Rice is a necessity, so people must buy it whatever the price.
  • (B) As the price of rice rises, the household buys less because its budget is limited.
  • (C) A rise in the price of rice makes it dearer relative to other foods, so the household substitutes towards them; it also lowers the household's real income, and because rice is normal, being poorer reduces the quantity demanded further. Both effects point the same way.
  • (D) A rise in the price of rice lowers the household's real income, so it buys less rice. No other effect is involved.
  • (E) The marginal utility of rice falls as more is consumed, so the household would only buy more at a lower price — and no other explanation is acceptable.

8. Train travel is a normal good for a commuter. The price of train tickets rises.

In which direction does each effect push the quantity of train travel demanded?

  • (A) Substitution pushes it up; income pushes it down.
  • (B) Both push it down. Substitution moves the commuter towards cheaper alternatives, and the loss of real income reduces demand for a normal good as well.
  • (C) Substitution pushes it down; income pushes it up.
  • (D) Both push it up, which is why rail revenue rises.
  • (E) Substitution pushes it down; there is no income effect for a normal good.

9. A family's income falls by a third after a job loss. Their consumption of supermarket own-brand goods rises, and their consumption of branded goods falls.

What does this tell you?

  • (A) Own-brand goods are normal and branded goods are inferior.
  • (B) Both kinds of goods are inferior.
  • (C) The family is behaving irrationally, since less income should mean less of everything.
  • (D) Nothing, because a change in income does not reveal anything about the type of good.
  • (E) Own-brand goods are inferior for this family and branded goods are normal — demand for an inferior good rises when income falls.

10. Instant noodles are an inferior good for a particular student, and their price rises.

In which direction does each effect push the quantity of noodles demanded?

  • (A) Both push it down, as they do for every good.
  • (B) Substitution pushes it up; income pushes it down.
  • (C) Neither effect operates, because inferior goods do not obey the law of demand.
  • (D) Substitution pushes it down, because noodles are now relatively dearer. Income pushes it up, because the price rise makes the student poorer in real terms and demand for an inferior good rises when income falls.
  • (E) Both push it up, which is what makes the good inferior.

11. The two effects for an inferior good pull in opposite directions. Yet the demand curve for almost every inferior good still slopes downward.

What does that imply, and why?

  • (A) The substitution effect must be the larger of the two. If the income effect were bigger, a price rise would raise the quantity demanded and the curve would slope upward — which is the Giffen case, and essentially never observed.
  • (B) The two effects must be exactly equal, so that they cancel and the curve behaves normally.
  • (C) The income effect must be the larger, because it works through purchasing power.
  • (D) It implies nothing about their relative sizes; the slope of the demand curve is a separate matter.
  • (E) It implies the good is only mildly inferior, because strongly inferior goods have upward-sloping demand curves.

12. A student claims: "Bus travel is an inferior good for most commuters, so it must be a Giffen good."

What is wrong with the claim?

  • (A) Nothing — every inferior good is a Giffen good.
  • (B) Bus travel is actually a normal good for most commuters.
  • (C) Giffen goods must be normal goods, not inferior ones.
  • (D) Giffen status depends on the number of substitutes available, which the claim does not mention.
  • (E) Being inferior is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. A Giffen good must also absorb a large share of spending, so that the income effect can outweigh the substitution effect — and for most commuters bus fares are a small share of the budget.

13. A dairy sells milk by the litre; its competitor sells the same milk by the pint. Both raise their prices by the same proportion and lose the same proportion of sales.

Why do the two firms report the same price elasticity of demand?

  • (A) Because the two firms sell the same product, and elasticity is a property of the product.
  • (B) Because elasticity is a ratio of two percentage changes, and percentages do not depend on the units a good is measured in. Litres or pints, the proportional changes are identical.
  • (C) Because elasticity is always measured in litres by convention.
  • (D) Because both firms have the same costs.
  • (E) They would not report the same elasticity; the pint seller's figure would be larger, because a pint is smaller than a litre.

14. A manager reports: "We raised the price by 50p and sales fell by 300 units."

Why is this not yet an elasticity, and what else is needed?

  • (A) An elasticity needs the firm's costs as well as its prices.
  • (B) Nothing else is needed — 300 ÷ 0.50 = 600 is the elasticity.
  • (C) Both figures are changes in units rather than percentages. To turn them into an elasticity you need the starting price and the starting quantity, because 50p on a £1 good and 50p on a £50 good are not the same event.
  • (D) The report is unusable because elasticity can only be computed for a price fall, not a price rise.
  • (E) It needs the sign, since elasticity is always negative.

15. A cinema cuts its ticket price by 8% and finds that the number of tickets sold rises by 20%.

What is the price elasticity of demand over this range?

  • (A) 0.4
  • (B) 12
  • (C) 1.6
  • (D) 2.5
  • (E) 28

16. A clinic charges $40 for a vaccination and administers 200 a week. It raises the price to $42 and administers 196 a week.

Using the starting values to compute each percentage change, what is the price elasticity of demand?

  • (A) 0.4
  • (B) 2.5
  • (C) 0.02
  • (D) 4.0
  • (E) 0.1

17. A supplier raises its price from $8 to $10. Sales fall from 300 units to 240.

A student computes the percentage changes against the starting values, divides, and reports an elasticity of 1.25. What has gone wrong, and what is the correct figure?

  • (A) Nothing has gone wrong; 1.25 is correct.
  • (B) The student used the midpoint method by mistake; the answer should be 1.0.
  • (C) The student dropped the minus sign too early; the answer should be −1.25.
  • (D) The student used the wrong starting price; the answer should be 1.6.
  • (E) The ratio is upside down. Price rose 25% and quantity demanded fell 20%, so the elasticity is 20 ÷ 25 = 0.8, not 25 ÷ 20.

18. A bakery's weekly sales of sourdough rise from 1,200 loaves to 1,500 loaves.

Computing the percentage change against the starting value, by how much did sales rise?

  • (A) 300%
  • (B) 20.0%
  • (C) 25.0%
  • (D) 22.2%
  • (E) 80.0%

19. A price moves from $100 to $120 one month and back from $120 to $100 the next. Computed against the starting value each time, the two percentage changes are 20% and 16.7%.

Why are they different, and what does that imply for elasticity?

  • (A) They are different because the second move is a fall and falls are always smaller.
  • (B) The denominator changes: the first divides $20 by $100, the second divides $20 by $120. So the same pair of prices gives two different percentage changes — and therefore two different elasticities for the same stretch of the same demand curve, depending on which way you travel.
  • (C) They are different because the quantity demanded also changed between the two months.
  • (D) They are not really different; one of the two figures must be a rounding error.
  • (E) They differ because the second calculation should have used the final value as the denominator, which would give 20% again.

20. Two goods have price elasticities of demand reported as −0.3 and −2.5.

Which has the more responsive demand, and why?

  • (A) The one at −0.3, because −0.3 is the larger number.
  • (B) Neither; a negative elasticity means demand does not respond at all.
  • (C) It cannot be said without knowing the prices of the two goods.
  • (D) The one at −2.5. Elasticity is compared by magnitude — the minus sign is just the law of demand showing up in the arithmetic, and 2.5 is far larger than 0.3.
  • (E) The one at −2.5, because a bigger negative number means a steeper demand curve.

21. A grower lowers the price of raspberries from $6 to $4 a punnet, and weekly sales rise from 300 punnets to 500.

Using the midpoint method, what is the price elasticity of demand?

  • (A) 0.80
  • (B) 2.00
  • (C) 0.50
  • (D) 1.00
  • (E) 1.25

22. Measured from the United States, European petrol is 200% dearer. Measured from Europe, American petrol is 66.7% cheaper. The two prices are the same pair of numbers both times.

What problem does this illustrate, and how does the midpoint method solve it?

  • (A) The same two points give two different percentage changes, and so two different elasticities, because each calculation divides by a different starting value. The midpoint method divides by the average of the two values instead, which is the same number whichever end you start from — so the elasticity comes out the same in both directions.
  • (B) It shows that European and American petrol are different goods and cannot be compared at all.
  • (C) It shows that percentage changes should never be used; absolute changes are reliable.
  • (D) It shows that the elasticity of demand for petrol is different in the two countries, which the midpoint method averages out.
  • (E) It shows that taxes distort prices, and the midpoint method removes the tax.

23. An exam question reads: "A 6% fall in the price of a good leads to a 3% rise in the quantity demanded. Calculate the price elasticity of demand."

What is the best approach, and what is the answer?

  • (A) Recompute both percentages by the midpoint method first, then divide; the answer is 0.5.
  • (B) No method is needed, because both percentage changes are given. Divide the quantity change by the price change: 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5.
  • (C) The question cannot be answered without the starting price and quantity.
  • (D) Divide the price change by the quantity change, giving 2.
  • (E) Apply the midpoint formula to find the starting values, then divide; the answer is 2.

24. Your textbook reports a price elasticity of demand of 0.4 for beef and 2.4 for airline travel.

What does the comparison say?

  • (A) Beef sells in larger quantities than airline travel.
  • (B) Airline travel is more expensive than beef.
  • (C) For a given percentage change in price, the quantity of airline travel demanded moves six times as much, in percentage terms, as the quantity of beef.
  • (D) Airline travel costs 2.4 times as much as it used to.
  • (E) Beef is a normal good and airline travel is an inferior good.

25. Your textbook's table of estimates runs from eggs at 0.1 to foreign travel at 4.1.

Which explanation best fits that pattern?

  • (A) Cheap goods always have low elasticities and expensive goods always have high ones.
  • (B) The estimates reflect how often each good is bought, with daily purchases being the least elastic.
  • (C) Goods bought by poorer households are inelastic and goods bought by richer households are elastic.
  • (D) Goods with no close substitute that take a tiny share of spending sit at the inelastic end; goods that can be postponed, replaced or done without, and that take a large share of spending, sit at the elastic end.
  • (E) The numbers are arbitrary, since elasticity cannot be estimated from real data.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Substitution effect · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Both effects exist in principle; the question is which one is big enough to matter. At 0.4% of spending, a 15% price rise costs the household about 0.06% of its purchasing power, which changes nothing. The switch to margarine is relative prices doing their work.

  • (A) ✗ Describes an income effect that is real but negligible here, and mislabels the switch to a

named substitute as affordability.

  • (B) ✗ "Every price change produces both" is true and useless. The module's point is that their

sizes differ enormously.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. It names the effect and gives the share-of-spending reason.
  • (D) ✗ It is indeed a movement along the demand curve for butter — that is what the substitution

effect produces. Naming the movement does not replace explaining it.

  • (E) ✗ Real income has not fallen by 15%. The price of butter rose 15%; purchasing power fell by

15% of 0.4%.

Question 2 — Substitution effect · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The substitution effect is driven by relative prices. If both prices rise by the same proportion, their ratio is unchanged, so there is no reason to switch between them.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Zero between the two, and the fall in both quantities is explained by the

income effect and by substitution towards other goods whose prices did not move.

  • (B) ✗ "Negative substitution effect" is not what the fall shows. The quantities fall for the

other two reasons given in (A).

  • (C) ✗ A substitution effect is not measured by the price change that provoked it, and 12% is the

price rise, not a quantity response.

  • (D) ✗ Preferences would matter if the relative price had moved. It has not, so the answer is the

same for every set of preferences.

  • (E) ✗ Reaches for symmetry that is not there. Nothing has become relatively cheaper.

Question 3 — Income effect · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The size of an income effect is roughly the price change multiplied by the share of the budget the good absorbs: 20% × 18% ≈ 3.6% for the Ahmeds, 20% × 1% = 0.2% for the Bakers.

  • (A) ✗ Right household, wrong one: this names the Bakers. The reasoning about "room in the

budget" is also not the mechanism.

  • (B) ✗ The percentage price rise is the same; the effect on purchasing power is not, because

it depends on how much of their money goes on petrol.

  • (C) ✗ The income effect works on real income, which is exactly where spending patterns enter.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. It names the household and quantifies the mechanism.
  • (E) ✗ Inferior or normal decides the direction of the income effect, not whether it is

large. A large share of spending makes it large either way.

Question 4 — Income effect · Analysis

Answer: (B)

This is the error the module is most often failed on. The income effect does not require money income to move; it requires purchasing power to move, which a price change does on its own.

  • (A) ✗ Repeats the misdefinition. The textbook's own phrase is "a change in the consumer's

purchasing power when the price of the good changes".

  • (B) ✓ Correct. It names real income as the thing that changed.
  • (C) ✗ Rent is normally a normal good, and in any case inferior goods have income effects — they

run the other way.

  • (D) ✗ The income effect is defined on an individual consumer.
  • (E) ✗ Wage adjustment is a different question entirely, and irrelevant to this month's rent.

Question 5 — Real income · Application

Answer: (E)

Real income is money income divided by prices. 1.02 ÷ 1.05 = 0.9714, a fall of about 2.9%. The quick approximation — 2% minus 5% — gives −3%, which is close enough to choose the option but worth knowing is an approximation.

  • (A) ✗ Gets the sign backwards and uses the raw difference. Prices rose faster than pay.
  • (B) ✗ Counts the price rise alone and ignores the 2% pay rise.
  • (C) ✗ They both rose, but not by the same amount, which is the whole point.
  • (D) ✗ The 2% is the money income rise, which is precisely the number that does not answer

the question.

  • (E) ✓ Correct. 1.02 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = −2.86%, which rounds to −2.9%.

Question 6 — Why demand curves slope downward · Analysis

Answer: (A)

An explanation names a mechanism. The other four options restate the law of demand in different words, which is what students do when they have not met the two effects.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. This is the substitution effect, stated as a mechanism.
  • (B) ✗ The law of demand itself.
  • (C) ✗ The same statement in geometry.
  • (D) ✗ Circular: "people buy less because things cost more" is the claim being explained.
  • (E) ✗ The same statement again, in the language of relationships.

Question 7 — Why demand curves slope downward · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

The question specifies a large budget share and a normal good, so a complete answer must use both effects and must get the direction of the income effect right.

  • (A) ✗ "A necessity, so people must buy it whatever the price" denies the law of demand rather

than explaining it.

  • (B) ✗ A budget constraint on its own is not an explanation — it is true at every price.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. Substitution, then income, then the direction, then the conclusion that they

agree.

  • (D) ✗ Reaches the right conclusion by half the reasoning, and explicitly denies the

substitution effect, which is the larger of the two.

  • (E) ✗ Diminishing marginal utility is an acceptable alternative route — but "and no other

explanation is acceptable" is false, and this question asks for the two-effect answer.

Question 8 — Normal goods · Analysis

Answer: (B)

For a normal good, the two effects agree: substitution moves the commuter away from the dearer good, and the real-income loss reduces demand for a normal good as well.

  • (A) ✗ Reverses both.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ This is the inferior good case.
  • (D) ✗ Would mean an upward-sloping demand curve. Revenue may rise, but only because price rose,

not because quantity did.

  • (E) ✗ Normal goods have income effects; being normal is what fixes their direction.

Question 9 — Inferior goods · Application

Answer: (E)

Income fell and demand for own-brand goods rose — that is the definition of an inferior good. Branded goods moved with income, so they are normal.

  • (A) ✗ Exactly backwards.
  • (B) ✗ Branded goods fell when income fell, which makes them normal.
  • (C) ✗ Buying more of a cheaper substitute when poorer is the textbook response, not an

irrational one.

  • (D) ✗ A change in income is precisely how normal and inferior are defined.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 10 — When the two effects pull apart · Analysis

Answer: (D)

A price rise always makes the good relatively dearer, so substitution always points down. For an inferior good the real-income loss points up, because demand for an inferior good rises when the buyer is poorer.

  • (A) ✗ True for normal goods only.
  • (B) ✗ Reverses both directions.
  • (C) ✗ Inferior goods obey the law of demand; the module says so explicitly.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Both directions, with the reason for each.
  • (E) ✗ Would be a Giffen good and then some, and is not what "inferior" means.

Question 11 — When the two effects pull apart · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

If the two effects oppose and the net result is still a fall in quantity demanded, the downward one must be the bigger. That is an inference about relative size, and it is the module's own conclusion.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, and it names the alternative — the Giffen case — and dismisses it on

evidence.

  • (B) ✗ Exact cancellation would give a vertical demand curve, not a downward-sloping one.
  • (C) ✗ If income were larger, the curve would slope upward.
  • (D) ✗ The slope is exactly what reveals which effect dominates.
  • (E) ✗ Invents a scale of "mild" and "strong" inferiority that does the work the budget share

actually does.

Question 12 — Giffen goods · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

A Giffen good needs two things at once: inferiority, so the income effect pushes the wrong way, and a large budget share, so that push is big enough to win. Almost no good has both.

  • (A) ✗ The claim under test. If it were true, Giffen goods would be everywhere.
  • (B) ✗ Bus travel is a standard example of an inferior good; that part of the claim is fine.
  • (C) ✗ Backwards: a normal good can never be Giffen, because both effects point the same way.
  • (D) ✗ Substitutes matter for the size of the substitution effect, but the missing condition here

is the share of spending.

  • (E) ✓ Correct. Necessary, not sufficient — and it supplies the missing condition.

Question 13 — Elasticity · Analysis

Answer: (B)

This is the reason the measure is built from percentages. A litre is 1.76 pints, but a 10% rise is a 10% rise in either unit, so the ratio is unaffected.

  • (A) ✗ Elasticity is a property of a market and a price range, not of a substance. The same

good has different elasticities in different places and over different ranges.

  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ There is no such convention, and if there were it would not matter.
  • (D) ✗ Costs have nothing to do with the elasticity of demand.
  • (E) ✗ The denial of the point. Changing the unit changes neither percentage.

Question 14 — Elasticity · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Elasticity is a ratio of two percentage changes. Raw changes in pence and units cannot be divided into one, and the missing ingredients are the two starting values.

  • (A) ✗ Costs are not part of the price elasticity of demand.
  • (B) ✗ Dividing a quantity change by a price change gives a slope in units, not an elasticity —

and the figure moves if you switch from pence to pounds.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. It says what is missing and why it matters.
  • (D) ✗ Elasticity can be computed for a rise or a fall; that is what the midpoint method is for.
  • (E) ✗ The sign is known already from the law of demand, and is dropped in any case.

Question 15 — Price elasticity of demand · Application

Answer: (D)

Both percentages are given, which is how exam items usually present this. 20% ÷ 8% = 2.5.

  • (A) ✗ The ratio upside down: 8 ÷ 20.

  • (D) ✓ Correct. Quantity on top: 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5.

Question 16 — Price elasticity of demand · Application

Answer: (A)

Quantity: 4 ÷ 200 = 2%. Price: $2 ÷ $40 = 5%. Elasticity: 2 ÷ 5 = 0.4. These are the textbook's own Figure 46.1 proportions at a different scale.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The ratio upside down: 5 ÷ 2.
  • (C) ✗ The quantity percentage expressed as a decimal and never divided by anything.
  • (D) ✗ 0.4 with the decimal point lost.
  • (E) ✗ Divides the quantity change by the price change in raw units, 4 ÷ 40.

Question 17 — Price elasticity of demand · Application

Answer: (E)

Price: $2 ÷ $8 = 25%. Quantity: 60 ÷ 300 = 20%. The elasticity is 20 ÷ 25 = 0.8. Reporting 1.25 means the student divided price by quantity.

  • (A) ✗ 1.25 is 25 ÷ 20 — the ratio inverted.
  • (B) ✗ The midpoint method would give 22.2 ÷ 22.2 = 1.0, which is a different calculation, not

this one. The student did use the starting values, as instructed.

  • (C) ✗ Dropping the sign is correct practice, and the magnitude would still be wrong.
  • (D) ✗ $8 is the starting price and is the right one to use.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. It diagnoses the inversion and gives the figure.

Answer: (C)

300 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 25.0%. Divide by where you started.

  • (A) ✗ The change itself, labelled as a percentage.
  • (B) ✗ Divides by the final value: 300 ÷ 1,500.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The midpoint answer, 300 ÷ 1,350. A good number to recognise, but the question specified

Question 19 — Percent change · Analysis

Answer: (B)

The initial-value method makes the denominator depend on the direction of travel, so one stretch of one demand curve yields two elasticities. That is the problem the next entry solves.

  • (A) ✗ There is no rule that falls are smaller; it is the denominator, not the direction, that

does this.

  • (B) ✓ Correct. It identifies the denominator and draws the consequence for elasticity.
  • (C) ✗ The scenario concerns price only, and the asymmetry would arise even with quantity held

Question 20 — Absolute value · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Price elasticity of demand is compared by magnitude. The minus sign is always there and carries no information, which is why it is normally dropped.

  • (A) ✗ Treats −0.3 as a signed number on a number line. In this topic "larger" means larger in

absolute value.

  • (B) ✗ Every price elasticity of demand is negative before the sign is dropped; it does not mean

no response.

  • (C) ✗ The elasticities already encode the price changes. Nothing else is needed.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Right answer, wrong reason — and the reason is backwards. A larger elasticity goes

with a flatter demand curve over the range, not a steeper one. A student who picks this needs different feedback from one who picked (A).

Question 21 — The midpoint method · Application

Answer: (E)

Quantity: 200 ÷ 400 = 50%. Price: −$2 ÷ $5 = −40%. Elasticity: 50 ÷ 40 = 1.25, and it would be 1.25 travelling the other way too.

  • (A) ✗ The ratio upside down: 40 ÷ 50.
  • (B) ✗ The initial-value answer: 66.7 ÷ 33.3. Right method for a different question.
  • (C) ✗ Halves the quantity change but not the price change — the classic mixed-method slip.
  • (D) ✗ Would require the two percentage changes to be equal; they are 50 and 40.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 22 — The midpoint method · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The petrol example is the module's own motivation for the method. Two prices, two starting points, two percentages — and therefore two elasticities unless the denominator is fixed.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. It states the problem and the mechanism of the fix.
  • (B) ✗ They are the same good at two prices; comparing them is the entire exercise.
  • (C) ✗ Absolute changes are worse: they depend on units as well as on direction.
  • (D) ✗ Elasticity in the two countries may well differ, but that is not what the 200% and 66.7%

figures show — those are two measurements of the same price gap.

  • (E) ✗ Taxes explain why the prices differ; they have nothing to do with the arithmetic problem.

Question 23 — Which calculation your exam expects · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

The percentages are given, so there is no method to choose: the definition is the ratio, and the ratio is all that is wanted. 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5.

Question 24 — Elastic and inelastic, a first look · Analysis

Answer: (C)

An elasticity is a ratio of percentage changes, so comparing two of them compares responsiveness and nothing else. 2.4 ÷ 0.4 = 6.

  • (A) ✗ Elasticity says nothing about the quantities traded.
  • (B) ✗ Nor about price levels. A cheap good can be elastic and a dear one inelastic.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Reads the elasticity as though it were a price index.
  • (E) ✗ Confuses price elasticity with income elasticity, which is a different measure and a later

topic.

Question 25 — What real elasticities look like · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 16 concept entries appear. Price elasticity of demand takes three items (15, 16, 17) and the two calculation methods take three more (18, 21, 23), because MKT-3.E is a calculate objective and Topic 2.3's suggested skill is the quantitative one. The income and substitution effects take twelve items between them, which matches their share of the module's pages.

Bloom levels. Application 7 · Analysis 12 · Synthesis 6, a ratio of 1 : 1.7 : 0.9 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The paper is more application-weighted than the standard because half of its content is a calculation; it is less so than the Module 51 paper, because the other half — the two effects — carries no arithmetic at all and can only be tested by scenario.

Question 23 is the one that matters most. It tests whether a student who has spent three pages on the midpoint method can recognise a question that does not need it. Students who choose (A) or (E) have learned the technique as though it were the definition, which is precisely the risk this module creates.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: the ratio inverted (15A, 16B, 17A, 21A, 23D); the mixed-method slip, where the average is used on one half of the ratio and the starting value on the other (21C, 18D); treating raw unit changes as an elasticity (14B, 16E); the "income effect means income changed" misreading (4A, 1A); and reversing the direction of the income effect for an inferior good (10A, 10B, 8A).

Three distractors reach a right-looking conclusion by wrong reasoning, and are flagged as such in the solutions: 3A, 7D and 20E. The most useful is 20E — it picks the right good for a reason that is exactly backwards, and a student who chooses it needs different feedback from one who chose (A).

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 5 · D 5 · E 5 — exactly even, with no letter repeating in consecutive questions.

What this paper deliberately does not test. The benchmark of 1, unit elastic, the determinants of elasticity and the effect of a price change on total revenue are all Module 47. Question 24 and question 25 go as far as the textbook does here — "bigger means more responsive" and the pattern behind the estimates — and stop. Setting this paper after Module 47 would leave two-thirds of that module untested; set it in between.
