01

Economics

★ Exam
Definition

Economics is the study of scarcity and choice.

Textbook page 2.

Five words containing the whole subject. There is never enough of what people want, so somebody has to choose — and every choice gives something up. Everything you study this year is a version of that one idea.

Your textbook draws the line sharply: "you might say that it isn't economics if it isn't about choice." If a question has no trade-off in it, it is not an economic question, however much money is involved.

Notice that size is not part of the definition. The same reasoning works for one person deciding how to spend an afternoon and for a country deciding how to spend a budget. In this course it is almost always the second one — that is what makes it macroeconomics.

Watch out. Economics is not "the study of money." Money is just one of the scarce things you will study, and the scarcest thing in this module is time, which has no price tag at all.

Textbook

A supermarket aisle. Thousands of products, and you could not afford all of them. Even if you could, there is only so much room at home. Both limits bind, so you choose what to leave on the shelf.

Numeric

A national budget. A government splits a fixed $500 billion between health and transport. Every billion to one is a billion the other cannot have. Wanting both does not make the trade-off go away.

Illustrative

A coffee shop. One espresso machine, two staff, a morning rush. Making one complicated drink properly means three simpler customers wait. Money is not the problem — minutes on the machine are.

How it connects. Economics is made of scarcity (entry 2) and choice (entry 4). It is not the same word as economy (entry 8) — that entry has a test you can use.

Check yourself

1.1 Your school has a fixed budget. Is deciding how to spend it an economic question? Why?

Show answer

Yes. The budget is limited, so spending on one thing means not spending on another. That trade-off is what makes a question economic.

1.2 Is "which colour should I paint my room?" an economic question?

Show answer

Not unless something is given up. If both paints cost the same and take the same time, nothing is sacrificed — it is a question of taste. If the blue costs more, or painting uses a Saturday you would otherwise have worked, then it is.

02

Scarcity

★ Exam
Definition

A resource is scarce when there is not enough of it available to satisfy all the different ways a society wants to use it.

Textbook pages 3–4.

Scarcity is about competing uses. It does not mean "rare" and it does not mean "running out." Something is scarce when using it one way means you cannot use it another way.

Two things surprise people. Something can be plentiful and still scarce — there is an enormous amount of fresh water on Earth, and it is very scarce in Arizona. And something can be rare without being scarce, if nobody wants it.

The scarcest thing in this module is time. Your textbook makes the point deliberately: money is not your only limit, because "there are only 24 hours in a day." Time never sends you a bill, which is exactly why students forget to count it in entry 5.

⚑ One word your exam cares about and your textbook does not say. The framework's sentence is that most resources are scarce — not all. Some are non-rival: using them does not use them up. If you use the Pythagorean theorem, everyone else can still use it at the same time. Land, labour and capital are scarce; ideas need not be. Learn that exception; it is the kind of thing a question is built on.

Watch out. Do not write "scarce" when you mean "expensive" or "almost gone." A high price is a result of scarcity in a market. It is not the same thing.

Textbook

Convenience stores. They charge more than supermarkets and stay in business anyway. Some customers would rather pay extra than spend the time driving further and queueing. They are trading money for time — which only makes sense if time is scarce.

Numeric

Four hours, six hours of work. You have 4 hours before an exam and 6 hours of revision you would like to do. The 4 hours are scarce: every hour on one subject is an hour not on another. Give yourself 10 free hours and the problem disappears.

Illustrative

The exception. A recipe, a language, a formula. Using it does not stop anyone else using it, so it is not scarce — however valuable it is.

How it connects. One half of economics (entry 1). The things that are scarce are resources (entry 3), and scarcity is the reason opportunity cost (entry 5) exists at all.

Check yourself

2.1 Which is scarce: a rare coin nobody wants, or an ordinary meeting room two teams both need on Tuesday?

Show answer

The meeting room. Rarity is not the test — competing uses are. The coin is rare and nobody is competing for it; the room is ordinary and two uses conflict.

2.2 Name a resource that is not scarce, and say why.

Show answer

Any non-rival input: a mathematical formula, a language, an open-source program, a published recipe. Using it does not stop anyone else using it, so no use competes with another.

03

Resources (factors of production)

★ Exam
Definition

A resource is anything that can be used to produce something else. Resources are sometimes called factors of production and fall into four categories:

Land — everything that comes from nature: timber, water, minerals, oil.
Labour — the effort of workers.
Capital — machinery, buildings and other manufactured goods used to make goods and services.
Entrepreneurship — risk-taking, innovation, and organising the other three for production.

Textbook page 3.

Resources are inputs, not outputs. A cheeseburger is not a resource; the cook's labour, the grill and the land the restaurant stands on are.

Capital is the one people get wrong, because in ordinary English "capital" means money. In economics, capital is manufactured things used to make other things — machines, buildings, tools. Money is not capital. Money is what you buy capital with.

Watch out. When a business says "we need capital to expand", they mean money. The capital is the ovens and vans the money would buy. Examiners test this exact confusion.

Textbook

The review question. Time spent flipping hamburgers is labour; a bulldozer is capital; a river is land. Those three are from your book's own Check Your Understanding.

Numeric

A bakery. 6 staff, 2 ovens, 200 m² of floor space. Labour 6, capital 2, land 200 m². The recipe it uses is not a scarce resource at all — see entry 2.

Illustrative

Four in a row. A delivery van is capital. A farmer's field is land. An accountant's afternoon is labour. Deciding to launch a new drink is entrepreneurship.

How it connects. Resources are the things that are scarce (entry 2), and they are what an economy (entry 8) coordinates. In Module 3 you will draw a curve whose shape depends on them.

Check yourself

3.1 Classify: a delivery van, a wheat field, an accountant's afternoon, deciding to open a second branch.

Show answer

Van = capital. Field = land. Afternoon = labour. Decision = entrepreneurship.

3.2 A firm says it needs capital and applies for a loan. What is the capital?

Show answer

Not the money. The capital is the machinery, vehicles or premises the loan would pay for. The loan is finance; the capital is what it buys.

04

Individual choice

★ Exam
Definition

Individual choice is decisions by individuals about what to do, which necessarily involve decisions about what not to do.

Textbook page 2.

The definition contains its own second half, and that half is the point: choosing to do one thing is choosing not to do the others. That is what gives every choice a cost, and entry 5 is the name of that cost.

Your exam framework says individuals and societies are forced to choose. In this course the second half matters more — every macroeconomic policy question is a society choosing. A government that funds a hospital has chosen not to fund the road that money would have built.

Watch out. "I haven't decided yet" is a decision. Not signing up is the choice not to join, and it gives up exactly the same alternatives. Delay does not remove a choice; it makes it for you.

Textbook

The definition itself. Your book writes "decisions about what to do, which necessarily involve decisions about what not to do." The second clause is the content, not decoration.

Numeric

Four hours, one club. You have 4 free hours and join a 3-hour club. You have simultaneously chosen not to spend those 3 hours on a shift, on revision, or on rest.

Illustrative

A society choosing. A country with enough engineers for a railway or a flood barrier, not both. Funding one is choosing against the other — and this is the version your exam asks about.

How it connects. The other half of economics (entry 1), and the reason opportunity cost (entry 5) exists.

Check yourself

4.1 You put off deciding whether to join a club until the deadline passes. Did you make a choice?

Show answer

Yes — the choice not to join, with the same forgone alternatives as an explicit refusal. Not deciding is deciding by default.

4.2 Why does the framework say "individuals and societies"?

Show answer

Because scarcity forces groups to choose exactly as it forces people to. A government with a fixed budget faces the same structure as a student with a fixed afternoon — and in macroeconomics that is nearly always the case being analysed.

05

Opportunity cost

★ Exam
Definition

The real cost of an item is its opportunity cost: what you must give up in order to get it.

Textbook pages 4–5.

This is the most important entry in the module and the only one your exam asks you to calculate. Two rules do almost all the work.

Rule one: it is the single best alternative you gave up, not everything you gave up. If you could have worked, revised, or slept, and you choose to sleep, the opportunity cost is whichever of work and revision was better — not both added together.

Rule two: it has two parts and students count only one. Explicit cost is money you actually hand over. Implicit cost is the value of the time or the thing you used up. Your textbook's college example is built on it: tuition is explicit, the salary you did not earn for four years is implicit, and the second is usually bigger.

Opportunity cost calculator

Explicit + implicit — try changing one number at a time

Explicit cost$6
Implicit cost$72
Opportunity cost$78

Set the money you pay to 0 and the total does not fall to zero. That is what "free is not free" means.

Watch out. A cost you would pay either way is not part of the opportunity cost of either option. If both jobs require the same $200 uniform, the uniform is irrelevant to choosing between them.

Textbook

Going to college. Tuition and books are explicit. The salary you did not earn for four years is implicit, and for most people it is the larger number. For LeBron James it was so large that college was not worth it.

Numeric

A free ticket. A free 4-hour concert, $6 travel, and the alternative is a shift paying $18 an hour. Explicit $6, implicit 4 × 18 = $72, opportunity cost $78. The ticket was free; attending was not.

Illustrative

Changing jobs. Moving from a $45,000 job to a $50,000 one: the $45,000 you gave up is part of the cost, the extra commuting time is, and the nicer office is not — that is a benefit of the new job, not something you gave up.

How it connects. It exists because of scarcity (entry 2) and choice (entry 4). In Module 3 you will read it straight off a graph — the slope of the curve is the opportunity cost.

Check yourself

5.1 On Saturday you could work a shift worth $90, revise, or see family. You rank the shift best and revision second, and you see family. What is the opportunity cost?

Show answer

The shift — the single best alternative you gave up. Not the shift plus the revision; you could only have done one of them instead.

5.2 Two friends each pay $40 for the same concert. One would otherwise have worked a $95 shift; the other would have watched television they value at $10. Do they bear the same cost?

Show answer

No. Same explicit cost, different implicit cost: $40 + $95 = $135 against $40 + $10 = $50. Same price, very different opportunity cost — which is why price never measures cost on its own.

⚑ Topic 1.1 ends here; the rest of the module is Topics 1.2, 1.5 and 1.6. Entries 1–5 are the scarcity block. What follows is the other half of Unit 1's opening: marginal analysis (entries 6–7), the three economic systems (9–11), and the two ideas that make a market work at all (12–13). Only entries 8, 14, 15, 16 and 17 are background — and even those are worth one reading.

06

Marginal benefit and marginal cost

★ Exam
Definition

Marginal benefit is the gain from doing something one more time. Marginal cost is what that one extra time costs.

Textbook page 3. Topics 1.5 and 1.6.

"Marginal" means one more. Not the total, not the average — just the next one. It is one of the most useful ideas in economics, and in this course it is everywhere.

⚑ Where this pair goes. Marginal benefit and marginal cost are not background here. They are Topic 1.5 (cost-benefit analysis) and Topic 1.6 (marginal analysis and consumer choice) in Unit 1, and they return as marginal revenue against marginal cost in Unit 3, marginal revenue product in Unit 5, and marginal social benefit against marginal social cost in Unit 6. Half of this course is one comparison, applied in four places.

Watch out. Costs that do not change when you do one more thing are not marginal costs. A theatre's building is already paid for and does not change when one more ticket is sold.

Textbook

The definition. Your book introduces it as the study of "doing a little bit more of an activity versus a little bit less". Always the next unit, never the whole.

Numeric

Pizza. After three slices the fourth gives less extra satisfaction than the third. If that fourth slice is worth 40 to you and costs 45, it makes you worse off — even though pizza is a good thing.

Illustrative

One more seat. A theatre with empty seats sells one more ticket for $12. Cleaning and printing cost $3. The $900 building bill does not change, so the sale adds $9.

How it connects. Different from opportunity cost (entry 5) — that is the whole sacrifice, this is the cost of one more. It feeds marginal analysis (entry 7).

Check yourself

6.1 Is the theatre's rent part of the marginal cost of selling one more ticket?

Show answer

No. The rent is paid whether or not that seat sells, so it does not change with the extra ticket. Only costs that vary with the extra unit count.

6.2 Why does marginal benefit usually fall as you do more of something?

Show answer

Because the first units satisfy the strongest wants. The fourth slice of pizza meets a much weaker need than the first, so it adds less.

07

Marginal analysis

★ Exam
Definition

The study of the costs and benefits of doing a little bit more of an activity versus a little bit less.

Textbook page 3. Topic 1.6.

The rule is short: keep going while marginal benefit is above marginal cost, and stop when it is not. The best point is where they are equal.

It is worth understanding because it is how economists think about any "how much?" question — and it makes Unit 3's arithmetic feel natural when you get there. It is not worth revising for this exam.

Watch out. "Do it while the benefit is positive" is the wrong rule. The first unit almost always has a positive benefit, so that rule never stops. Compare the benefit with the cost of the same unit.

Textbook

The framing. "A little bit more versus a little bit less" — the comparison is always at the edge, never between the totals.

Numeric

40 against 45. Marginal benefit 40, marginal cost 45. Do not do that one: it makes you worse off by 5, even if the activity overall is worthwhile.

Illustrative

Revision sessions. The first extra session helps a lot. The eighth helps barely at all and still costs a teacher's evening. The right number is where the two meet.

How it connects. Built from marginal benefit and cost (entry 6), and it uses the same logic as opportunity cost (entry 5) — every extra unit gives something up.

Check yourself

7.1 Marginal benefit is 40 and marginal cost is 45. Should you do it?

Show answer

No. The cost of that unit exceeds its benefit, so it reduces your net gain by 5 — regardless of how worthwhile the activity is in general.

7.2 What is wrong with "keep going while the benefit is positive"?

Show answer

It never stops. Benefit is almost always positive; the question is whether it beats the cost of that same extra unit.

08

Economy

○ Background
Definition

An economy is a system for coordinating a society's productive and consumptive activities.

Textbook page 2.

The word you need to separate from economics. Economics is the study; an economy is the thing being studied. Japan has an economy; you study economics.

The quickest test is the article. "A market ___" and "Japan's ___" both need a countable noun, so those are economy. "Studying ___" takes no article, so that is economics.

Every economy answers three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom.

Watch out. Mixing the two words up in a written answer reads as carelessness even when the economics is right. It costs you nothing to get it right and it is a two-second check.

Textbook

The emphasis. Your book's definition stresses coordinating. An economy is not the pile of goods; it is the system that decides who makes them and who gets them.

Numeric

$20 trillion, nobody in charge. The U.S. economy coordinated roughly $20 trillion of production in a recent year across millions of independent decisions with no one directing it.

Illustrative

A household. It decides what gets cooked, who cooks it and who eats — the three questions in miniature. It is also far too small to be a society, so do not push the analogy.

How it connects. Not the same word as economics (entry 1). It coordinates resources (entry 3), and entries 9, 10 and 11 are three ways of doing it.

Check yourself

8.1 Fill in: "She studies ___ at university." and "Japan's ___ grew last year."

Show answer

Economics at university; Japan's economy grew. Article test: "Japan's" needs a countable noun, "studies" does not.

8.2 What are the three questions every economy answers?

Show answer

What to produce; how to produce it; for whom — who ends up consuming it.

09

Market economy

★ Exam
Definition

In a market economy, the decisions of individual producers and consumers largely determine what, how and for whom to produce, with little government involvement in those decisions.

Textbook page 2. Topic 1.2, MKT-1.B.2.

Nobody is in charge, and that is the point. Coordination happens through prices: a high price says something is wanted and rewards whoever supplies it, so producers move toward it without being told.

Watch out. "Market economy" does not mean "no government." The definition says little government involvement in the decisions about what, how and for whom. Taxes and regulations do not disqualify a country from the label.

Textbook

Prices and profits. High prices and profits lead producers to make more of the most-needed goods, because whoever produces them keeps the profit.

Numeric

$2 to $5. A shortage pushes a price from $2 to $5. No authority ordered the rise, and no authority ordered the extra production that follows. The $3 is the whole message.

Illustrative

Who finds the cheapest method? The producers do, each one looking for it — because whatever they save, they keep.

How it connects. One way to run an economy (entry 8), the opposite of a command economy (entry 10). It works through incentives (entry 12).

Check yourself

9.1 A shortage of a good ends without anyone ordering more production. How?

Show answer

The price rises. That rations the existing supply and simultaneously rewards anyone who produces more, so producers move toward the good because they gain by it.

9.2 Can a country with high taxes still be a market economy?

Show answer

Yes. What matters is that firms and consumers are the ones deciding what gets produced and bought. Taxes and regulation do not settle the question.

10

Command economy

★ Exam
Definition

In a command economy, industry is publicly owned and a central authority makes the production and consumption decisions.

Textbook pages 2–3. Topic 1.2, MKT-1.B.2.

Two conditions, and you need both. Public ownership alone is not a command economy, and central planning alone is not either.

Your textbook's explanation of why they work badly is an incentive argument, and it is the useful part: Soviet producers "had little incentive to produce what consumers wanted", so shortages persisted and queues formed. Nobody gained anything by noticing a shortage and fixing it.

Watch out. A country with public healthcare and private industry is not a command economy. Industry is not publicly owned, and firms still decide what to make.

Textbook

The Soviet Union, 1917–1991. Command economies, your book notes, "are famous for long lines at shops."

Numeric

10 million against 14. A plan sets shoe output at 10 million pairs; demand turns out to be 14 million. In a market the price rises and output follows. Under the plan the gap stays until the next plan is written.

Illustrative

A school canteen. One administrator fixes the menu, the quantities and the price for the year. Queues and waste both follow, from the same cause.

How it connects. The other way to run an economy (entry 8), opposite to a market economy (entry 9).

Check yourself

10.1 What two things must be true for a command economy?

Show answer

Industry is publicly owned, and a central authority makes the production and consumption decisions. Both, at the same time.

10.2 Why do shortages persist in a command economy?

Show answer

Because nobody gains from noticing one and producing more. Without that reward, a shortage carries no signal that anyone has a reason to act on.

11

Mixed economy

★ Exam
Definition

An economy in which both markets and government make resource allocation decisions — which is what every real economy is.

Not in your textbook. Topic 1.2, MKT-1.B.2.

⚑ Required, and not in your book. This is the one term in Module 1 your framework demands and your textbook never defines. MKT-1.B.2 reads: "Resource allocation is significantly influenced by the economic system adopted by society, such as command economy, market economy, or mixed economy. Each system involves a particular set of institutional arrangements and a coordinating mechanism for allocating scarce resources and distributing output."

All three are named. Krugman defines two. Learn the third from this entry — it is the only gap in the module you cannot close by reading the book more carefully.

Pure market and pure command economies are extremes that help you think. Every real country sits between them, and the interesting question about any of them is which decisions are not left to the market.

Watch out. "Mixed" is not a third type sitting alongside the other two. It is the whole region between them — and essentially everything real is in it.

This course

Every Unit 6 topic. Price ceilings, taxes, antitrust, pollution charges, public goods — every intervention you will study is a government making an allocation decision inside a market economy. That is what a mixed economy is, and Unit 6 is nine weeks of examples.

Numeric

35–45%. Government spending is roughly 35–45% of GDP across most developed economies. Neither extreme describes that.

Illustrative

A typical country. Firms set prices and choose products; the state runs schools, roads and courts and taxes to pay for them. Markets make most decisions, government makes some.

How it connects. What every real economy (entry 8) is, sitting between a market economy (entry 9) and a command economy (entry 10).

Check yourself

11.1 Why study pure market and pure command economies if no country is either?

Show answer

Because they are limiting cases. Comparing a real country to a pure market shows you exactly which decisions are not being made by the market — and that is the interesting part.

11.2 Which of the three economic systems does your textbook not define, and why must you learn it anyway?

Show answer

The mixed economy. MKT-1.B.2 names all three systems, so it is examinable; Krugman defines only market and command. This is a gap between the book and the framework, and you close it here.

12

Incentives

★ Exam
Definition

Incentives are rewards or punishments that motivate particular choices.

Textbook pages 2–3. Topic 2.1, MKT-3.A.2.

An incentive changes what you gain from doing something, which is why it changes behaviour when information and appeals do not. Your textbook's example is exact: asking people to pollute less voluntarily probably will not work, while charging them for polluting is more likely to.

⚑ This is an examinable idea, not a preamble. MKT-3.A.2 states flatly that "Economic agents respond to incentives", and MKT-3.A.3 adds that they respond "to incentives, such as those presented by prices, but also face constraints, such as income, time, and legal and regulatory frameworks." That pair is the law of demand, three weeks from now. Every shift you draw in Unit 2 is an incentive changing.

Watch out. An incentive is not the same as information. Telling people recycling helps changes what they know. Charging for unsorted waste changes what they gain. Only the second is an incentive.

Textbook

Pollution. Economists are "skeptical of any attempt to change behaviour that doesn't change incentives" — a voluntary appeal against a financial one.

Numeric

The Unit 2 version. A $2 tax per unit raises the price a buyer pays from $8 to $9.50. Nothing about the good changed and nobody was persuaded of anything; the payoff changed by $1.50, and quantity demanded falls. That is the whole mechanism of Topic 2.8.

Illustrative

A tidy classroom. A class that gets a free period when the room is left tidy will tidy the room. A poster asking them to tidy the room changes information, not payoff.

How it connects. Prices work by being incentives, which is how a market economy (entry 9) coordinates. Most incentives exist because of property rights (entry 13).

Check yourself

12.1 A city wants less food waste. Leaflets about the environment, or $2 per bag of unsorted waste — which works better, and why?

Show answer

The charge. It changes what a household gains by sorting; the leaflet only changes what it knows. Changing the payoff is what changes behaviour.

12.2 Give an example of a punishment used as an incentive.

Show answer

A fine, a late fee, a lost deposit, losing a licence — any penalty attached to a specific action. Incentives run in both directions.

13

Property rights

★ Exam
Definition

Property rights establish ownership and grant individuals the right to trade goods and services with one another.

Textbook page 3. Topic 2.1, MKT-3.A.1; Topic 6.2, POL-3.A.2.

⚑ This term carries two units. MKT-3.A.1 opens the whole of Unit 2 with it: "A well-defined system of property rights is necessary for the market system to function well." And POL-3.A.2 closes the course with the other side of it — externalities "arise from lack of well-defined property rights and/or high transaction costs." The litter in the park below is not an aside. It is Unit 6.

Ownership decides who bears the consequences of a decision, and that is what makes incentives work at all. An owner who plans to harvest the same forest in twenty years loses money by clear-cutting it now, so they replant. Nobody owns the litter in a public park, so nobody bears the cost of the mess.

Watch out. Property rights are not only about possession. The right to trade is half the definition, and it is the half that makes a market possible.

Textbook

The pairing. Your book puts property rights and incentives together: ownership is what "creates many of the incentives" in a market economy.

Numeric

Twenty years. An owner who can sell timber in twenty years harvests gradually and replants. On open-access land, whoever leaves a tree standing gains nothing by it — so nobody does.

Illustrative

Two gardens. Litter in a public park and litter in your own garden. The mess is identical; who pays for it is not.

How it connects. Ownership is what makes an incentive (entry 12) stick, which is how a market economy (entry 9) coordinates at all. Where rights are missing, Unit 6 calls the result an externality.

Check yourself

13.1 Why does a forest owner replant when an open-access forest gets cleared?

Show answer

The owner bears the future cost of an empty forest, so avoids it. On open land nobody bears that cost, so no one has a reason to leave a tree standing.

13.2 Which half of the definition makes trade possible?

Show answer

The right to trade. Owning something you may not sell or exchange does not create a market.

14

Microeconomics and macroeconomics

○ But read this one
Definitions

Microeconomics is the study of how people make decisions and how those decisions interact.

Macroeconomics is concerned with the overall ups and downs in the economy.

Textbook page 5, with Table 1.1.

No exam question will ask you to define these. Read the entry anyway — it tells you which column of Table 1.1 this course lives in, and it is the left one.

Everything you study this year is on the micro side: one buyer, one firm, one market, one factor. Table 1.1 on page 5 lays the pairs out, and the useful exercise is to read only the left-hand column — how many people work at this firm, whether this price rose. Those are the questions you will be answering in June.

Watch out. Do not carry a micro result up to the whole economy. One farmer with a bumper crop earns more; every farmer with a bumper crop can earn less, because the price falls. The reasoning that is correct for one market is not automatically correct for all of them at once — which is why macroeconomics is a separate course.

Textbook

Table 1.1. Each row puts a micro question beside its macro counterpart. It is the clearest half-page in the module.

Numeric

Check yourself

14.1 Which of these is a macro question? (a) Should I buy this car? (b) Has the overall price level risen this year? (c) Why does this café charge more than that one?

Show answer

(b). "Overall" is the giveaway — it is a question about the whole economy. The other two are about one person and one market.

14.2 Give an example where what is true for one person is not true for everyone at once.

Show answer

A bumper harvest: one farmer with a big crop sells more and earns more; every farmer with a big crop pushes the price down and they can all earn less. Standing at a concert works as well.

15

Economic aggregates

○ Background
Definition

Economic measures that summarise data across many different markets.

Textbook page 5.

An aggregate is many markets added into one number: aggregate output, aggregate demand, the aggregate price level. The word does not appear once in the AP Microeconomics framework, and you will not be asked for it. It is here because it is the cleanest way to see what this course is not doing.

What is worth a minute now is what aggregation hides. One national price index can be steady while some prices double and others halve. Microeconomics is the study of those individual prices — the ones the aggregate averages away — which is why we never aggregate in this course.

Watch out. An average may describe nobody. Output per person is a number divided by a population, not a description of how any particular person lives.

Textbook

The definition. "Summarize data across many different markets" — the word summarize is doing the work.

Numeric

+2% overall. Rents rise 12% and electronics fall 8%. A single index of +2% describes neither market, and the two stories it hides are exactly the two a microeconomist would want to explain.

Illustrative

One average grade. A school reporting a single average across every subject. Useful for a trend, useless for deciding which department needs help.

How it connects. Aggregates are what makes macroeconomics (entry 14) macro. Every model in this course runs on a single market instead.

Check yourself

15.1 A price index rises 2% while rents rise 12% and electronics fall 8%. What does the index hide, and why does it matter here?

Show answer

It hides two opposite movements in two separate markets. It matters because those two markets are what this course studies — the aggregate averages away precisely the thing microeconomics is about.

15.2 Name an aggregate you already know.

Show answer

The unemployment rate, the inflation rate, GDP, the overall price level — any single number summarising many markets at once. None of them is examined in this course.

16

Positive and normative economics

○ Background
Definitions

Positive economics describes the way the economy actually works.

Normative economics makes prescriptions about the way the economy should work.

Textbook pages 6–8. Not assessed in either AP economics course.

Positive statements have right answers, at least in principle. Normative statements turn on values. "The price of gasoline is rising" is positive. "The price of gasoline is too high" is normative, and no amount of data settles it.

Worth knowing so you can tell what economists are and are not claiming. Not worth revising.

Watch out. A forecast is positive, not normative. "Gas prices are expected to fall" is a claim about what will happen — it will turn out right or wrong. Uncertainty is not the same as opinion.

Textbook

Describing against prescribing. Your book's contrast is exactly this: how the economy works against how it should work.

Numeric

Three statements. "Prices are rising" — positive. "Prices are too high" — normative. "Prices are expected to fall" — positive. Only the middle one turns on values.

Illustrative

Two economists. Both agree a carbon tax would cut emissions 8% and manufacturing jobs 1%. One supports it, one opposes it. They agree on the positive question and disagree on the normative one, and no extra data will settle it.

How it connects. Models (entry 17) are the tool for answering positive questions. They cannot answer normative ones.

Check yourself

16.1 Positive or normative: "Raising the minimum wage reduces youth employment by 2%"?

Show answer

Positive. It is a claim about what happens, and evidence can support or refute it — whether or not anyone currently knows the answer.

16.2 Two economists agree on all the numbers and still disagree about a policy. Has one made a mistake?

Show answer

No. They agree on the positive question and differ on the normative one — how to weigh the gains against the losses. That is a question about values, not arithmetic.

17

Economic model

○ But read this one
Definition

A simplified representation of reality, such as a graph or an equation, used to understand a real situation.

Textbook pages 6–8.

No exam question will ask you what a model is. Read this anyway, because Units 3 to 6 are made almost entirely of models — aggregate demand and supply, the money market, loanable funds, the Phillips curve, the foreign exchange market. You will be drawing and shifting them for the rest of the year.

A model deliberately leaves things out. That is not a flaw; it is the method. The question to ask is never "is this realistic?" It is "does it keep the things that matter for the question I am asking?"

Watch out. Do not throw a model out because one assumption is unrealistic. Every assumption is unrealistic — that is what makes it an assumption. Ask whether it changes the answer.

Textbook

The wind tunnel. The Wright brothers built one a year after their first glider flight. Testing a wing in a box of moving air is cheaper and safer than building an aircraft and hoping.

Numeric

Module 3. The curve you draw in two modules' time assumes an economy producing exactly two goods. None does — and that assumption is what turns a trade-off into a line with a measurable slope.

Illustrative

This course. The supply-and-demand diagram represents every buyer and every seller of a good with two lines. It is absurd as a description, and it is how you will answer most of Units 2 to 6.

How it connects. Models answer positive questions (entry 16), not normative ones.

Check yourself

17.1 Why is a wind tunnel a good description of what an economic model does?

Show answer

It reproduces the one force you care about — air over a wing — and leaves out everything else about flying. Cheap, safe, and you can change one thing at a time.

17.2 A classmate says the supply-and-demand model is useless because no market has only two curves. What do you say?

Show answer

That the simplification is the point. The question is not whether it looks like a real market but whether leaving things out changes the answer to the question being asked. Usually it does not.

2. A student says: "The government spent nothing on flood defences this year, so the flood defences cost nothing." Explain why this is wrong, using the idea of opportunity cost.

Show the rubric

The five things to get right from Module 1

  1. Scarcity means competing uses — not rare, not running out. And most resources are scarce, not all: non-rival things like knowledge are the exception.
  2. Opportunity cost is the single best alternative given up, and it includes your time. This is the one idea here your exam asks you to calculate.
  3. Money is not capital. Capital is manufactured things used to produce other things.
  4. Choosing is refusing, for governments as much as for people. "We didn't spend anything on it" does not mean it cost nothing.
  5. Mixed economy is the one term your book does not define and your framework does name. Entry 11 is the only place you will meet it — do not skip it because it is missing from the reading.
