01

Trade-off

★ Exam
Definition

You make a trade-off when you give up something in order to have something else.

Textbook page 16.

Module 1 told you resources are scarce. A trade-off is what scarcity looks like once you have to act: more of one thing means less of another. Your textbook is careful to say this holds for any economy — "whether it contains one person or millions of people."

Keep two ideas apart from the start. The trade-off is the relationship: more fish means fewer coconuts. The opportunity cost is the amount — how many coconuts per fish. You will put a number on the second one in entry 6.

Watch out. A trade-off is not a mistake and not evidence that somebody chose badly. It is what a world with limited resources is like. No amount of effort or good intention removes one.

Textbook

The castaway. If Tom puts more effort into catching fish he catches more fish — but that same time and effort cannot go on coconuts, so the trade-off is fewer coconuts.

Numeric

Two numbers are the whole trade-off. Tom can catch at most 40 fish a week, or gather at most 30 coconuts. Every fish costs him some coconuts, at a rate you will calculate exactly in entry 6.

Illustrative

A national budget. A government with a fixed budget decides between hospitals and roads. Wanting both does not make the trade-off disappear; it only decides which side of it you take.

How it connects. It comes from scarcity (Module 1). It is drawn as the production possibilities curve (entry 2) and measured by opportunity cost (entry 6).

Check yourself

1.1 Name a trade-off you faced this week, and say what was on both sides of it.

Show answer

Any real one, as long as both halves are named — “I went to practice, so I gave up two hours of revision.” Naming what you gave up is the part that matters.

1.2 Why can a country not escape trade-offs by simply working harder?

Show answer

Working harder can add resources, which shifts the curve outward — but you still have to pick a point on the new curve. More resources move the trade-off; they never remove it.

02

Production possibilities curve (PPC)

★ Exam
Definition

The production possibilities curve shows the trade-offs facing an economy that produces only two goods. It shows the maximum quantity of one good that can be produced for each possible quantity of the other.

Textbook pages 16–17, Figure 3.1.

This is the first real model in the course, and it does four jobs at once. It shows what is possible and what is not. It shows what using all your resources looks like. Its steepness gives you the opportunity cost. And when it moves, that is economic growth.

You will object that no economy makes only two goods. Correct — and that is the point. Your textbook explains why: "in a model with many goods, it would be much harder to study trade-offs, efficiency, and economic growth." Cutting the world down to two goods is exactly what turns a trade-off into a line you can measure. Everything you learn from it survives in a world of millions of goods.

One thing to notice now, because entry 5 depends on it: the curve represents what could be produced with all its resources in use. A point below it therefore means something is idle. Hold that thought.

Watch out. The top of the curve is not “what the economy produces.” Every point on the curve is equally producible. Which one gets chosen is a separate question.

Textbook

Figure 3.1. Tom can catch 40 fish if he gathers no coconuts, or gather 30 coconuts if he catches no fish. The curve answers questions of the form “what is the most fish Tom can catch if he also gathers 15 coconuts?”

Numeric

The same curve as a table. 0 fish → 30 coconuts · 20 fish → 15 · 28 fish → 9 · 40 fish → 0. Get used to both forms; the exam supplies data either way.

Illustrative

It is not only about islands. A country choosing between capital goods and consumer goods faces the same diagram, and so does a single firm choosing between two products. Entry 13 comes back to the first of those.

How it connects. It draws the trade-off (entry 1). It has three regions (entry 3), its steepness is the opportunity cost (entry 6), and it shifts with growth (entry 10) and contraction (entry 12).

Check yourself

2.1 Using the table above, what is the most coconuts Tom can have if he catches 28 fish?

Show answer

9 coconuts. Read it straight off — the curve gives the maximum of one good for a given quantity of the other.

2.2 Why does the curve slope downward? What would an upward-sloping “PPC” mean?

Show answer

It slopes down because resources are fixed: more of one good means less of the other. An upward slope would mean you could get more of both at once — which would mean you were not on the frontier at all.

03

Feasible and not feasible

★ Exam
Definition

A production point is feasible if it lies inside or on the curve. It is not feasible if it lies outside the curve.

Textbook page 17.

Your textbook calls this "a crucial distinction," and it is the first thing to get solid, because three other ideas are defined against it. Use the explorer above: click inside the curve, on it, and beyond it, and read what the status box says each time.

One word needs care. A point outside the curve is not impossible forever — it is impossible now, with the resources and technology this economy currently has. Growth (entry 10) is precisely the event that turns an outside point into an inside one.

Watch out. This is the single most-missed idea in the module, and your textbook has a true/false question built to catch it. Points inside the curve are wasteful but possible. Points outside are impossible. Students reverse these constantly.

Textbook

Points C and D. C — 20 fish and 9 coconuts — is feasible, because at 20 fish Tom could manage as many as 15 coconuts, so 9 is well within reach. D — 40 fish and 30 coconuts — is not feasible at all.

Numeric

Test any bundle against the curve. Tom’s curve is coconuts = 30 − ¾ × fish. At 24 fish it allows 30 − 18 = 12 coconuts. So (24, 14) is not feasible; (24, 10) is feasible but wasteful; (24, 12) is on the curve.

Illustrative

A hospital. With a fixed number of theatres and surgeons it can do 12 knee operations in a day, or 8 heart operations, or a mix. Twelve of each is not an ambitious target — it is arithmetically impossible until the hospital gets more of something.

How it connects. These are the regions of the PPC (entry 2). On the curve means efficient in production (entry 4); inside it means resources are underutilized (entry 5). Growth (entry 10) turns not-feasible points into feasible ones.

Check yourself

3.1 Tom’s curve is coconuts = 30 − ¾ × fish. Is 24 fish and 14 coconuts feasible? Show your working.

Show answer

At 24 fish the curve allows 30 − ¾(24) = 30 − 18 = 12 coconuts. The bundle asks for 14, which is more than 12, so it is not feasible.

3.2 True or false: “points inside the curve are efficient and points outside are inefficient.”

Show answer

False, and both halves are backwards. Points inside are possible but wasteful. Points outside are not possible at all with current resources and technology.

04

Efficient in production

★ Exam
Definition

An economy is efficient if there is no way to make anyone better off without making at least one person worse off. An economy producing on its PPC is efficient in production — it cannot get more of one good without giving up some of the other.

Textbook pages 17–18.

“Efficient in production” and “on the curve” are the same statement. Say that to yourself until it feels obvious, because a large share of the questions in this module are testing it.

Your textbook’s example is a good one. A class meets in a room too small for it while a bigger room stands empty during the same period. Nothing about the school’s resources changed — they are simply badly arranged. Somebody could be made better off and nobody worse off, so the situation is inefficient.

Watch out. An efficient economy is not automatically a good one, and certainly not a fair one. Efficiency says nothing about which mix was chosen or who gets what. Tom gathering 30 coconuts and catching no fish at all is perfectly efficient in production — and very hungry.

Textbook

The crowded classroom. Also Tom at point A: “the 15 coconuts he gathers are the maximum quantity he can get given that he has chosen to catch 20 fish.” Nothing is wasted, so A is efficient in production.

Numeric

On the curve, nothing is free. A (20 fish, 15 coconuts) and B (28, 9) are both on the curve, so both are efficient. Moving from A to B gains 8 fish and costs 6 coconuts. That is a real trade — and that is the test.

Illustrative

A delivery firm. It reorganises its routes and delivers 40 more parcels a day with the same vans, drivers and fuel. Before, it was inside its curve; now it is on it. It gained nothing new — it stopped wasting what it had.

How it connects. It means being on the PPC (entry 2). Its opposite is underutilized resources (entry 5). A different and broader idea, allocative efficiency (entry 14), is background in this course.

Check yourself

4.1 You are producing on the curve and want more fish. What must happen, and why?

Show answer

You must give up some coconuts. On the curve there is no spare capacity, so more of one good can only come from less of the other.

4.2 Is a point on the curve where the economy produces only one good still efficient in production?

Show answer

Yes. An end point is still on the curve, so nothing is wasted. It may be a terrible choice — but that is a question about the mix, not about productive efficiency.

05

Underutilized resources

★ Exam
Definition

Resources are underutilized when production sits at a point inside the PPC: some resources are available and are not being used, so more of both goods could be had at no cost.

Textbook page 18. Topic 1.3, MKT-1.C.2.

⚑ The word your book uses is not the word your framework uses. MKT-1.C.2 lists what the PPC illustrates: “scarcity, opportunity cost, efficiency, underutilized resources, and economic growth or contraction.” Underutilized is the examinable term. Krugman writes about unemployment and full employment here because the same chapter serves AP Macroeconomics, where those are topics in their own right. In this course they are not — write underutilized resources and you are always safe.

The textbook sentence, with that translation in mind:

“The production possibilities curve shows the amount that can possibly be produced if all resources are fully employed. In other words, changes in unemployment move the economy closer to, or further away from, the production possibilities curve. But the curve itself is determined by what would be possible if there were full employment in the economy.”

Read it as a statement about utilisation and it is exactly right for this course: the frontier assumes every resource is being used, so anything that leaves resources idle puts production below it. The further inside the point, the more is idle.

The test is arithmetic, not vocabulary. At a point inside the curve you can get more of one good without giving up any of the other — the only place in this whole course where that is true.

Watch out. Do not confuse inside with outside. Inside the curve = wasteful but possible. Outside = impossible. And do not confuse a point inside the curve with the curve itself moving — that second thing is contraction, entry 12.

Textbook

Point C. Producing 20 fish and 9 coconuts “would definitely not be efficient in production… it is missing the opportunity to produce more of both goods.”

Numeric

The free gain. At 20 fish the curve allows 15 coconuts. At C, Tom has 9. Six coconuts are available for free — no fish given up at all. That free gain is the fingerprint of underutilisation, and it can never happen on the curve.

Illustrative

A half-empty factory. A plant runs one shift instead of three. The machines exist, the workers exist, the raw materials exist. Nothing was destroyed and nothing became scarcer — it is simply not being used. Production has moved inside the curve, and the curve has not moved at all.

How it connects. It means being inside the PPC (entry 2), the opposite of efficiency in production (entry 4). Distinguish it sharply from contraction (entry 12), where the curve itself moves. The same distinction — a movement along against a shift of the whole line — is the single most-tested idea of Unit 2.

Check yourself

5.1 How can you tell, just by looking at a point, that resources are underutilized?

Show answer

If you could get more of one good without giving up any of the other, resources are idle. On the curve that is impossible; inside it, it is always possible.

5.2 A hurricane destroys a quarter of a country’s factories. Is that underutilisation, or something else?

Show answer

Something else. Underutilisation leaves the curve where it is and moves production inside it. Destroyed factories move the curve itself inward — a contraction (entry 12).

06

Opportunity cost on a PPC

★ Exam
Definition

On a PPC, the opportunity cost of one more unit of the good on the horizontal axis is the quantity of the other good given up. On a straight-line PPC that is the slope.

Textbook page 19.

This is the calculation the exam actually asks for — the learning objective says “calculate” in so many words — and your textbook states the key fact once, almost in passing, so it is easy to read past:

The slope of a straight-line PPC is the opportunity cost — the cost of the horizontal good, measured in the vertical good.

The method never changes: pick two points, find the change in each good, divide. Then say what the answer means in words — “one fish costs three-quarters of a coconut” — because the exam wants units and this is where marks are lost.

Always do it both ways. If one fish costs ¾ of a coconut, then one coconut costs 4/3 of a fish. They are reciprocals. Module 4 leans on this constantly, so get fluent now.

Watch out. Flipping the ratio is the most common error in the module. Anchor yourself with one question: cost of what, in what? The cost of the horizontal good, measured in the vertical good. Write the units every time and you will not go wrong.

Textbook

A to B. Tom “will produce 8 more fish but 6 fewer coconuts. So the opportunity cost of those 8 fish is the 6 coconuts not gathered. Since 8 extra fish have an opportunity cost of 6 coconuts, 1 fish has an opportunity cost of 6/8 = 3/4 of a coconut.”

Numeric

Same answer three times. A→B: 6 ÷ 8 = ¾ coconut per fish. B→the axis: 9 ÷ 12 = ¾. The whole curve: 30 ÷ 40 = ¾. That sameness is what “straight line” means. And in reverse, one coconut costs 40 ÷ 30 = 4/3 of a fish.

Illustrative

Say it the natural way round. A bakery’s curve runs from 200 loaves to 50 cakes. One loaf costs ¼ of a cake; one cake costs 4 loaves. Notice how much more natural the second sounds — which is exactly why you should always work out both.

How it connects. It measures the trade-off (entry 1) and is read off the PPC (entry 2) as its slope. Whether it stays the same depends on the curve’s shape — entries 7, 8 and 9. In Module 4 it becomes the definition of comparative advantage.

Check yourself

6.1 A PPC runs from 60 pizzas to 20 cakes. What is the opportunity cost of one cake? And of one pizza?

Show answer

One cake costs 60 ÷ 20 = 3 pizzas. One pizza costs 20 ÷ 60 = 1/3 of a cake. Always state the units.

6.2 A student writes “the opportunity cost is 0.75.” Why is that incomplete?

Show answer

It has no units and does not say the cost of what, in what. The full answer is “one fish costs 0.75 of a coconut.”

07

Constant opportunity cost

★ Exam
Definition

Opportunity cost is constant when one more unit of a good always costs the same amount of the other good. The PPC is then a straight line.

Textbook page 19.

The straight line is an assumption, and your textbook says so plainly: "whenever we assume that the opportunity cost of an additional unit of a good doesn't change regardless of the output mix, the production possibilities curve is a straight line."

What it means in real terms is that your resources are equally good at both jobs, so swapping them over always trades at the same rate, in either direction.

Switch the explorer at the top to each of the three shapes in turn. One question identifies any of them: moving right along the curve, does it get steeper, stay straight, or get flatter?

Watch out. Do not assume every PPC is straight just because the first one you met was. Your textbook says that in reality “opportunity costs are typically increasing.” A curved PPC in a question is checking whether you noticed.

Textbook

Figure 3.1. It has “a constant slope of −3/4, implying that Tom faces a constant opportunity cost per fish equal to 3/4 of a coconut.”

Numeric

Equal steps cost equal amounts. 0→10 fish costs 7.5 coconuts. 10→20 costs 7.5. 20→30 costs 7.5. 30→40 costs 7.5. That is the test.

Illustrative

A print shop. Its workers make posters or flyers and everyone is equally quick at both. Moving one worker across always trades the same numbers, whichever direction you move them. Straight line.

How it connects. It is one possible shape of the PPC (entry 2) and makes the opportunity cost (entry 6) the same everywhere. Compare entry 8 and entry 9. Module 4 assumes this case throughout.

Check yourself

7.1 Looking at the explorer set to Constant, how can you tell the cost never changes without doing arithmetic?

Show answer

The curve is a straight line, and a straight line has the same steepness everywhere — so one more fish always costs the same number of coconuts.

7.2 What does a constant opportunity cost tell you about how well suited your resources are to the two goods?

Show answer

That they are equally suited to both, so switching them across always trades at the same rate.

08

Increasing opportunity cost

★ Exam
Definition

Opportunity cost is increasing when each extra unit of a good costs more of the other good than the last one did. The PPC is bowed outward — away from the origin.

Textbook pages 19–20, Figure 3.2.

The reason behind the shape is the part worth remembering, because it turns an arbitrary curve into something obvious. When you make only a little of a good, you use the resources best suited to it, so you sacrifice very little. Push further and you must bring in resources that are bad at that job and good at the other — so every extra unit costs more.

Your textbook’s farming example makes it concrete. Grow a little corn and you plant it where the soil suits corn and not wheat, giving up almost no wheat. Grow a great deal of corn and you are planting it on land that was excellent for wheat.

This is the realistic case, and your textbook says so: “economists believe that in reality, opportunity costs are typically increasing.”

Watch out. “Bowed outward” is not “shifted outward.” Bowed is about the shape — how costs behave along the curve. Shifted is about movement — the whole curve moving because capacity changed. Both contain the word outward and they are unrelated.

Textbook

Figure 3.2. “To go from producing zero fish to producing 20 fish, he has to give up 5 coconuts… But to increase his fish production from 20 to 40 — that is, to produce an additional 20 fish — he must give up 25 more coconuts, a much higher opportunity cost.”

Numeric

The same step, priced twice. First 20 fish: 5 coconuts, so ¼ coconut each. Next 20 fish: 25 coconuts, so 1¼ coconuts each. Five times as expensive for exactly the same increase in fish.

Illustrative

Farmland to biofuel. The first fields a country converts are the ones poor for food anyway. Keep going and it is converting prime wheat land, so every extra litre of biofuel costs progressively more food.

How it connects. It is one possible shape of the PPC (entry 2). It makes the opportunity cost (entry 6) different at every point, so you must always say over which interval you measured it. Compare entry 7 and entry 9.

Check yourself

8.1 A PPC is bowed outward. Moving right, does it get steeper or flatter, and what does that mean about cost?

Show answer

Steeper. A steeper curve means each extra unit of the horizontal good costs more of the vertical good — increasing opportunity cost.

8.2 Why does moving resources into a good you already produce a lot of tend to cost more than the first move did?

Show answer

Because you have already used the resources best at that good. To make more you must bring in resources that are poor at it and good at the other one, so you give up more of the other good each time.

09

Decreasing opportunity cost

★ ⚑
Definition

Opportunity cost is decreasing when each extra unit of a good costs less of the other good than the last one did. The PPC is bowed inward — toward the origin.

Not in your textbook.

Not in your textbook. The phrase “decreasing opportunity cost” appears zero times in all 927 pages of your textbook, and no bowed-inward curve is ever drawn. The framework requires all three shapes, so this one is written for you here.

The logic is entry 8 run backwards. If your resources get better suited to a good the more of it you make, then each extra unit costs less of the other good. The curve bends toward the origin, and it gets flatter as you move right.

Be clear about what you are being asked. This case is unusual in the real world — it needs specialisation or learning-by-doing, which are exactly what the resource argument in entry 8 rules out. On the exam it appears as a shape-recognition question, not as a claim about how economies usually behave.

The whole skill in one question. Move right along the curve. Getting steeper means increasing cost. Straight means constant. Getting flatter means decreasing. Set the explorer at the top to each shape in turn and watch the opportunity-cost readout change — or not.

Watch out. A bowed-inward PPC is not inefficient, even though it sits below the straight line. Every point on any PPC is efficient in production. The bow describes the pattern of costs, not how well the economy is doing.

Textbook

There isn't one. The phrase does not occur anywhere in the book, and no bowed-inward curve is drawn. That is why this entry exists.

Numeric

The mirror image of entry 8. First 20 fish: about 21 coconuts, so roughly 1 coconut each. Next 20 fish: about 9 coconuts, under ½ each. Same axes, same end points, opposite pattern — and telling those two apart is exactly what the exam tests.

Illustrative

Why it could happen. A software company splits engineers between two products. As more move onto one product they specialise, share tools and reuse code — so each additional engineer moved costs less output on the other product than the one before.

How it connects. It is the third possible shape of the PPC (entry 2), making the opportunity cost (entry 6) fall as you produce more of the horizontal good. Compare entry 7 and entry 8.

Check yourself

9.1 Sketch a bowed-inward PPC with fish on the horizontal axis. Where is a fish expensive, and where is it cheap?

Show answer

The curve is steep on the left and flat on the right. Fish are expensive where it is steep (few fish produced) and cheap where it is flat (many fish produced).

9.2 Your friend says a bowed-inward PPC shows an inefficient economy because it lies below the straight line. Why is that wrong?

Show answer

Every point on any PPC is efficient in production — nothing is wasted anywhere on it. The bow describes how costs change as you move along the curve, not how well the economy performs.

10

Economic growth

★ Exam
Definition

Economic growth is an expansion of what the economy is able to produce — it can produce more of everything. On the diagram it is an outward shift of the whole PPC.

Textbook pages 20–21, Figure 3.3.

The distinction to nail here will follow you all year: moving along the curve versus shifting the curve.

  • Along the curve — you changed the mix of what you produce. Resources unchanged.
  • Shifting the curve — what is possible changed. That is growth.

Two causes, and you need both: more resources (labour, land, capital, entrepreneurship), or better technology. Press Growth in the explorer to see the whole frontier move out.

One subtlety worth keeping. Growth does not force the economy to produce more of everything, only to be able to. As your textbook says, "what the economy actually produces depends on the choices people make." Tom might get a fishing net and live on fish alone. His economy still grew.

Watch out. Not every increase in output is growth. Moving from inside the curve onto it raises output with no growth at all — that is just putting underutilized resources back to work (entry 5). Growth means the frontier itself moved.

Textbook

Figure 3.3. If Tom starts at A with 20 fish and 25 coconuts, growth means “he could move to point E (25 fish and 30 coconuts). Point E lies outside the original curve.” The cause in the story is concrete — he finds a fishing net washed up on the beach.

Numeric

A 40% rise in resources. The curve moves from 40 fish / 30 coconuts to 56 / 42. The bundle 28 fish and 21 coconuts was impossible before and is available now. The slope is unchanged at −¾, because both ends grew in the same proportion.

Illustrative

A hospital opens a new wing. It can now do more knee operations and more heart operations than before. It did not reorganise anything; its frontier moved out.

How it connects. It shifts the PPC (entry 2) outward. Its causes are more resources or better technology (entry 11). It turns not-feasible points (entry 3) into feasible ones. Its opposite is contraction (entry 12), and it is not the same as putting underutilized resources (entry 5) back to work. Capital goods (entry 13) are how a country buys it.

Check yourself

10.1 An economy moves from a point inside its curve to a point on it. Has it grown?

Show answer

No. Its frontier did not move — it simply stopped wasting resources. Growth means the curve itself shifts outward.

10.2 Name the two causes of growth and give a real example of each.

Show answer

More resources — a rising population, or discovering oil. Better technology — a new farming method, or a faster machine.

11

Technology

★ Exam
Definition

Technology is the technical means for producing goods and services.

Textbook page 21.

Technology is the second cause of growth, and it works differently from the first. More resources means having more stuff. Better technology means getting more out of the stuff you already have. The definition covers methods and know-how, not just machines.

Here is the subtle bit, and it is a favourite of exam writers. Technology that helps only one good tilts the curve rather than shifting all of it. Your textbook explains why: "a fishing hook will be of no use if Tom produces nothing but coconuts. So the point on the PPC that represents the number of coconuts that can be produced if there is no fishing will not change."

Press Fishing hook in the explorer. Watch the coconut end stay exactly where it is while the fish end moves out, and watch the opportunity cost readout change as a result.

Watch out. Do not assume every technological improvement shifts the whole curve outward. Always ask: which good does this help? If it helps only one, the other end of the curve stays exactly where it was.

Textbook

A hook, or a wagon. Either “would shift his production possibilities curve outward. However, the shift would not be a simple outward expansion of every point along the PPC.”

Numeric

The pivot. A hook doubles Tom’s fishing and does nothing for coconuts: 40 fish → 80, coconuts stay at 30. The slope changes from −¾ to −⅜. One fish now costs only 3/8 of a coconut.

Illustrative

A robotic surgical system. Used only for knee operations, it raises knee capacity sharply and leaves heart capacity exactly as it was. On the graph, one end moves and the other does not.

How it connects. It is one of the two causes of economic growth (entry 10) and shifts — or pivots — the PPC (entry 2). Losing it causes contraction (entry 12). It changes the opportunity cost (entry 6) whenever it pivots the curve.

Check yourself

11.1 A new fertiliser doubles wheat yields and does nothing for cars. Describe what happens to a wheat-and-cars PPC.

Show answer

The wheat end moves out; the cars end stays exactly where it was. The curve pivots around the cars intercept and becomes flatter in the wheat direction.

11.2 What is the difference between “more resources” and “better technology” as causes of growth?

Show answer

More resources means a larger quantity of inputs. Better technology means more output from the same inputs. Both shift the curve outward, but technology helping only one good pivots it instead of moving all of it.

12

Contraction

★ ⚑
Definition

A contraction is an inward shift of the PPC — the economy has become smaller and can now produce less than before. It is caused by losing resources or losing technology.

One sentence on textbook page 21, and no diagram.

Not in your textbook. The word “contraction” appears zero times in this module — your textbook gives the idea one sentence and draws no diagram, against a full section and a figure for growth. The framework treats growth and contraction as equally important, so the missing half is supplied here.

It is entry 10 in reverse. Lose resources or lose technology and the frontier moves in. Your textbook names the causes in its single sentence: “war or a natural disaster.”

The point to really understand: after a contraction, a bundle the economy used to be able to produce is now impossible. Press Contraction in the explorer with a point sitting on the old curve and watch the status box flip from efficient to unattainable. That is completely different from underutilisation (entry 5), where the bundle was always available and simply was not being produced.

Watch out. Do not use a point inside the curve to show a contraction. Losing resources moves the curve. Leaving resources idle moves the production point. Keep those apart and you will get every shift-versus-movement question right — here, and in every supply-and-demand question in Unit 2.

Textbook

The one sentence. “This could happen if the economy loses resources or technology (for example, if it experiences war or a natural disaster).” That is the entire treatment.

Numeric

A storm takes 30%. The curve moves from 40 fish / 30 coconuts to 28 / 21. Point A (20 fish, 15 coconuts) used to be on the curve and is now outside the new one. The best Tom can manage at 20 fish is now 6 coconuts.

Illustrative

Two factories, two different stories. One is flooded and its machines destroyed — that is a contraction, the curve moved in. Another idles a shift in a slow month — that is underutilisation, the curve is unchanged and the production point moved inside. Rebuilding fixes the first; simply using what you have fixes the second.

How it connects. It shifts the PPC (entry 2) inward, caused by losing resources or technology (entry 11). It is the opposite of economic growth (entry 10) and must not be confused with underutilized resources (entry 5). Both growth and contraction are named in MKT-1.C.2.

Check yourself

12.1 An earthquake destroys a quarter of a country’s factories. Describe and label what happens to its PPC.

Show answer

The whole curve shifts inward, toward the origin. Label the original curve and the new one, and label both axes.

12.2 Explain the difference between a contraction and underutilized resources, using the word “curve” in each answer.

Show answer

A contraction means the curve itself moves inward, so some bundles become impossible. Underutilisation means the curve stays where it is and the production point sits inside it, so nothing has become impossible — it is just not being produced.

13

Capital goods and future growth

★ Exam
Definition

Capital goods are manufactured goods used to produce other goods. Producing more of them today means fewer consumer goods today — and a further-out PPC tomorrow.

Textbook page 22, the AP Review graph and MCQ 5.

Everything so far used fish and coconuts. Your textbook’s own review questions switch to a different pair, and it is the pair the exam is most likely to hand you: capital goods against consumer goods.

The reason is that this version of the diagram has a second story built into it. Consumer goods are enjoyed now and then gone. Capital goods — machines, factories, tools — are themselves resources, so producing more of them today increases what the economy can produce in every future year. Your textbook puts it plainly in MCQ 5: “since capital goods are a resource, an increase in the production of capital goods today will increase the economy’s production possibilities in the future.”

So a country choosing a point far up the capital-goods axis is choosing less consumption now for a bigger curve later. This is also the cleanest illustration of MKT-1.C.4: the PPC shifts when the factors of production change, and capital is a factor of production you can choose to make more of.

Watch out. This does not mean capital goods are “better” than consumer goods. An economy that produced nothing but capital goods would have nothing to eat. It is a trade-off between present and future consumption, not a ranking.

Textbook

MCQ 5. Given a capital-goods/consumer-goods PPC, you are asked which point today produces the largest outward shift in the future. The answer is the point with the most capital goods — and the reasoning, not the letter, is what to take away.

Numeric

Two countries, same curve. Both can make 100 machines or 400 meals. One chooses 20 machines and 320 meals; the other 60 machines and 160 meals. Ten years later the second country’s curve is much further out — and its people ate far less along the way.

Illustrative

A farmer choosing. Eat all the grain this winter, or keep some back as seed. Seed is capital: it is grain not consumed now, and it is why there is a harvest next year.

How it connects. It is the trade-off (entry 1) again, drawn on the same PPC (entry 2), with time as the second axis. Choosing more capital goods today causes economic growth (entry 10) tomorrow. Capital is also one of the three resources named by MKT-1.A.2, which is what makes this work.

Check yourself

13.1 Why does producing more capital goods today shift tomorrow’s PPC outward, when producing more consumer goods does not?

Show answer

Because capital goods are themselves resources — machines and factories are used to make other things. Consumer goods are used up. Adding to the stock of resources is exactly what moves the frontier.

13.2 Is a country producing at the maximum-capital-goods end of its PPC making the best choice?

Show answer

Not necessarily. It would have the fastest growth and nothing to consume. The right point balances consumption now against capacity later — which is a choice about values, not a fact the diagram settles.

14

Efficient in allocation

★ Exam
Definition

An economy is efficient in allocation when it produces the mix of goods that makes people as well off as possible — not merely the largest quantity.

Textbook page 18. Returns as Topic 3.7, PRD-3.A.5.

Entry 4 said being on the curve means nothing is wasted. Your textbook adds that this is only half of being efficient — the economy must also make the right things.

Points A and B are both on the curve, so neither wastes anything. But if Tom would much rather have 28 fish and 9 coconuts than 20 fish and 15, then A is inefficient for the economy as a whole, because you could make him better off without making anyone worse off.

⚑ This one is not background — it is the destination. Allocative efficiency is where a large part of this course is heading. PRD-3.A.5 makes it precise: at a competitive equilibrium the price “equals both the private marginal benefit received by the last unit consumed and the private marginal cost incurred to produce the last unit, thus achieving allocative efficiency.” PRD-3.A.9 then states that a perfectly competitive market in long-run equilibrium is “allocatively and productively efficient.”

You meet it here as a shape on a curve. In Unit 3 it becomes a condition you can check, in Unit 4 the thing monopoly destroys, and in Unit 6 the thing externalities break.

Watch out. On a PPC question, “efficient” almost always means on the curve — efficient in production. Allocative efficiency needs information the PPC does not contain: what people actually want. Do not claim a point is allocatively efficient from the diagram alone.

Textbook

Points A and B. Both productively efficient. If Tom prefers B, then A is allocatively inefficient even though nothing is being wasted.

Illustrative

Producing hard, producing the wrong thing. A country runs its factories flat out making goods nobody wants. Productively efficient; allocatively hopeless.

Illustrative

A school timetable. Every room and every teaching hour is used, but the school offers six sections of Latin and none of computer science. Nothing is idle. The mix is wrong.

How it connects. It is broader than efficiency in production (entry 4) — it adds the question of which point on the curve to pick. Answering that question is what marginal benefit and marginal cost (Module 1, entries 6 and 7) are for, and it is the whole argument of Topic 3.7.

Check yourself

14.1 Can an economy be efficient in production and inefficient in allocation at the same time?

Show answer

Yes. Being on the curve means nothing is wasted, but the particular mix chosen may not be the one people most want.

14.2 In this course, a question asks “is point B efficient?” What is it asking?

Show answer

Whether point B lies on the curve — efficient in production, nothing wasted. The diagram cannot tell you whether the mix at B is the one people want, so it cannot settle allocative efficiency.

The five things to get right from Module 3

  1. Label both axes and the curve. Every time. These are free marks and they are the ones most often dropped.
  2. Inside = wasteful but possible. Outside = impossible. Never the other way round.
  3. Say “underutilized resources”, not “unemployment”. Your book uses the second because it also serves the macro course. MKT-1.C.2 uses the first.
  4. The slope of a straight PPC is the opportunity cost — and always state it both ways round, with units.
  5. Moving along the curve is not the same as shifting the curve. A shift means resources or technology changed. This distinction returns in every later unit.
