Long-Run Costs and Economies of Scale

Fifteen terms about the long run, when a firm can choose its plant. The lowest cost at each output makes the long-run average total cost curve; its slope tells you about economies and diseconomies of scale; and the point where it bottoms out decides how many firms a market can hold.

Krugman pp. 559–566· 15 terms·Topic 3.3·2 periods

How to use this guide

Entries 1 to 5 build the long-run cost curve from short-run ones; entries 6 to 8 are economies and diseconomies of scale; entries 9 to 11 are returns to scale; entries 12 to 14 are the bottom of the curve and what it means for a market; entry 15 is sunk cost.

The one picture. Long-run average total cost falls (economies of scale), flattens (efficient scale) and rises (diseconomies of scale). Every short-run curve sits on or above it. The first point of the flat bottom is the minimum efficient scale.

⚑ One thing to translate. Your textbook says returns to scale for inputs and output and economies of scale for cost, and keeps them apart. Your exam sometimes calls the flat part of the long-run cost curve constant returns to scale, and its bottom efficient scale.

The fifteen terms

01

Long run

★ Exam
Definition

The period in which a firm can change every input — its building and machines as well as its workers — so every cost is variable. Nothing is fixed.

Textbook page 559.

In the short run Selena's equipment is fixed: she can hire more hands or buy more tomatoes, but she cannot change how much kitchen she has. Given time, she can buy more equipment or sell some. Then her “fixed” cost becomes a choice, like any other cost.

So the long run is not a number of months. It is long enough to change every input: a few days for a food truck that can rent a second truck, years for a steel mill that must build a new furnace.

Watch out. There is no fixed cost in the long run, and so no average fixed cost. Every cost the firm has, it can change.

1

A bakery deciding whether to buy a second oven. A long-run decision: the oven is an input it can now change.

2

The same bakery next week, with the oven installed. Back in a short run: the oven is fixed again.

3

A food truck that rents a second truck for the summer. Its long run arrived in days.

How it connects. In the long run the firm chooses its fixed cost (entry 2); returns to scale (entry 9) is the long-run result of growing every input; and money already spent is a sunk cost (entry 15).

Check yourself

1.1 Which time period is always the long run: a month, a year, or five years?

Show answer

None of them. The long run is defined by which inputs can change, not by a length of time.

1.2 Why is there no average fixed cost in the long run?

Show answer

Because no cost is fixed: the firm can change every input, so every cost is variable.

02

Choosing the fixed cost

★ Exam
Definition

In the long run a firm chooses its plant. A bigger plant has a higher fixed cost but a lower variable cost at each output; which plant is cheaper per unit depends on how much the firm plans to produce.

Textbook pages 559–560, Figure 56.1.

Selena can keep $108 of equipment or double it to $216. More equipment makes each worker more productive, so variable cost halves, from 12Q² to 6Q²:

At 3 cases the small plant costs $72 a case and the large one $90: too few cases to spread the extra $108 over. At 9 cases it is the other way round: $120 against $78. Up to 4 cases the small plant is cheaper; from 5 cases, the large one.

Watch out. The bigger plant is not always better. It wins only at outputs large enough to spread its larger fixed cost.

1

Selena plans 3 cases a day. Keep the $108 plant: $72 a case against $90.

2

Selena plans 9 cases a day. Buy the equipment: $78 a case against $120.

3

A city where it rarely snows. It keeps a small snowplow fleet: a low fixed cost for the little snow it expects.

How it connects. It is a decision for the long run (entry 1); each plant has its own short-run ATC curve (entry 3); the lowest of them at each output is long-run average total cost (entry 4); and once the equipment is bought and cannot be resold, its cost is sunk (entry 15).

Check yourself

2.1 Using Selena's numbers, which plant should she choose to make 6 cases a day, and what does a case cost?

Show answer

The $216 plant: ($216 + $216) ÷ 6 = $72 a case, against ($108 + $432) ÷ 6 = $90 with the small plant.

2.2 Why does the large plant cost more per case at low output?

Show answer

Its fixed cost is twice as high, and at low output there are too few cases to spread it over.

03

Short-run average total cost

★ Exam
Definition

Average total cost for one given plant, with its fixed cost already chosen. Each plant has its own U-shaped short-run ATC curve.

Textbook pages 560–561.

Everything in Module 55 was short-run cost: Selena's ATC with $108 of equipment is one curve, her ATC with $216 is another. Krugman now calls each of them a short-run average total cost curve, to show that its fixed cost is given.

A firm with many possible plant sizes has a whole family of short-run ATC curves, one for each plant. At any moment it is on exactly one of them: the one for the plant it has.

Watch out. A change in output with the plant unchanged is a movement along the same short-run curve. Changing the plant moves the firm to a different curve.

1

Selena with $108 of equipment. One short-run ATC curve, lowest at 3 cases, $72.

2

Selena with $216 of equipment. Another, lowest at 6 cases, $72.

3

A plant built for 9 thousand bicycles. Plant C in the second diagram: cheap near 8 or 9 thousand, dear at 5.

How it connects. Each curve comes from choosing the fixed cost (entry 2); together they make long-run average total cost (entry 4); and a firm moves (entry 5) along one before changing it.

Check yourself

3.1 Selena has the $108 plant and makes 5 cases instead of 3. Has she changed short-run curve?

Show answer

No. She has moved along the same curve, from $72 to $81.60 a case.

3.2 How many short-run ATC curves can a firm be on at one time?

Show answer

One: the curve for the plant it actually has.

04

Long-run average total cost

★ Exam
Definition

The lowest average total cost of producing each output when the firm can choose its plant. The LRATC curve touches each short-run ATC curve at one output and lies below or on all of them.

Textbook pages 560–561, Figure 56.2.

For every output, ask: which plant makes it most cheaply? Join those cheapest points and you have the long-run average total cost curve. With Selena's two plants it is the lower of her two rows: the $108 plant up to 4 cases, the $216 plant after that.

With many plant sizes the curve becomes smooth. In the second diagram the dark curve is LRATC for Ridgeline Cycles' industry; the dashed curves are three of the plants under it. Each plant touches LRATC at one output and is above it everywhere else.

Watch out. LRATC is not the lowest point of each short-run curve joined up. It is the lowest short-run cost at each output, which is why each short-run curve touches it only once.

1

Selena at 3 cases. The cheapest plant is the $108 one: the long-run cost of 3 cases is $72.

2

Selena at 9 cases. The $216 plant: the long-run cost of 9 cases is $78.

3

Ridgeline at 2 thousand bicycles. LRATC is $280, on plant A's curve.

How it connects. It is built from short-run ATC curves (entry 3) by choosing the fixed cost (entry 2); the firm reaches it in the long run (entry 5); its falling part is economies of scale (entry 6), its rising part diseconomies of scale (entry 7); and its lowest point starts at the minimum efficient scale (entry 13).

Check yourself

4.1 A firm has two possible plants. At 10 units, plant X costs $14 a unit and plant Y $11. What is long-run average total cost at 10 units?

Show answer

$11: the lower of the two, because in the long run the firm can choose plant Y.

4.2 Can a short-run ATC curve ever lie below LRATC?

Show answer

No. LRATC is the lowest cost of any plant at each output, so every short-run curve is on or above it.

05

Moving from the short run to the long run

★ Exam
Definition

When output changes, a firm first moves along its current short-run curve. If the change is expected to last, it later changes its plant and moves onto LRATC, where cost per unit is lower.

Textbook pages 561–562, Figure 56.2.

Suppose Selena chose the plant that is best for 6 cases, and demand then grows so that she makes 9 cases for the foreseeable future. Tomorrow she can only hire more hands for the same kitchen: she moves up her short-run curve to a high cost per case (point Y in the textbook). Given time, she buys more equipment and moves down onto LRATC (point X).

The same works in reverse. A firm whose output falls for good keeps paying for a plant that is too big until it can sell some of it.

Watch out. A temporary change in output is not a reason to change the plant. If output will fall back, the old plant is still the cheapest for the output the firm will usually produce.

1

Selena on the 6-case plant makes 9 cases tomorrow. Short run: up her plant's curve, above LRATC.

2

She expects 9 cases for years. Long run: she buys equipment and moves onto LRATC.

3

A rise in demand that lasts one month. Stay on the old plant; it is still right for normal output.

How it connects. The firm moves along a short-run ATC curve (entry 3) and then to long-run average total cost (entry 4).

Check yourself

5.1 Ridgeline built plant B, the right plant for 5.5 thousand bicycles, and now makes 8 thousand. Is its cost on or above LRATC?

Show answer

Above: plant B's cost at 8 thousand is $450 a bicycle, against an LRATC of $220.

5.2 Why does a firm that must suddenly increase output usually face rising average cost at first?

Show answer

Because in the short run its plant is fixed, so it moves up its short-run ATC curve; only in the long run can it add capacity.

06

Economies of scale

★ Exam
Definition

Long-run average total cost falls as output increases. It is the downward-sloping part of LRATC.

Textbook page 562.

Ridgeline's LRATC falls from $380 a bicycle at 1 thousand bicycles a month to $200 at 4 thousand. Over that range, a bigger firm makes each bicycle more cheaply: the industry has economies of scale.

Economies of scale give big firms a cost advantage over small ones. Where they are strong, small firms cannot survive, and the market ends up with few firms (entry 14 (entry 14)).

Watch out. Economies of scale are about cost per unit in the long run. A falling short-run AFC or AVC is not an economy of scale; nor is a fall in total cost.

1

Ridgeline from 2 to 3 thousand bicycles. LRATC falls from $280 to $220.

2

Selena from 1 to 3 cases. Her long-run cost falls from $120 to $72 a case.

3

A diamond mine. A huge set-up cost spread over more output: economies of scale.

How it connects. It is the falling part of LRATC (entry 4); it ends where the minimum efficient scale (entry 13) begins; it is caused by increasing returns to scale (entry 9) and specialization and set-up costs (entry 8); it gives way to diseconomies of scale (entry 7); and it decides how many firms fit (entry 14).

Check yourself

6.1 Which observation shows economies of scale: short-run AFC falls, long-run ATC falls, or marginal cost falls?

Show answer

Long-run ATC falls. The other two are short-run curves.

6.2 LRATC is $50 at 10,000 units and $42 at 20,000. Economies or diseconomies of scale?

Show answer

Economies of scale: long-run average total cost falls as output rises.

07

Diseconomies of scale

★ Exam
Definition

Long-run average total cost rises as output increases. It is the upward-sloping part of LRATC.

Textbook pages 562–563.

Above 7 thousand bicycles a month Ridgeline's LRATC rises: $220 at 8 thousand, $280 at 9, $380 at 10. A firm that grows this large finds each bicycle costing more to make.

Diseconomies of scale limit how large firms grow: past the point where they begin, getting bigger raises cost per unit.

Watch out. Diseconomies of scale are a long-run idea. A rising short-run ATC is caused by diminishing marginal returns to a fixed plant, not by diseconomies of scale.

1

Ridgeline from 8 to 9 thousand bicycles. LRATC rises from $220 to $280.

2

An interior design firm that takes on more projects. Its owner's expertise is spread thin; coordination costs rise.

3

Selena beyond her best plant. Her textbook says her LRATC rises above 6 cases: diseconomies of scale.

How it connects. It is the rising part of LRATC (entry 4); it follows economies of scale (entry 6); it is caused by decreasing returns to scale (entry 10) and by coordination problems (entry 8).

Check yourself

7.1 LRATC is $30 at 1 million units and $33 at 2 million. What is happening?

Show answer

Diseconomies of scale: long-run average total cost rises as output rises.

7.2 A firm's short-run ATC rises as it hires more workers for the same factory. Is that diseconomies of scale?

Show answer

No. With the factory fixed it is the short run; the rise comes from diminishing marginal returns.

08

Sources of economies and diseconomies of scale

○ Background
Definition

Economies of scale come mainly from specialization and from large set-up costs spread over more output. Diseconomies come mainly from problems of coordination and communication in very large firms.

Textbook page 563.

A bigger operation lets each worker do one narrow task well, and lets the firm use machinery that would be wasted at small scale. Some industries — car making, electricity, oil refining — need a very large plant before they produce anything, so cost per unit falls steeply as output grows.

Past some size, managing the firm gets harder: more layers of managers, slower decisions, more mistakes in passing information. Those costs rise faster than output.

Watch out. Specialization appears twice in Unit 3: in the short run it makes marginal cost fall; in the long run it is a source of economies of scale. Say which one a question is about.

1

A car maker. Enormous fixed cost in plant and equipment: strong economies of scale.

2

Software. Writing the program costs a lot; one more copy costs almost nothing.

3

A company with ten layers of management. Slow decisions and lost information: diseconomies of scale.

How it connects. These are the causes of economies of scale (entry 6) and diseconomies of scale (entry 7).

Check yourself

8.1 Give one reason a bigger factory can make each unit more cheaply.

Show answer

Workers can specialize in narrow tasks, or large machines that would be wasted at small scale can be used fully.

8.2 Why do very large firms sometimes find their cost per unit rising?

Show answer

Coordination and communication become harder and more costly as the firm grows.

09

Increasing returns to scale

★ Exam
Definition

When all inputs increase in the same proportion, output increases by more than that proportion. Doubling every input more than doubles output.

Textbook page 562.

A bakery with 2 ovens and 4 bakers makes 500 loaves a day for $1,000. It doubles everything: 4 ovens, 8 bakers, $2,000 a day. What happens to output?

Returns to scaleInputsCostLoavesCost per loaf
Before2 ovens, 4 bakers$1,000500$2.00

Constant4 ovens, 8 bakers$2,0001,000$2.00

With increasing returns, output more than doubles, to 1,200 loaves, while cost only doubles: cost per loaf falls from $2.00 to $1.67. Increasing returns to scale bring economies of scale.

Watch out. Returns to scale are about inputs and output, all inputs together. Economies of scale are about cost. Increasing returns lead to economies, but the two are different ideas.

1

Inputs double, output rises from 500 to 1,200. Output rises 2.4 times: increasing returns.

2

Inputs up 10 percent, output up 15 percent. Increasing returns to scale.

3

Selena doubles everything and makes more than twice as much salsa. Her textbook's own example of increasing returns.

How it connects. It is the long-run relationship between inputs and output, so it needs the long run (entry 1); it causes economies of scale (entry 6); and it contrasts with decreasing returns (entry 10) and constant returns (entry 11).

Check yourself

9.1 All inputs rise by 50 percent and output rises by 80 percent. Which kind of returns to scale?

Show answer

Increasing: output rises by more than the inputs.

9.2 With increasing returns to scale, what happens to long-run average total cost as the firm grows?

Show answer

It falls: the firm has economies of scale.

10

Decreasing returns to scale

★ Exam
Definition

When all inputs increase in the same proportion, output increases by less than that proportion. Doubling every input less than doubles output.

Textbook page 563.

If the bakery doubles every input and output rises only to 800 loaves, cost per loaf rises from $2.00 to $2.50. Decreasing returns to scale bring diseconomies of scale.

Do not confuse it with diminishing marginal returns. Diminishing returns change one input with the others fixed, in the short run. Decreasing returns to scale change all inputs together, in the long run.

Watch out. Your textbook once says Selena has “decreasing returns to scale” just because her long-run cost rises. Rising LRATC is diseconomies of scale; decreasing returns to scale is a statement about inputs and output.

1

Inputs double, output rises from 500 to 800. Decreasing returns: cost per loaf rises to $2.50.

2

Inputs up 20 percent, output up 10 percent. Decreasing returns to scale.

3

A farm that doubles its land, machinery and workers. If the extra land is poorer, output may less than double.

How it connects. It causes diseconomies of scale (entry 7); and it contrasts with increasing returns (entry 9) and constant returns (entry 11).

Check yourself

10.1 All inputs double and output rises by 60 percent. Which kind of returns to scale?

Show answer

Decreasing: output rises by less than the inputs.

10.2 A firm adds workers to a fixed factory and each adds less output. Is that decreasing returns to scale?

Show answer

No. Only one input changes and the factory is fixed: that is diminishing marginal returns, in the short run.

11

Constant returns to scale

★ Exam
Definition

When all inputs increase in the same proportion, output increases by exactly that proportion. Cost per unit stays the same, so LRATC is flat.

Textbook page 563.

If the bakery doubles every input and makes exactly 1,000 loaves, cost per loaf stays at $2.00. Growing neither helps nor hurts.

On the exam, constant returns to scale is also the name for the flat part of LRATC, between economies and diseconomies of scale.

Watch out. Constant returns to scale does not mean output stays constant. It means output grows in the same proportion as the inputs.

1

Inputs double, output rises from 500 to 1,000. Constant returns: $2.00 a loaf before and after.

2

A chain opening an identical second restaurant. Twice the inputs, roughly twice the meals.

3

Ridgeline between 4 and 7 thousand bicycles. LRATC is flat at $200.

How it connects. It sits between increasing (entry 9) and decreasing returns (entry 10), and it gives efficient scale (entry 12).

Check yourself

11.1 All inputs rise by 25 percent and output rises by 25 percent. What happens to long-run average total cost?

Show answer

Nothing: with constant returns to scale cost per unit is unchanged.

11.2 What does constant returns to scale look like on an LRATC graph?

Show answer

A flat, horizontal stretch of the curve.

12

Efficient scale

★ ⚑
Definition

The range of output over which long-run average total cost is at its lowest and flat: constant returns to scale. A firm anywhere in this range produces at the lowest possible cost per unit.

Not in Module 56, or anywhere in your textbook.

⚑ Not in Module 56 — learn it from here. Your textbook draws long-run average total cost as a smooth U, with no flat part and no name for its bottom. Your exam calls the bottom efficient scale, and the flat part constant returns to scale.

Ridgeline's LRATC is $200 a bicycle for every output from 4 to 7 thousand a month. A firm making 5 thousand and a firm making 7 thousand have the same, lowest, cost per bicycle: both are at efficient scale.

Many real industries look like this. Once a firm is big enough, growing further hardly changes its cost per unit.

Watch out. Efficient scale is a range, not a single output. Its first point has its own name, the minimum efficient scale.

1

Ridgeline at 5 thousand bicycles. $200 a bicycle: at efficient scale.

2

Ridgeline at 7 thousand. Still $200: the end of the flat range.

3

Ridgeline at 9 thousand. $280: past efficient scale, into diseconomies.

How it connects. It is the flat stretch that constant returns to scale (entry 11) gives, and it starts at the minimum efficient scale (entry 13).

Check yourself

12.1 LRATC is $50 at 10,000, 15,000 and 20,000 units and rises after that. Over what range is the firm at efficient scale?

Show answer

From 10,000 to 20,000 units, where LRATC is flat at its lowest.

12.2 Is a firm at efficient scale making the lowest possible cost per unit?

Show answer

Yes: efficient scale is where long-run average total cost is at its minimum.

13

Minimum efficient scale

★ ⚑
Definition

The smallest output at which long-run average total cost reaches its minimum. Below it, the firm still has economies of scale to gain; at it, all have been used up.

Not in Module 56, or anywhere in your textbook.

⚑ Not in Module 56 — learn it from here. The phrase minimum efficient scale is nowhere in your textbook. Your exam uses it for the first output at the bottom of the long-run average total cost curve.

Ridgeline's LRATC falls until 4 thousand bicycles a month and is flat after that. So the minimum efficient scale is 4 thousand bicycles, at $200 each. A firm making 2 thousand pays $280 a bicycle: it is below the minimum efficient scale.

If LRATC is a smooth U with no flat part, the minimum efficient scale is simply its lowest point.

Watch out. The minimum efficient scale is a cost idea, from the long-run curve. It is not the output that maximizes profit, and it is not the bottom of a short-run ATC curve.

1

Ridgeline at 3 thousand bicycles. Below MES: $220, $20 above the lowest cost.

2

Ridgeline at 4 thousand. Exactly at MES: $200.

3

A smooth U-shaped LRATC lowest at 50,000 units. MES is 50,000 units.

How it connects. It is the first point of efficient scale (entry 12), where economies of scale (entry 6) end; it is the low point of LRATC (entry 4); and compared with market demand it decides how many firms fit (entry 14).

Check yourself

13.1 LRATC falls until 30,000 units, is flat until 60,000 and then rises. What is the minimum efficient scale?

Show answer

30,000 units: the smallest output at which LRATC is at its minimum.

13.2 A firm produces below its industry's minimum efficient scale. What happens to its long-run cost if it grows?

Show answer

It falls: there are still economies of scale to gain.

14

Minimum efficient scale and market structure

★ ⚑
Definition

Divide the quantity the market buys at the lowest cost by the minimum efficient scale: that is roughly how many firms can produce at the lowest cost. A large MES relative to the market means few firms; a small one leaves room for many.

Not in Module 56. Your textbook links economies of scale to the number of firms in Module 57, pages 571 and 573.

⚑ Not in Module 56 — learn it from here. Your textbook never compares the minimum efficient scale with the size of the market. Your exam asks how it shapes the number of firms and the market structure.

Ridgeline's minimum efficient scale is 4 thousand bicycles a month. How many firms fit depends on the size of the market:

If the whole market buys less than one firm's MES, long-run cost is still falling where it meets demand: one firm can supply everyone more cheaply than two. That is a natural monopoly. Large economies of scale also keep new firms out, because a small newcomer would have higher costs than the firms already there.

Watch out. It is the size of MES compared with the market that matters, not its size alone. A large MES in a huge market can still leave room for many firms.

1

A market of 8 thousand bicycles. 8 ÷ 4 = 2: room for two firms at the lowest cost.

2

A market of 40 thousand. 40 ÷ 4 = 10: room for about ten firms.

3

A town's water supply. One network serves the whole town more cheaply than two: a natural monopoly.

How it connects. It compares the minimum efficient scale (entry 13) with the market, and economies of scale (entry 6) are what make MES large.

Check yourself

14.1 MES is 50,000 units and the market buys 600,000 units at the lowest cost. About how many firms can produce at minimum cost?

Show answer

600,000 ÷ 50,000 = 12 firms.

14.2 Why might an industry with very large economies of scale have only one firm?

Show answer

If LRATC is still falling at the output the whole market buys, one firm producing everything has lower cost than any two firms sharing it.

15

Sunk cost

★ Exam
Definition

A cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered. It is the same whatever you decide next, so it should be ignored when deciding.

Textbook pages 563–564.

You have just paid $250 for new brake pads when you learn the whole brake system must be replaced for $1,500. Or you could sell the car and buy a similar one for $1,600. The $250 is gone either way. Compare only what is still to come: $1,500 to repair against $1,600 to replace. Repair it.

Adding the $250 to the repair — $1,750 against $1,600 — would lead to the wrong choice.

Watch out. A fixed cost and a sunk cost are not the same. A fixed cost does not change with output but may still be avoided in the long run; a sunk cost can never be recovered. Neither should change how much a firm produces.

1

The $250 brake pads. Sunk: ignore them; repair for $1,500 rather than replace for $1,600.

2

A city's finished metro tunnel. Its cost is sunk; whether to run trains depends on future costs and fares.

3

A non-refundable concert ticket on a night you feel ill. Sunk: go only if the concert is still worth going to.

How it connects. It is a cost that even the long run (entry 1) cannot undo, and it often comes from choosing the fixed cost (entry 2).

Check yourself

15.1 A firm has spent $2 million on research for a product that cannot be used elsewhere. Finishing it will cost $3 million more and earn $4 million. Should it finish?

Show answer

Yes. The $2 million is sunk; finishing costs $3 million and earns $4 million, a gain of $1 million.

15.2 Why should a sunk cost not affect a decision?

Show answer

Because it is the same whatever you choose, so it cannot make one choice better than another.

Show the mark scheme

(a) 2 points — plant X for 10,000 units ($5.00 against $7.00), and plant Y for 40,000 ($2.50 against $3.50).

(b) 1 point — $4.00: at 20,000 units both plants cost $4.00, and long-run cost is the lowest cost of any plant.

(c) 1 point — economies of scale: the lowest available cost falls from $5.00 to $4.00 to $2.50 as output rises.

(d) 2 points — a curve labeled LRATC on cost and quantity axes that falls, is flat and rises, with the three regions labeled (1 point), and MES marked at the start of the flat part (1 point).

Long runEvery input can change, so every cost is variable. Defined by inputs, not by time.
Choosing the fixed costA bigger plant: higher fixed cost, lower variable cost. Best for large planned output.

Decreasing returns to scaleAll inputs up x percent, output up less: brings diseconomies. Not diminishing returns.

Efficient scaleThe flat bottom of LRATC: the lowest cost per unit.

Things to get right

  • The long run is about inputs, not months: every input can change.
  • LRATC is the lowest short-run cost at each output. Every short-run curve is on or above it.
  • Economies and diseconomies of scale are long-run ideas. Falling AFC or rising short-run ATC are not.
  • Returns to scale change all inputs together. Diminishing marginal returns change one.
  • Increasing returns bring economies of scale; decreasing returns bring diseconomies.
  • The minimum efficient scale is the first output at the bottom of LRATC.
  • Market demand ÷ MES gives roughly how many firms can produce at the lowest cost.
  • Ignore sunk costs when deciding what to do next.
