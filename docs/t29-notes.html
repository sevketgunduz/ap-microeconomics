The headline. Topic 2.9 is the one topic in Unit 2 that Krugman does not teach at all, and the reference book teaches its second half far better than its first. Hoang's Chapter 28 is an excellent treatment of tariffs and quotas — four labelled areas, a worked calculation, every stakeholder — and that is POL-1.B.2 and POL-1.B.3. But POL-1.B.1 ends "Opening an economy to trade with other countries affects consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total economic surplus", and Hoang's Chapter 27, which draws the free-trade diagrams, uses consumer surplus and producer surplus 0 times. The book never says who gains and who loses from trade itself. That half has to be supplied, and OpenStax supplies it (Figure 20.3, p. 465). The reference book is an IB text, and its vocabulary is not the exam's. Hoang writes welfare loss (11 times in Chapter 28) where the exam writes deadweight loss (30 times across the released papers; welfare loss 0), and social or community surplus where the framework writes total economic surplus (21 times in the CED, 10 on the released papers, 0 times in the whole of Hoang). Translate both, every lesson. Two of the book's worked figures need correcting before you use them. Hoang's tariff calculation (pp. 440–444) uses points that do not lie on straight lines, so its two methods give different answers — a fall in consumer surplus of £140m by one and £170m by the other. And its quota section says the government "will receive some revenue from the cheese quota", which is the one answer the 2024 AP exam treats as the difference between a tariff and a quota. Both are in section 5.

Verdict on alignment. The reference book covers about two-thirds of the topic and covers it well. What it lacks is not content but direction: it starts from trade and asks what protection costs, where the framework starts from autarky and asks what trade gains. Teach Hoang's Chapter 28 nearly as written; build the first lesson from OpenStax's Figure 20.3 and the guide.

The essential knowledge, verbatim (CED p. 56):

POL-1.B.1 — "Equilibria in competitive markets may be altered by the decision to open an economy to trade with other countries; equilibrium price can be higher or lower than under autarky, and the gap between domestic supply and demand is filled by trade. Opening an economy to trade with other countries affects consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total economic surplus."
POL-1.B.2 — "Tariffs, which governments sometimes use to influence international trade, affect domestic price, quantity, government revenue, and consumer surplus and total economic surplus."
POL-1.B.3 — "Quotas can be used to alter quantities produced and therefore affect price, consumer surplus, and total economic surplus."
Exclusion — "The graphing of quotas is beyond the scope of the course and the AP Exam, but understanding how quotas affect quantities produced is within the scope."

Three things in that wording are worth reading aloud.

  1. "Higher or lower than under autarky." Both cases are required: a country can import (the

world price is below the autarky price) or export (it is above). Every book in this project leads with the importing case; the exporting case is where students are weakest.

  1. POL-1.B.2 lists government revenue; POL-1.B.3 does not. That single difference is the

whole of the 2024 exam's multiple-choice question 23, which asks how a tariff on imported tea differs from a quota, and keys the answer that only the tariff raises revenue for the government.

  1. Neither statement names domestic producer surplus — but the released papers ask about it

anyway (2024 MCQ 23 offers it as a distractor; the 2012 FRQ 3(c) asks for the tariff that maximises "the sum of … consumer surplus and producer surplus"). Teach it as examinable.

This topic is Module 50 with a horizontal line added. A tariff is a per-unit tax that falls only on imported units, and the world price is a perfectly elastic supply at that price. Students who have mastered Module 50's surplus account can do every calculation in Topic 2.9 once they see that.

The released papers set the standard for this topic, and two of them are worth knowing by heart — not reproduced here, but described:

  • 2012, free-response question 3 (5 points). A price-taking country imports sugar at a world

price of $2; a tariff raises the domestic price to $4. Students identify imports, the new domestic production, consumer surplus and tariff revenue from a linear graph, then name the tariff that maximises the sum of consumer and producer surplus — $0. The whole of POL-1.B(c) in one question.

  • 2024, multiple-choice question 23. A country importing tea weighs a tariff against a quota.

Which statement about the difference is true? Keyed: a tariff raises government revenue and a quota does not. The four distractors — consumer surplus, producer surplus, deadweight loss and the domestic price — are all things a tariff and a quota share.

  • 2024, multiple-choice question 54. A country opens its hat market to free trade at a world

price of $7; what is total spending on imported hats? World price × (quantity demanded − domestic quantity supplied).

The test for this topic carries an original item modelled on each.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Hoang's tariff calculation does not come from one market

Figures 28.4 and 28.5 (pp. 440–444) give a demand curve that meets the price axis at £20 and passes through 90m bottles at £8 and 80m at £10, and a supply curve that meets it at £4 and passes through 50m at £8 and 60m at £10. Those points are not on straight lines — the demand slope is −0.125 from the intercept to 80m and −0.2 between 80m and 90m; the supply slope is 0.08 and then 0.2. So the two ways of computing the change in surplus disagree:

Triangle with the intercept (Hoang)Area between the two prices
Change in consumer surplus−£140m (£540m → £400m)−£170m
Change in producer surplus+£80m (£100m → £180m)+£110m
Tariff revenue£40m£40m
Welfare (deadweight) loss£20m£20m

The revenue and the deadweight loss agree only because both are read directly off the four points. On the AP exam every graph is linear and the two methods always agree — so set the corrected example, or the guide's, rather than Hoang's. Note also the slip on p. 443: "the price of a bottle of wine in the UK increases to £8" should read £10; the calculation that follows uses £10.

A quota does not raise government revenue on the AP exam

Hoang (p. 446): "Technically, the British government will receive some revenue from the cheese quota … through the sale of import licences." That is a real-world possibility, and it is also the one difference the 2024 exam keys between a tariff and a quota. In the AP model the gap between the domestic and world price on the quota's imports is quota rent, and it goes to whoever holds the import licences — usually foreign exporters or domestic importers, not the government. AMSCO says the same as the AP model: area c "now becomes a transfer of consumers surplus that goes to importers" (p. 152). Teach AMSCO's version.

Do not drill the quota graph

The CED excludes it in so many words. Hoang draws it three times (Figures 28.7–28.9) and AMSCO once (p. 152). Show it if you like, as background; assess only what the framework names — a quota cuts the quantity imported, raises the domestic price, lowers consumer surplus and total economic surplus, and raises domestic producer surplus.

The world price is not "the price of imports plus a bit"

Hoang's diagrams draw S<sub>world</sub> as perfectly elastic and say why: the world can supply any amount at P<sub>w</sub>. Students need that assumption stated — a small country is a price taker in the world market — or the tariff analysis makes no sense. It is the same horizontal line as Module 47's perfectly elastic supply.

Trade does not make everyone better off

AMSCO (pp. 148–149) argues that a country exporting computers raises producer surplus and, through lower costs, consumer surplus. That is not the model the exam uses. When a country exports, its domestic price rises to the world price, so domestic consumers pay more and consumer surplus falls; producers gain by more than consumers lose. AMSCO's own coffee paragraph a page earlier (p. 147) gets this right ("Coffee drinkers in Colombia would pay more").

Producer surplus is not profit

OpenStax's Work It Out (p. 467) says "producer surplus is another name for profit". It is not: fixed costs are not subtracted. Module 49 made the same point.

"Welfare loss" and "deadweight loss" are one thing; "social surplus" and "total economic surplus" are one thing

Already in section 1, and worth repeating because the reference book never uses the exam's words.

  • Anything about exchange rates. The world price is in domestic currency throughout; currency

belongs to AP Macroeconomics.

  • Retaliation, trade wars, the WTO. Hoang and AMSCO both discuss them at length (Hoang p. 439,

AMSCO pp. 149, 153). Context only.

  • Subsidies as trade policy. Hoang's production and export subsidies (pp. 448–451) are sound

economics and outside Topic 2.9; the per-unit subsidy is Module 50.

  • The large-country case. Every model in the course assumes a price-taking country. A country

big enough to move the world price is beyond the framework.

7. Suggested allocation — two periods

Period 1 — opening to trade

  1. Autarky: the domestic market on its own, with its equilibrium (5 min).
  2. The world price, and why a small country takes it as given: a horizontal line (10 min).
  3. World price below autarky: quantity demanded rises, quantity supplied falls, imports fill the

gap. Who gains, who loses, and the net gain — the triangle (15 min).

  1. World price above autarky: the mirror image, exports, and the net gain again (10 min).
  2. OpenStax Figure 20.3 as the summary: both countries gain in total; someone loses in each

(10 min).

Period 2 — tariffs and quotas

  1. A tariff as a per-unit tax on imports: the domestic price rises by the tariff; domestic

production rises; consumption and imports fall (10 min).

  1. The four areas — consumer surplus lost, producer surplus gained, revenue, two deadweight triangles

— on the guide's numbers (15 min).

  1. A quota: the same price rise, the same triangles, and no revenue — the rectangle becomes quota

rent. No graph (10 min).

  1. The vocabulary map: welfare loss and social surplus into the exam's words (5 min).
  2. A Hoang Paper 3 calculation, renamed, then set the test (10 min).
