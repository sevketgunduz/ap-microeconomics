1. A hospital buys exactly 400 doses of a particular antivenom each year. When the manufacturer raises the price by 30%, the hospital buys 400 doses.

How is this demand best described, and what does its curve look like?

  • (A) Inelastic, and the curve is steeply downward sloping.
  • (B) Perfectly inelastic, and the curve is a vertical line. The percentage change in quantity demanded is zero, so the elasticity is zero whatever the price does.
  • (C) Perfectly elastic, and the curve is a horizontal line.
  • (D) Unit elastic, because the hospital's spending rises in proportion to the price.
  • (E) It cannot be classified without knowing the hospital's budget.

2. A thousand farmers sell identical wheat at the market price of $7 a bushel. If one farmer asks $7.05, buyers simply buy from someone else; at $7 that farmer can sell as much as they can grow.

What kind of demand curve does the individual farmer face?

  • (A) A steeply sloping one, because wheat is a necessity.
  • (B) A vertical one, because the quantity the farmer grows is fixed.
  • (C) A downward-sloping one, like any other demand curve.
  • (D) A horizontal one at $7 — perfectly elastic demand, with an infinite elasticity. Any price above $7 sells nothing, because buyers have a thousand perfect substitutes.
  • (E) A horizontal one, which means the elasticity is zero.

3. A ferry raises its fare from $8 to $12 and the number of passengers falls from 500 to 300 a day.

Using the midpoint method, how should this demand be classified?

  • (A) Elastic, because the elasticity is 1.25
  • (B) Inelastic, because the elasticity is 0.8
  • (C) Unit elastic, because both figures changed by the same amount
  • (D) Elastic, because the elasticity is 2.0
  • (E) Inelastic, because passengers still travel despite the higher fare

4. A student writes: "Demand for electricity is inelastic, so when the price rises households use exactly the same amount."

What is wrong with the claim?

  • (A) Nothing — that is what inelastic means.
  • (B) It is wrong because electricity demand is actually elastic in the short run.
  • (C) It overstates it. Inelastic means the quantity demanded changes proportionally less than the price, not that it does not change at all. No change at all is perfectly inelastic demand, a vertical curve, which is a different and much rarer case.
  • (D) It is wrong because inelastic demand means quantity demanded rises when price rises.
  • (E) It is wrong because elasticity cannot be applied to utilities, which are regulated.

5. Three goods have price elasticities of demand reported as 0.3, 1.0 and 4.5.

How should each be classified?

  • (A) All three are elastic, because all three are positive.
  • (B) 0.3 elastic, 1.0 unit elastic, 4.5 inelastic.
  • (C) 0.3 and 1.0 inelastic, 4.5 elastic.
  • (D) It cannot be said without knowing the prices.
  • (E) 0.3 inelastic, 1.0 unit elastic, 4.5 elastic — the benchmark is a magnitude of 1, above it elastic and below it inelastic.

6. A bus company carries 1,800 passengers a day at a fare of $12.

What is its daily total revenue, and what does the figure tell you about its profit?

  • (A) $21,600, and that is also its profit, since revenue is what the company keeps.
  • (B) $21,600, and it says nothing about profit, because no costs have been subtracted.
  • (C) $150, the revenue per passenger-hour.
  • (D) It cannot be found without knowing the elasticity of demand.
  • (E) $1,800, because total revenue counts passengers rather than money.

7. On a demand diagram, a student shades a rectangle whose height is a price of $9 and whose width is the quantity 400 — but at a price of $9 the demand curve says buyers take only 250 units.

Why is the rectangle not the firm's total revenue?

  • (A) The corner of a revenue rectangle must sit on the demand curve. At $9 only 250 units are actually bought, so the firm's revenue is $9 × 250 = $2,250, not $9 × 400.
  • (B) Because total revenue is measured as an area under the curve, not as a rectangle.
  • (C) Because the height should be the quantity and the width the price.
  • (D) Because revenue rectangles can only be drawn at the equilibrium price.
  • (E) It is correct; any rectangle of the right area measures revenue.

8. A café sells 400 coffees a day at $5. It raises the price to $6 and sells 360.

Taking the two effects separately, what happens to daily revenue?

  • (A) It falls by $200, because 40 customers were lost at $5 each.
  • (B) It rises by $360, because 360 customers now pay $1 more.
  • (C) It is unchanged, because the two effects always cancel.
  • (D) It rises by $160: the 360 who stay pay $1 more, which gains $360, while the 40 who leave used to pay $5 each, which loses $200.
  • (E) It falls by $40, which is the number of customers lost.

9. A water utility with a price elasticity of demand of 0.4 is considering a tariff increase.

What will happen to its total revenue, and why?

  • (A) It will fall, because customers will use less water.
  • (B) It will stay the same, because water is a necessity.
  • (C) It will rise. With demand inelastic the quantity effect is weaker than the price effect: the percentage fall in water used is smaller than the percentage rise in the tariff, so price × quantity goes up.
  • (D) It will rise, because a higher price always means more revenue.
  • (E) It cannot be predicted without knowing the utility's costs.

10. A theatre raises its ticket price from $20 to $25 and weekly attendance falls from 1,000 to 700.

What happens to weekly total revenue?

  • (A) It rises by $2,500.
  • (B) It is unchanged at $20,000.
  • (C) It falls by $300, the number of lost customers.
  • (D) It rises by $5 per remaining ticket, so by $3,500.
  • (E) It falls by $2,500, from $20,000 to $17,500.

11. A streaming service with a price elasticity of demand of 2.2 cuts its monthly subscription.

What happens to total revenue, and why?

  • (A) It falls, because each subscriber now pays less.
  • (B) It rises. Demand is elastic, so the percentage gain in subscribers outweighs the percentage cut in price and the quantity effect dominates.
  • (C) It is unchanged, because price cuts and subscriber gains always offset.
  • (D) It falls, because price cuts always reduce revenue.
  • (E) It rises, but only if the firm's costs fall at the same time.

12. A corner shop runs a "20% off everything" week. Its goods are everyday staples with a price elasticity of demand of about 0.5. Takings for the week fall sharply, and the owner is baffled.

What happened, and what would the right advice have been?

  • (A) With demand inelastic, cutting the price loses more from the lower price than it gains from extra sales, so takings had to fall. Raising prices would have raised takings — though it would have cost the shop its customers' goodwill, which elasticity does not measure.
  • (B) The discount was too small; a 50% cut would have raised takings.
  • (C) Takings fell because elasticity below 1 means customers buy less when prices fall.
  • (D) Nothing can be concluded, because elasticity does not apply to a whole shop.
  • (E) The shop should have cut prices further on luxuries only, since all staples are perfectly inelastic.

13. A government imposes a price floor on potatoes, raising the market price. The absolute value of the price elasticity of demand for potatoes is 0.92.

What happens to total consumer spending on potatoes, and why?

  • (A) It falls, because consumers buy fewer potatoes.
  • (B) It is unchanged, because 0.92 is close enough to 1 to count as unit elastic.
  • (C) It cannot be determined without knowing how far the price floor raises the price.
  • (D) It rises. 0.92 is below 1, so demand is inelastic: the percentage fall in the quantity bought is smaller than the percentage rise in the price, so price × quantity — what consumers spend in total — goes up.
  • (E) It rises, but only because a price floor also reduces supply.

14. A question asks what happens to total expenditure by consumers when a price rises. A student protests that they were only taught about total revenue.

What should the student be told?

  • (A) They are different measures, and total expenditure requires a separate formula.
  • (B) Total expenditure applies only to necessities.
  • (C) Total revenue is for firms and total expenditure is for governments.
  • (D) The question cannot be answered from an elasticity alone.
  • (E) They are the same number seen from the two sides of the transaction: price × quantity is simultaneously what sellers receive and what buyers spend. Every rule about revenue applies unchanged.

15. An economist states: "The price elasticity of demand for coffee is 0.25."

What is the most accurate reading of that statement?

  • (A) Coffee is a necessity and will always be inelastic.
  • (B) Coffee demand would be unresponsive at any price, however high.
  • (C) Demand for coffee is inelastic at the current price. On almost any demand curve the elasticity differs from point to point, so an elasticity describes a price or a range, not a good for all time.
  • (D) The slope of the demand curve for coffee is 0.25.
  • (E) A 25% rise in the price of coffee will reduce quantity demanded by 25%.

16. A firm's demand schedule gives the following total revenue at each price.

Price$5$6$7$8$9
Total revenue$24$25$24$21$16

Over which range is demand elastic?

  • (A) Between $5 and $6
  • (B) Between $6 and $9, where each further price rise reduces total revenue
  • (C) Over the whole range, because revenue changes throughout
  • (D) Between $5 and $7, where revenue is highest
  • (E) Nowhere — revenue never reaches zero

17. A straight-line demand curve meets the price axis at $20 and the quantity axis at 100 units.

At what price is demand unit elastic?

  • (A) $20, where quantity demanded is zero
  • (B) $5, one quarter of the way up
  • (C) $15, where the curve is steepest
  • (D) It is unit elastic along the whole curve, because the slope is constant
  • (E) $10, the midpoint of the curve — where 50 units are bought and total revenue reaches its maximum of $500

18. A student reasons: "Diamonds are expensive, and expensive things are things you really want, so demand for diamonds must be inelastic. Cheap things like matches must be elastic."

What is wrong with the reasoning?

  • (A) Elasticity is not determined by how expensive a good is. On a single demand curve the high-price end is the elastic one and the low-price end is inelastic, and across goods it is substitutes, necessity and budget share that decide — matches are cheap and inelastic.
  • (B) Nothing is wrong, provided the goods have no substitutes.
  • (C) It is wrong because diamonds are a necessity and matches are a luxury.
  • (D) It is wrong because elasticity applies only to goods sold in competitive markets.
  • (E) It is wrong because all luxury goods are perfectly elastic.

19. Two points are taken on the same straight-line demand curve: one near the top, one near the bottom. The elasticity at the first is 4; at the second it is 0.25.

What does this show?

  • (A) That the demand curve must have been drawn wrongly, because a straight line has one elasticity.
  • (B) That the slope of the curve changes between the two points.
  • (C) That elasticity and slope are measured in the same units but on different scales.
  • (D) That slope and elasticity are different things. The line has a single, constant slope throughout, yet its elasticity runs from very high at the top to very low at the bottom — so the one cannot be read off the other.
  • (E) That the second point must lie on a different demand curve.

20. A demand curve is a vertical line at 500 units.

What are its slope and its price elasticity of demand?

  • (A) Slope zero and elasticity zero.
  • (B) Slope zero and elasticity infinite.
  • (C) Slope undefined (or infinite) and elasticity zero — the two numbers sit at opposite extremes for the very same curve.
  • (D) Slope 500 and elasticity 500.
  • (E) Slope undefined and elasticity undefined.

21. A toll operator discovers that the price elasticity of demand for its bridge is 0.6 at the current toll. It wants to raise as much money as possible.

What should it do, and where should it stop?

  • (A) Raise the toll. Demand is inelastic, so revenue rises with every increase — and it should keep going until the elasticity reaches 1, which is where total revenue is at its maximum.
  • (B) Cut the toll, because lower prices attract more drivers.
  • (C) Leave the toll unchanged, because 0.6 is close to 1.
  • (D) Raise the toll without limit, since inelastic demand means revenue always rises.
  • (E) Raise the toll until the elasticity falls to zero, which is where revenue peaks.

22. Demand for one brand of bottled water is far more elastic than demand for bottled water in general.

Why?

  • (A) Because the brand is more expensive than the category average.
  • (B) Because the category is a necessity and the brand is a luxury.
  • (C) Because consumers spend a larger share of income on the category than on the brand, and share of income is the only determinant that matters.
  • (D) Because demand for a category is always perfectly inelastic.
  • (E) Because the brand has close substitutes — every rival brand on the same shelf — while the category as a whole has far fewer. Availability of substitutes is the determinant doing the work.

23. Housing is a necessity, which points towards inelastic demand. It also absorbs a large share of income, which points towards elastic demand. Your textbook's own table reports a price elasticity of demand for housing of 1.2.

What does that tell you about the two determinants?

  • (A) That the necessity test is always the stronger of the two.
  • (B) That the two tests can point in opposite directions, and which one dominates is a question for the evidence rather than for the definitions. Here the budget-share effect wins narrowly: 1.2 is only just into the elastic range.
  • (C) That housing is not really a necessity.
  • (D) That the estimate of 1.2 must be wrong, because necessities cannot be elastic.
  • (E) That the determinants only apply to goods with close substitutes.

24. Fuel duty rises sharply. After one month, consumption has barely moved. After eight years, consumption is far below where it started, even though the price has not risen again.

What does this illustrate?

  • (A) That the first month's measurement must have been in error.
  • (B) That the long-run elasticity of demand is lower than the short-run elasticity.
  • (C) That demand becomes more elastic as buyers have more time to adjust. In the first month drivers have the same cars, jobs and journeys; over years they change vehicles, routes and habits, which is why the long-run elasticity exceeds the short-run one.
  • (D) That the price change eventually reversed itself.
  • (E) That fuel is a luxury rather than a necessity.

25. Four goods: insulin, one supermarket's own-brand baked beans, a two-week cruise, and table salt.

Which ordering runs from least to most elastic, and what mainly explains it?

  • (A) Cruise, own-brand beans, salt, insulin — explained by price alone.
  • (B) Insulin, cruise, salt, own-brand beans — explained by how often each is bought.
  • (C) Salt, insulin, cruise, own-brand beans — explained by which are perishable.
  • (D) Insulin, salt, own-brand beans, cruise — necessity and the absence of substitutes make the first two deeply inelastic; close substitutes make the beans elastic; and a cruise is both a luxury and a large share of spending.
  • (E) Own-brand beans, salt, insulin, cruise — explained by how much each costs.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Perfectly inelastic demand · Analysis

Answer: (B)

The quantity does not move at all, so the percentage change in quantity demanded is zero, and zero divided by any price change is zero. That is the vertical curve of Figure 47.1(a).

  • (A) ✗ Close, but not the same. Inelastic means a small response; this is no response.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. The elasticity is zero and the curve is vertical.
  • (C) ✗ Perfectly elastic is the horizontal case, where a tiny price rise kills demand entirely.
  • (D) ✗ Spending does rise in proportion, but unit elastic means the elasticity is 1, which would

require quantity to fall by 30%.

  • (E) ✗ The budget would matter at some extreme price. Over the range described, the behaviour

itself classifies the demand.

Question 2 — Perfectly elastic demand · Analysis

Answer: (D)

One seller among a thousand identical ones faces a horizontal demand curve at the market price: any higher price sells nothing, and at the market price the farmer can sell everything.

  • (A) ✗ Describes the market demand curve for wheat, not the demand facing one farmer.
  • (B) ✗ Vertical is perfectly inelastic. The farmer's output is a supply question, not a demand

one.

  • (C) ✗ True of market demand; the individual seller's curve is flat.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Horizontal, infinite elasticity, and the reason is perfect substitutes.
  • (E) ✗ Right shape, wrong number — and the error is worth noting. A horizontal curve has slope

zero and elasticity infinite; it is the vertical curve whose elasticity is zero.

Question 3 — Elastic demand · Application

Answer: (A)

Midpoint method. Quantity: −200 ÷ 400 = −50%. Price: $4 ÷ $10 = +40%. Elasticity: 50 ÷ 40 = 1.25, which is above 1, so demand is elastic.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ 0.8 is the ratio upside down: 40 ÷ 50.
  • (C) ✗ They did not change by the same percentage — 50% against 40%. Unit elastic would need

them equal.

  • (D) ✗ 2.0 would require a 80% fall in passengers.
  • (E) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reasoning. "Passengers still travel" describes every case except

perfectly elastic demand; the classification comes from the two percentages.

Question 4 — Inelastic demand · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The commonest overstatement in this topic. Inelastic is a statement about proportions; no response at all is a different and much stronger claim.

  • (A) ✗ Repeats the error.
  • (B) ✗ Short-run electricity demand is strongly inelastic, which is the opposite claim.
  • (C) ✓ Correct, and it names the case the student is thinking of.
  • (D) ✗ That would be a Giffen good, which is Module 46 and essentially never observed.
  • (E) ✗ Regulation affects the price; it does not stop elasticity from being measurable.

Question 5 — The benchmark of 1 · Application

Answer: (E)

The framework's own wording: the separating benchmark is a magnitude of 1, above it elastic, below it inelastic, at it unit elastic.

  • (A) ✗ Every price elasticity of demand is positive once the sign is dropped, so that tells you

nothing.

  • (B) ✗ Exactly backwards for the two extremes.
  • (C) ✗ 1.0 is not inelastic; it is the dividing case and has its own name.
  • (D) ✗ The classification is read off the elasticity itself.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 6 — Total revenue · Application

Answer: (B)

$12 × 1,800 = $21,600. Nothing has been subtracted, so the figure says nothing about profit.

  • (A) ✗ Right number, wrong conclusion — revenue is not profit, and this is the error the entry

warns about.

  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Invents a per-hour measure the question does not ask for.
  • (D) ✗ Total revenue is price × quantity; no elasticity is needed to compute it.
  • (E) ✗ Revenue is measured in money, not passengers.

Question 7 — The revenue rectangle · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The rectangle has to describe a transaction that actually happens: a price and the quantity demanded at that price.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, with the right figure.
  • (B) ✗ The area under a demand curve is consumer willingness to pay, which is a different idea

and a later topic.

  • (C) ✗ Height is price and width is quantity. Swapping them would not change the area anyway.
  • (D) ✗ A revenue rectangle can be drawn at any price on the curve.
  • (E) ✗ Area alone is not enough; the corner must sit on the curve.

Question 8 — The price and quantity effects · Application

Answer: (D)

Price effect: the 360 who stay pay $1 more, gaining $360. Quantity effect: the 40 who leave used to pay $5 each, losing $200. Net +$160 — and indeed $5 × 400 = $2,000 against $6 × 360 = $2,160.

  • (A) ✗ The quantity effect alone.
  • (B) ✗ The price effect alone.
  • (C) ✗ They cancel only when demand is unit elastic. Here it is about 0.58.
  • (D) ✓ Correct, with both effects named and netted.
  • (E) ✗ Counts customers rather than money.

Question 9 — A price rise and total revenue · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Elasticity 0.4 is inelastic, so the price effect beats the quantity effect and revenue rises. A full answer names both effects.

  • (A) ✗ Customers do use less — that is the quantity effect — but it is the smaller of the two.
  • (B) ✗ Being a necessity is a reason demand is inelastic, not a reason revenue is unchanged.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Reaches the right answer by a false rule. A higher price reduces revenue whenever demand

is elastic, which is half of all cases.

  • (E) ✗ Costs affect profit, not revenue.

Question 10 — A price rise and total revenue · Application

Answer: (E)

$20 × 1,000 = $20,000 before; $25 × 700 = $17,500 after. Revenue falls by $2,500, which is what elastic demand does — the midpoint elasticity here is about 1.6.

  • (A) ✗ Right magnitude, wrong direction.
  • (B) ✗ Revenue is unchanged only when demand is unit elastic.
  • (C) ✗ Counts people instead of money.
  • (D) ✗ The price effect alone, ignoring the 300 lost customers.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 11 — A price fall and total revenue · Analysis

Answer: (B)

With elastic demand a price cut raises revenue, because the proportional gain in subscribers exceeds the proportional cut in price.

  • (A) ✗ The price effect alone; the quantity effect is the larger here.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ They offset only at an elasticity of exactly 1.
  • (D) ✗ A false general rule: price cuts raise revenue whenever demand is elastic.
  • (E) ✗ Costs are irrelevant to revenue.

Question 12 — A price fall and total revenue · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

With an elasticity of 0.5 a discount is a bad way to raise takings: the quantity gained is proportionally far smaller than the price given up.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, and it draws the practical conclusion while noting what elasticity does not

capture.

  • (B) ✗ A bigger cut makes it worse, not better — the same logic applies at every size.
  • (C) ✗ Reaches the right outcome by inventing a rule. Customers buy more when prices fall;

inelastic means they buy only a little more.

  • (D) ✗ A shop's basket of goods has an average elasticity like anything else.
  • (E) ✗ No staple is perfectly inelastic, and the advice does not follow from the figure given.

Question 13 — Total expenditure · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

This is the shape of a real exam item, and it is the revenue rule under its other name. 0.92 < 1, so demand is inelastic, so a higher price raises total spending.

  • (A) ✗ Fewer potatoes are bought, but each costs proportionally more — and with an inelastic

elasticity the price effect wins.

  • (B) ✗ "Close enough to 1" is not a category. Anything below 1 is inelastic.
  • (C) ✗ The direction does not depend on the size of the price change; the elasticity settles it.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong mechanism — a price floor creates a surplus, and the spending

answer comes from the elasticity, not from the supply side.

Question 14 — Total expenditure · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Price × quantity is one number with two names. Everything learned about revenue transfers directly.

  • (A) ✗ There is no second formula.
  • (B) ✗ Nothing restricts expenditure to necessities.
  • (C) ✗ Invents a distinction that does not exist.
  • (D) ✗ An elasticity is exactly what answers it.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 15 — Elasticity changes along the curve · Analysis

Answer: (C)

An elasticity belongs to a point or a range on a demand curve, which is why the framework's own wording says "with respect to that price in the range of the given change".

  • (A) ✗ Overgeneralises in exactly the way the entry warns against.
  • (B) ✗ Move far enough up the curve and demand becomes elastic.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Confuses the two measures — see questions 19 and 20.
  • (E) ✗ That would be an elasticity of 1, not 0.25.

Question 16 — Elasticity changes along the curve · Application

Answer: (B)

Revenue peaks at $6. Above that, every further price rise reduces revenue — $25 → $24 → $21 → $16 — which is the signature of elastic demand.

  • (A) ✗ Revenue rises from $24 to $25 there, so demand is inelastic over that step.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Revenue changes in both directions; only the falling part is elastic.
  • (D) ✗ Mixes the two: between $5 and $6 revenue rises, which is inelastic.
  • (E) ✗ Revenue reaching zero is not the test. The test is which way revenue moves when price

rises.

Question 17 — Where the ranges sit · Application

Answer: (E)

On a straight-line demand curve, unit elasticity sits at the midpoint — half the price intercept and half the quantity intercept. Here that is $10 and 50 units, and revenue there is $500, the largest the curve allows.

  • (A) ✗ At the price intercept quantity is zero and demand is perfectly elastic in the limit, not

unit elastic.

  • (B) ✗ A quarter of the way up is deep in the inelastic range.
  • (C) ✗ A straight line has the same slope everywhere, so "where the curve is steepest" names no

point at all.

  • (D) ✗ The constant slope is exactly why the elasticity cannot be constant.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, with the revenue maximum as the check.

Question 18 — Where the ranges sit · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Two separate mistakes: that a high price means inelastic demand, and that price level alone decides elasticity. On a single curve the high-price end is the elastic end.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, and it supplies the counter-example of a cheap, inelastic good.
  • (B) ✗ Substitutes matter, but the reasoning would still be wrong without them.
  • (C) ✗ Reverses the labels; it does not fix the logic.
  • (D) ✗ Elasticity is measured in every market structure.
  • (E) ✗ Luxuries tend to be elastic, not perfectly elastic — that is the horizontal curve.

Question 19 — Slope is not elasticity · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

A straight line has one slope. The fact that its elasticity ranges from 4 to 0.25 is the demonstration that slope and elasticity are different quantities — the claim the framework makes and the textbook only illustrates.

  • (A) ✗ The opposite: a straight line is precisely where elasticity varies most visibly.
  • (B) ✗ A straight line's slope is constant by definition.
  • (C) ✗ They are not in the same units at all. Slope is money per unit; elasticity has no units.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Both points were specified as being on the same curve, and nothing is inconsistent.

Question 20 — Slope is not elasticity · Analysis

Answer: (C)

A vertical line has an undefined (or infinite) slope and an elasticity of zero. The horizontal case reverses both numbers. Those two rows together are the cleanest evidence that the measures differ.

  • (A) ✗ A zero slope belongs to the horizontal curve.
  • (B) ✗ Both numbers are from the horizontal case.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ 500 is a quantity, not a slope or an elasticity.
  • (E) ✗ The elasticity is perfectly well defined: it is zero.

Question 21 — The revenue peak · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Inelastic demand means a price rise raises revenue, so the operator should raise the toll — and the stopping point is where the elasticity reaches 1, the top of the revenue hill.

  • (A) ✓ Correct, with the stopping rule.
  • (B) ✗ Cutting the price of an inelastic good reduces revenue.
  • (C) ✗ 0.6 is firmly inelastic; there is money on the table.
  • (D) ✗ Right first move, no stopping rule — and the rule matters, because the elasticity rises as

the toll does. A student who picks this has half the answer.

  • (E) ✗ Revenue peaks at an elasticity of 1, not 0. At zero elasticity revenue would rise forever.

Question 22 — Availability of substitutes · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The number of close substitutes is the strongest determinant, and it is the only one the framework names. A brand has many; a category has few.

  • (A) ✗ Price level is not a determinant.
  • (B) ✗ Both are the same kind of good; the labels do not apply differently.
  • (C) ✗ Gets the budget shares backwards and claims a single determinant, which the module denies.
  • (D) ✗ No category is perfectly inelastic.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 23 — Necessity, luxury and budget share · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

The determinants are tendencies, not rules, and they can conflict. Housing is the standard case, and the published estimate settles it only narrowly.

  • (A) ✗ If it were, housing would be inelastic. It is not.
  • (B) ✓ Correct, and it reads the figure as the evidence.
  • (C) ✗ Housing is a necessity by any ordinary test.
  • (D) ✗ Assumes the conclusion the data contradict.
  • (E) ✗ The determinants apply to every good, substitutes or not.

Question 24 — Time · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Time is the fourth determinant, and it works by letting buyers find substitutes that do not exist today. The 1970s petrol case in the textbook is exactly this.

  • (A) ✗ The small short-run response is the expected result, not an error.
  • (B) ✗ Backwards: the long-run elasticity is the larger.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The question says the price did not rise again.
  • (E) ✗ Fuel behaves like a necessity in the short run, which is the first half of the story, not

a contradiction of it.

Question 25 — The determinants together · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Insulin and salt: no substitutes, necessity, tiny budget share — deeply inelastic. Own-brand beans: a shelf full of substitutes. A cruise: a luxury and a large share of spending.

  • (A) ✗ Exactly reversed, and price alone is not a determinant.
  • (B) ✗ Frequency of purchase is not one of the four.
  • (C) ✗ Perishability is not a determinant either, and the ordering puts salt below insulin for no

stated reason.

  • (D) ✓ Correct, with the determinant named for each.
  • (E) ✗ Cost is not a determinant, and the ordering puts a brand with many substitutes at the

inelastic end.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 18 concept entries appear. The revenue rules take six items (8 to 12, plus 21), which matches both their share of the module's pages and the fact that MKT-3.E.5 is the only predictive claim in Topic 2.3. The four determinants take four items, one each plus a combined one.

Bloom levels. Application 7 · Analysis 12 · Synthesis 6, a ratio of 1 : 1.7 : 0.9 — the same shape as the Module 46 paper. The arithmetic here is lighter than in Module 46 (no elasticity has to be computed from scratch in more than two items), so the weight shifts to interpretation, which is what this module is for.

Questions 19 and 20 are the pair to discuss aloud. Together they are MKT-3.E.2's second sentence, which the textbook demonstrates and never states. Question 20's option (A) — "slope zero and elasticity zero" — is the single most seductive distractor on the paper, and it is the same trap your textbook sets in its own MCQ 3.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: the ratio inverted (3B); one effect reported instead of both (8A, 8B, 10D, 11A); inelastic read as "no response" (4A); the two extreme cases swapped (1C, 2E, 20A, 20B); "a higher price always means more revenue" (9D); and price level treated as a determinant (18 stem, 22A, 25E).

Four distractors reach a right-looking conclusion by wrong reasoning, flagged in the solutions: 3E, 9D, 12C, 13E and 21D. The most useful is 21D — it gets the direction right and omits the stopping rule, which is precisely the half-answer that loses the second mark on an explain question.

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 5 · D 5 · E 5 — exactly even, with no letter repeating in consecutive questions.

What this paper assumes. That students can already compute an elasticity from two percentage changes or two price–quantity pairs — Module 46's work. Only questions 3 and 17 ask for a calculation from raw data; the rest hand over the elasticity, which is how released items behave.
