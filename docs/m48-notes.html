The headline. Module 48 carries two whole topics — 2.4 Price Elasticity of Supply and 2.5 Other Elasticities — in seven pages, and it carries them well. Cross-price elasticity, income elasticity, price elasticity of supply, both extreme supply curves, the determinants and a summary table are all here, and Table 48.1 is the single most useful page in the elasticity run. Two required elements are missing, and both are a third case the book never lists. MKT-3.E.11 says cross-price elasticity tells you whether goods are "substitutes, complements, or not related" — and not related occurs 0 times in all 927 pages. Krugman's own Table 48.1 has a row for complements and a row for substitutes and no row for zero. Separately, MKT-3.E.8 names "the price of alternative inputs" as a determinant of supply elasticity; Krugman names availability of inputs and time, and the phrase alternative input occurs 0 times in this module. The sign changes meaning here, and that is the single most important thing to say in class. In Modules 46 and 47 the minus sign was dropped. From this module on, the sign is the answer: it is what separates substitutes from complements, and normal goods from inferior ones. Krugman says so explicitly on p. 476; say it louder.

The essential knowledge statements this module delivers, verbatim:

MKT-3.E.6 (CED p. 51) — "Price elasticity of supply is measured by the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price, or the responsiveness of the quantity supplied to changes in price."
MKT-3.E.7 (CED p. 51) — "Ranges of values of elasticity of supply are described as elastic or inelastic with the separating benchmark being a magnitude of 1, where the change in the price and the change in the quantity supplied are proportional." (a, b and c then repeat the elastic, inelastic and unit elastic cases word for word from MKT-3.E.3.)
MKT-3.E.8 (CED p. 51) — "The price elasticity of supply depends on certain factors such as the price of alternative inputs."
MKT-3.E.9 (CED p. 52) — "Elasticity can be measured for any determinant of demand or supply, not just the price."
MKT-3.E.10 (CED p. 52) — "Income elasticity of demand is measured by the percentage change in the quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in consumers' income. Economists use the income elasticity of demand to determine whether a good is normal or inferior."
MKT-3.E.11 (CED p. 52) — "Cross-price elasticity of demand is measured by the percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another good. Economists use the cross-price elasticity of demand to determine whether goods are substitutes, complements, or not related."

MKT-3.E.7 is MKT-3.E.3 with one word changed. Demanded becomes supplied. Make that explicit: students who learned the demand benchmark have already learned this one, and the only genuinely new thing on the supply side is that there is no minus sign to drop.

The margin definitions, in Krugman's own words

Cross-price elasticity of demand (p. 475) — "The cross-price elasticity of demand between two goods measures the effect of the change in one good's price on the quantity demanded of the other good. It is equal to the percent change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percent change in the other good's price."
Income elasticity of demand (p. 476) — "The income elasticity of demand is the percent change in the quantity of a good demanded when a consumer's income changes divided by the percent change in the consumer's income."
Income-elastic / income-inelastic (p. 476) — "The demand for a good is income-elastic if the income elasticity of demand for that good is greater than 1. … The demand for a good is income-inelastic if the income elasticity of demand for that good is positive but less than 1."
Price elasticity of supply (p. 477) — "The price elasticity of supply is a measure of the responsiveness of the quantity of a good supplied to the price of that good. It is the ratio of the percent change in the quantity supplied to the percent change in the price as we move along the supply curve."
Perfectly inelastic supply (p. 478) — "There is perfectly inelastic supply when the price elasticity of supply is zero, so that changes in the price of the good have no effect on the quantity supplied. A perfectly inelastic supply curve is a vertical line."
Perfectly elastic supply (p. 479) — "There is perfectly elastic supply if the quantity supplied is zero below some price and infinite above that price. A perfectly elastic supply curve is a horizontal line."

Table 48.1 is worth photocopying. It is the only place in the book where all four elasticities sit side by side with their value ranges, and it is a better revision aid than anything a teacher would make. Note its one omission: the cross-price section has rows for complements and substitutes and no row for zero, which is section 3's first gap.

Krugman's two extreme supply examples are unusually good. Cell phone frequencies really are fixed by physics, and pizza really can be produced in any quantity at a constant cost. He even says so: "real-world instances of both perfectly inelastic and perfectly elastic supply are easier to find than their counterparts in demand."

The "not related" gap is small to close and easy to test. The framework names three outcomes and the textbook teaches two. A cross-price elasticity of approximately zero means a price change in one good leaves demand for the other where it was — the goods are unrelated. The CED's own sample multiple-choice question (Exam Information p. 128) offers five options that mix the sign and the magnitude, which is exactly the skill at stake.

The "alternative inputs" gap is a rewording rather than a hole. Krugman's determinant is the availability of inputs; the framework's is their price. They are the same economics — an input that is cheap and plentiful can be bid into this industry easily — but a student who has only met availability may not recognise a question phrased around the price of alternative inputs. One sentence connects them: if the inputs you need are cheap to attract away from other uses, you can expand output quickly, and supply is elastic.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

The minus sign is now information, not clutter

In Modules 46 and 47 the sign was always negative and was always dropped. Here:

  • Cross-price: the sign tells you substitutes (+) from complements (−).
  • Income: the sign tells you normal (+) from inferior (−).
  • Supply: there is no sign problem at all, because the supply curve slopes upward.

A student who carries over the "drop the minus sign" habit will report a cross-price elasticity of −3 as 3 and conclude the goods are substitutes. That is one of the most costly single errors in Unit 2, and it comes from good habits formed two modules earlier.

Elastic and income-elastic are different questions

Elastic on its own always means the price elasticity of demand exceeding 1. Income-elastic means the income elasticity exceeds 1. A good can be price-inelastic and income-elastic at the same time — restaurant meals are close. Always name which elasticity you mean.

Cross-price elasticity is not symmetric, and the order matters

Equation 48-1 divides the change in quantity of A by the change in the price of B. Swap them and you get a different number. Insist that students write which good is on top; Krugman's FRQ 1 answer key does exactly this, and the formula mark is awarded for it.

"Income elasticity is positive" is the whole answer for a normal good

Krugman's MCQ 3 is built on this. The income elasticity of a normal good is positive — it is not necessarily 1, or greater than 1, or any particular number. Normal goods subdivide into income-elastic and income-inelastic, and both are normal.

A vertical supply curve is not "no supply"

Perfectly inelastic supply means a fixed quantity, not an absent one. The cell phone frequencies exist; what cannot change is how many there are. Students who read "unresponsive" as "unavailable" will misread every tax-incidence diagram in Topic 2.8.

Short run and long run are about the producer's room to manoeuvre

Krugman's "a few weeks or months" against "several years" is not a calendar rule. It is about whether inputs can be changed. Say that now, because Unit 3 will define the two runs exactly that way.

Food is the worked case of two elasticities at once

The farming story needs both: demand for food is income-inelastic, so spending on it grows more slowly than income, and it is price-inelastic, so rising yields cut farmers' total revenue. Students often use one and think they have explained it. Both are needed, and they are different elasticities.

6. What this module does not give you for Unit 2

  • Any use of supply elasticity. The reason the framework cares about it — who bears a tax, how

much a subsidy raises output — is Topic 2.8 and Module 50. Here it is defined and measured and then left alone.

  • A graph of anything but the extremes. Figure 48.1 draws the vertical and horizontal cases and

nothing in between. Students should sketch an ordinary upward-sloping supply curve and ask where its elasticity is high and where it is low.

  • Cross-price elasticity in a market diagram. The number is computed, but the shift it implies is

never drawn. Connect it back to Module 5's demand shifters explicitly.

  • Any treatment of elasticity of demand for a firm's own product. That is Unit 4, where it

decides a monopolist's price.
