The 16 terms

01

Utility

★ Exam
Definition

Utility is a measure of personal satisfaction — the benefit you get from consuming something.

Textbook page 511. Topics 1.5 and 1.6.

Economics needs a word for “how much better off this makes me”, and utility is it. Your textbook is careful about what it does not claim: utility is “a concept we use to understand behaviour but don't expect to measure in practice.” Nobody has a utility meter.

The whole model rests on one modest assumption — that people try to get more satisfaction rather than less. You do not need to know how much satisfaction, or compare yours with anyone else's. You only need each person to prefer more to less.

Your framework gives utility a second name that your textbook never uses. For a consumer, total benefit is utility. Keep that translation in your head from the start; entry 14 comes back to it.

Watch out. Utility is not money and not price. A thing can be cheap and give you a lot of utility, or expensive and give you very little. Price is what you pay; utility is what you get.

Textbook

Soup or salad. Your book's own example: “I can have either soup or salad with my dinner. Which will I enjoy more?” That question, asked seriously, is utility maximisation.

Numeric

A number that only ranks. If a film is worth 40 to you and a concert 55, the model says you prefer the concert. It does not say the concert is 1.375 times as good, and it says nothing about how either compares with the 55 someone else reports.

Illustrative

Producing satisfaction. Your book suggests thinking of consumers as using consumption to “produce” utility, the way a firm uses inputs to produce output. The budget is the input; satisfaction is the output.

How it connects. It is measured in utils (entry 2) and totalled as total utility (entry 4). Your framework calls it the consumer’s total benefit (entry 14).

Check yourself

1.1 Why does the model not need to compare your utility with someone else's?

Show answer

Because every decision it analyses is one person choosing between their own options. You only ever compare your own alternatives, so a scale that ranks your preferences is enough.

1.2 A student says a $200 coat must give more utility than a $20 one. What is wrong?

Show answer

Price and utility are different things. The $200 coat costs more; whether it satisfies you more is a separate question, and for some buyers the answer is no.

02

Util

★ Exam
Definition

A util is a hypothetical unit of utility — invented so that satisfaction can be put in a table and arithmetic can be done on it.

Textbook page 512.

Utils are a measuring convenience, nothing more. Your book introduces them with a shrug: “it is useful to suppose that we can measure utility in hypothetical units called — what else? — utils.”

Every number in this module is in utils, and every exam question that gives you a utility table is using them. Use them freely inside one person's problem, and never carry them between people.

Watch out. Utils are not comparable between people. “Ali gets 50 utils and Bea gets 40, so Ali enjoys it more” is not a claim this model supports. Each person's scale is their own. The word util also never appears in your framework — it is your textbook's unit, and a very useful one.

Textbook

The reason for inventing them. Without a unit there is no table, and without a table there is no marginal utility. Utils exist to make the next four entries possible.

Numeric

Reading a utility table. Cassie's total utility from clams runs 0, 15, 28, 39, 48, 55, 60, 63, 64, 63. Every one of those figures is in utils, and all you will ever do with them is subtract and divide.

Illustrative

Like a rating out of ten. Your framework's own suggested classroom activity has students eat sweets and rate each one 1–10. Those ratings are utils. Nobody thinks a 7 means the same thing to two people.

How it connects. It is the unit of utility (entry 1), and it is what marginal utility (entry 5) is measured in.

Check yourself

2.1 Two people rate the same meal 60 utils and 45 utils. What can you conclude?

Show answer

Nothing about who enjoyed it more. You can only use each person's own numbers to rank that person's own options.

2.2 What two operations will you ever perform on a utility figure in this course?

Show answer

Subtraction, to get marginal utility, and division by a price, to get marginal utility per dollar. That is the whole of the arithmetic.

03

Utility function

★ Exam
Definition

A utility function shows the relationship between a consumer's utility and the consumption bundle they consume.

Textbook page 512, Figure 51.1.

It is the rule that turns quantities into satisfaction. Give it a bundle and it gives back a number of utils. Drawn as a curve, it usually rises — more is better — and gets flatter as you go, which is the next three entries in one sentence.

In this module the utility function almost always arrives as a table, not an equation. That is also how the exam gives it to you.

Watch out. The utility function is the whole relationship, not one number. “My utility is 48” is a single value of it; the function is the column of values for every quantity.

Textbook

Cassie's fried clams. Figure 51.1 shows her total utility from an all-you-can-eat clam dinner: a side of coleslaw that comes with the meal, plus however many clams she chooses.

Numeric

The function as a column. 0 clams → 0 utils; 1 → 15; 2 → 28; 3 → 39; 4 → 48; 5 → 55; 6 → 60; 7 → 63; 8 → 64; 9 → 63. That column is the utility function.

Illustrative

A recipe for satisfaction. Inputs go in, satisfaction comes out. The shape of the curve is what makes the choice interesting: if it were a straight line rising forever, you would just buy one good.

How it connects. It maps a consumption bundle (entry 7) to total utility (entry 4). Its slope is marginal utility (entry 5).

Check yourself

3.1 Why is a utility function usually drawn getting flatter as quantity rises?

Show answer

Because each extra unit adds less than the one before — diminishing marginal utility, entry 6. Flattening is that principle drawn.

3.2 Cassie's function turns downward after 8 clams. What does that mean?

Show answer

That the ninth clam makes her worse off: total utility falls from 64 to 63. A rational consumer stops at 8.

04

Total utility

★ Exam
Definition

Total utility is the whole amount of satisfaction from a consumption bundle — the number the consumer is trying to make as large as possible.

Textbook page 512. Topics 1.5 and 1.6.

This is the objective of the entire module. Your framework states it plainly: CBA-2.A.2 — “In a model of rational consumer choice, consumers are assumed to make choices so as to maximise their total utility.”

When there are two goods, total utility is just the sum: the utility from the clams plus the utility from the potatoes. Every bundle has a total, and the job is to find the biggest one you can afford.

Textbook

The peak. Figure 51.1's caption: total utility “increases until it reaches its maximum utility level of 64 utils at 8 clams consumed and decreases after that.”

Numeric

Adding two goods. Sammy consuming 2 pounds of clams (25 utils) and 6 pounds of potatoes (47.0 utils) has a total utility of 72.0. The whole of the next six entries is about finding that bundle.

Illustrative

A day out. The total satisfaction from a day is the sum of its parts — the film, the meal, the walk home. You choose the combination with the best total, not the best single item.

How it connects. It is the sum of the utility from every good in the bundle (entry 7), read off the utility function (entry 3). Maximising it, subject to the budget constraint (entry 8), defines the optimal consumption bundle (entry 11).

Check yourself

4.1 Cassie's total utility is 63 at 7 clams and 64 at 8. Is the eighth clam a big gain?

Show answer

No — it adds only 1 util, against 15 for the first. Total utility is near its peak, so the extra units are barely worth anything. That is the whole point of looking at the margin.

4.2 Sammy gets 25 utils from clams and 47.0 from potatoes. What is his total utility?

Show answer

72.0 utils. With two goods you simply add the two columns.

05

Marginal utility

★ Exam
Definition

Marginal utility is the change in total utility from consuming one additional unit of a good.

Textbook page 513, margin definition.

Subtract one row of the total-utility column from the row above it. That is the whole calculation, and it is the most common single arithmetic step in this topic.

Marginal utility is the slope of the utility function. That one sentence resolves most of the confusion in this module: total utility is the height of the hill, marginal utility is how steep it is. The hill is still going up while it is getting less steep, and it reaches its top exactly where the steepness hits zero.

The same nine numbers, twice. Left: total utility, peaking at 64. Right: marginal utility, falling steadily and turning negative at the ninth clam. Marginal utility is plotted between the whole numbers, because it measures a change from one quantity to the next.

Watch out. Marginal utility can be negative, and Cassie's ninth clam is. A negative marginal utility means the extra unit makes you worse off — which is why a rational consumer refuses it even when it is free.

Textbook

The all-you-can-eat problem. “Restaurants that offer all-you-can-eat meals depend on the proposition that you can have too much of a good thing.”

Numeric

One subtraction each. Cassie's totals 0, 15, 28, 39, 48, 55, 60, 63, 64, 63 give marginal utilities of 15, 13, 11, 9, 7, 5, 3, 1, −1. Every figure is one row minus the row above.

Illustrative

The first glass of water. After an hour in the sun the first glass is worth a great deal, the second rather less, and the fifth almost nothing. The sixth, forced down, is worth less than nothing.

How it connects. It is the change in total utility (entry 4) and the slope of the utility function (entry 3). Divided by price it becomes marginal utility per dollar (entry 12). Your framework calls it marginal benefit (entry 15).

Check yourself

5.1 Total utility goes 0, 20, 36, 48, 56. What are the marginal utilities?

Show answer

20, 16, 12, 8. Each is one row minus the previous row, and they are falling — diminishing marginal utility.

5.2 Total utility is rising and marginal utility is falling. Is that a contradiction?

Show answer

No, and it is the normal case. Marginal utility is the slope. A hill that is still climbing but getting less steep has rising total and falling marginal. Total utility peaks exactly where marginal utility reaches zero.

06

Diminishing marginal utility

★ Exam
Definition

According to the principle of diminishing marginal utility, each successive unit of a good adds less to total utility than the unit before it.

Textbook page 513, margin definition. CBA-2.A.3, CED p. 39.

Your framework states it in eleven words: CBA-2.A.3 — “Consumers experience diminishing marginal utility in the consumption of goods and services.”

The reason is satiation. The more of something you have already had, the closer you are to the point where another one adds nothing. Your textbook's example is bananas: a marvellous treat for someone who almost never gets one, and for someone who eats them daily “just, well, a banana.”

Diminishing marginal utility is why the marginal utility curve slopes downward, and that downward slope is what makes the rest of this module work. If marginal utility never fell, you would spend your entire income on one good.

Watch out. It is about marginal utility falling, not total utility falling. While marginal utility is positive but shrinking, total utility is still rising. Total only falls once marginal goes below zero.

Textbook

The exceptions, admitted. Your book's FYI box is honest: skiing and coffee take practice, and Alfred Marshall's wallpaper is worse than useless if you buy only enough for half a room. Marginal utility genuinely rises over some ranges — just not over the range that matters for most goods.

Numeric

Falling, and then negative. 15, 13, 11, 9, 7, 5, 3, 1, −1. Each step down is 2 utils. By the eighth clam an extra clam is worth a fifteenth of what the first one was.

Illustrative

Songs on repeat. The song you loved on the first play is tiresome by the twentieth. Nothing about the song changed.

How it connects. It is a claim about how marginal utility (entry 5) behaves, and it is what makes marginal utility per dollar (entry 12) fall as you buy more — which is what lets the optimal consumption rule (entry 13) pick out a single best bundle.

Check yourself

6.1 Why would a consumer refuse a free unit of a good?

Show answer

If its marginal utility is negative, consuming it lowers total utility. Cassie's ninth clam takes her from 64 utils to 63, so she declines it even at a price of zero.

6.2 Name a good whose marginal utility rises over some range, and say why it does not break the model.

Show answer

A skill that needs practice — skiing, or coffee — or a good you must buy in a minimum quantity, like wallpaper for a room. It does not break the model because over the range people actually consume, marginal utility is still falling.

07

Consumption bundle

★ Exam
Definition

A consumption bundle is the combination of goods and services a consumer consumes — so many of this, so many of that.

Textbook page 512.

A bundle is a complete answer to “what did you buy?”. Write it as a pair: (2 pounds of clams, 6 pounds of potatoes). In this course a bundle is almost always two goods, because two goods fit on a diagram.

Everything else in the module is a property of bundles. A bundle has a cost. A bundle has a total utility. Some bundles are affordable and some are not. One of the affordable ones is best.

Watch out. A bundle is a combination, not a quantity of one good. “Two pounds of clams” is not a bundle until you also say how many potatoes — and “and no potatoes” is a perfectly good answer.

Textbook

Cassie's dinner. Her bundle is “a side of coleslaw, which comes with the meal, plus a number of clams to be determined.” The coleslaw is fixed; the clams are the choice.

Numeric

Six bundles on one line. Sammy's six affordable bundles that spend all $20 are (0, 10), (1, 8), (2, 6), (3, 4), (4, 2) and (5, 0), writing clams first. Each costs exactly $20.

Illustrative

A weekly shop. The trolley at the checkout is a consumption bundle. Changing your mind about one item changes the bundle, and changes both its cost and its utility.

How it connects. It is what a utility function (entry 3) takes as its input, what the budget constraint (entry 8) limits the cost of, and what you are choosing when you pick the optimal consumption bundle (entry 11).

Check yourself

7.1 Clams cost $4 a pound and potatoes $2. What does the bundle (3, 4) cost?

Show answer

3 × $4 + 4 × $2 = $12 + $8 = $20.

7.2 Is (5 clams, 0 potatoes) a consumption bundle?

Show answer

Yes. Zero of a good is a legitimate quantity, and that bundle sits at one end of the budget line.

08

Budget constraint

★ Exam
Definition

A budget constraint limits the cost of a consumer's bundle to no more than their income: spending on one good + spending on the other ≤ income.

Textbook page 514, margin definition. CBA-2.A.1, CED p. 39.

This is scarcity, applied to one person. Your framework puts it as CBA-2.A.1 — “Consumers face constraints and have to make optimal decisions accounting for these constraints” — and CBA-2.A.4 adds the crucial adjective: consumers allocate their limited income.

The constraint is what makes the problem interesting. Without it you would buy every unit with positive marginal utility of every good, and there would be nothing to work out.

Watch out. The constraint says spending is no more than income — an inequality. But as long as more of a good is always better and saving gives no satisfaction, the consumer spends every dollar, so in practice it holds with equality. That is why the answer always sits on the budget line.

Textbook

The inequality itself. Equation 51-1: “Expenditure on clams + Expenditure on potatoes ≤ Total income.” Your book calls it “a simple way of saying that a consumer can't spend more than the total amount of income available.”

Numeric

Testing a bundle. Sammy has $20, clams cost $4 and potatoes $2. Bundle (2, 6) costs 2 × $4 + 6 × $2 = $8 + $12 = $20 — it satisfies the constraint exactly. Bundle (3, 6) would cost $24 and fails.

Illustrative

A prepaid card. Fifty pounds on a card and no overdraft. You can spend less; you cannot spend more. Every purchase you make is a purchase you cannot make.

How it connects. It is what makes total utility (entry 4) something to be maximised rather than simply increased. The bundles that satisfy it are the consumption possibilities (entry 9), and the ones that satisfy it exactly form the budget line (entry 10).

Check yourself

8.1 Income $30, good X at $3 and good Y at $6. Does the bundle (4 X, 3 Y) satisfy the constraint?

Show answer

4 × $3 + 3 × $6 = $12 + $18 = $30. Yes — and exactly, so it spends all the income.

8.2 Why does a consumer end up spending all their income in this model?

Show answer

Because unspent money produces no utility and more of either good is assumed to be better. Any dollar left over could have bought something worth having.

09

Consumption possibilities

★ Exam
Definition

A consumer's consumption possibilities are the set of all affordable bundles, given their income and the prices they face.

Textbook page 514, margin definition.

This is the shaded region in your textbook's Figure 51.2 — everything on or inside the budget line. It is the consumer's version of the production possibilities curve from Module 3: a boundary between what can be had and what cannot.

It depends on exactly two things: income and prices. Change either and the set changes. Tastes have nothing to do with it — utility decides which bundle you pick from the set, not what is in the set.

Watch out. Affordable is not the same as sensible. The bundle (0 clams, 0 potatoes) is affordable and nobody would choose it. The set tells you what is possible; the utility function tells you what is best.

Textbook

The shaded area. “Every bundle on or inside this line (the shaded area) is affordable; every bundle outside this line is unaffordable.”

Numeric

Counting the whole-unit bundles. With $20, clams at $4 and potatoes at $2, there are six whole-unit bundles that spend every dollar — and many more that spend less, such as (1, 2) at $8. All of them are in the consumption possibilities.

Illustrative

A menu you can pay for. The set is everything on the menu within your budget. Which one you order is a different question, and it is the rest of this module.

How it connects. It is the set of bundles allowed by the budget constraint (entry 8), bounded by the budget line (entry 10). Compare Module 3: this is the consumer’s version of the region on or inside a production possibilities curve.

Check yourself

9.1 What two things determine a consumer's consumption possibilities?

Show answer

Income and prices. Nothing else — in particular, not the consumer's tastes, which decide the choice rather than the options.

9.2 Prices halve while income stays the same. What happens to the set?

Show answer

It grows: every bundle that was affordable still is, and many that were not now are. Halving both prices does the same thing as doubling income.

10

Budget line

★ Exam
Definition

The budget line shows every bundle available to a consumer who spends all of their income. It is the boundary of the consumption possibilities.

Textbook page 514, margin definition, and Figure 51.2.

To draw it, find the two intercepts: all income on one good, then all income on the other. Sammy's $20 buys 5 pounds of clams at $4, or 10 pounds of potatoes at $2, so his line runs from (0, 10) to (5, 0).

The line slopes downward, and the slope is an opportunity cost — the same idea as Module 3. Moving along it, one more pound of clams means two fewer pounds of potatoes, because clams cost twice as much.

Watch out. The word budget occurs 0 times in the AP Microeconomics framework. The budget line is your textbook's apparatus, and it is worth knowing — the AP Review asks you to draw one — but the exam is more likely to hand you a table and ask for the marginal-utility-per-dollar condition. Be ready for both.

Textbook

The two ends. “If Sammy spends all of his income on clams (bundle F ), he can purchase 5 pounds of clams; if he spends all of his income on potatoes (bundle A), he can purchase 10 pounds.”

Numeric

The equation of the line. $4 × clams + $2 × potatoes = $20, so potatoes = 10 − 2 × clams. Put clams = 3 and you get 4 potatoes — bundle D.

Illustrative

Spending down to zero. Every point on the line is a way of arriving at the till with nothing left. There are usually several, and only one of them is best.

How it connects. It is the edge of the consumption possibilities (entry 9), and the optimal bundle (entry 11) always lies on it. Its slope is an opportunity cost, exactly as in Module 3.

Check yourself

10.1 Income $60, good X at $5, good Y at $10. What are the two intercepts of the budget line?

Show answer

12 units of X (60 ÷ 5) with no Y, and 6 units of Y (60 ÷ 10) with no X. Join them with a straight line.

10.2 Why is the optimal bundle always on the line rather than inside it?

Show answer

A bundle inside the line leaves income unspent. That money could buy another unit with positive marginal utility, so any interior bundle can be improved on.

This is the total approach, and your framework names it: CBA-1.B.2 says some decisions “must be evaluated by looking at total benefits and total costs.” Entry 13 solves the identical problem at the margin instead, and gets the same answer — which is the point of doing both.

Watch out. The optimum is not the bundle with the most of your favourite good, and not the one in the middle. Sammy likes clams — they give him 15 utils for the first pound against 11.5 for the first potato — and he still buys three times as many potatoes, because they cost half as much.

Textbook

The top of the hill. Figure 51.3 draws total utility against the bundle and the optimum is simply the peak: “Sammy's optimal consumption bundle puts him at the top of the total utility curve.”

Numeric

Every bundle, scored. 56.7, 68.2, 72.0, 67.8, 55.4, 36.0. The third is the largest, so bundle C wins. Six additions and one comparison.

Illustrative

Trying every option. If there are only six ways to spend the money, price all six and pick the best. It is slow but it never goes wrong, and it is the honest answer when the numbers are awkward.

How it connects. It maximises total utility (entry 4) subject to the budget constraint (entry 8), so it lies on the budget line (entry 10). The faster route to it is the optimal consumption rule (entry 13).

Check yourself

11.1 Why is bundle B (1 clam, 8 potatoes, 68.2 utils) not optimal?

Show answer

Because bundle C is affordable and gives 72.0 utils. B is on the budget line and spends every dollar — being on the line is necessary, not sufficient.

11.2 Sammy prefers clams unit-for-unit but buys three times as many potatoes. Why?

Show answer

Because potatoes cost half as much. What matters is satisfaction per dollar, not per pound — entry 12.

12

Marginal utility per dollar

★ Exam
Definition

Marginal utility per dollar is the additional utility from spending one more dollar on a good: MU ÷ price.

Textbook page 518, margin definition, and Table 51.3. CBA-2.A.4, CED p. 39.

This is the single most important calculation in Topics 1.5 and 1.6, and it is one division. Your textbook writes it as

MUgood ÷ Pgood

Why divide? Because marginal utility ranks units and you are not choosing between units — you are choosing where to send the next dollar. A clam gives Sammy more utility than a potato does, and costs twice as much; dividing is what makes the two comparable.

Because marginal utility falls as you buy more (entry 6), marginal utility per dollar falls too.

Watch out. Never compare raw marginal utilities across goods with different prices. The AP Review has an item built entirely to catch this: 100 utils from the last unit of a $2 good against 80 utils from the last unit of a $1 good. The $2 good looks better and is not — 50 utils per dollar against 80.

Textbook

The worked division. “Consider what happens if Sammy increases his clam consumption from 2 pounds to 3 pounds. This raises his total utility by 6 utils. But he must spend $4 … so the increase in his utility per additional dollar spent on clams is 6 utils/$4 = 1.5 utils per dollar.”

Numeric

Both columns of Table 51.3. Clams at $4: marginal utilities 15, 10, 6, 3, 2 give 3.75, 2.50, 1.50, 0.75, 0.50 per dollar. Potatoes at $2: 11.5, 9.9, 8.4, 7.0, 5.7, 4.5 give 5.75, 4.95, 4.20, 3.50, 2.85, 2.25.

Illustrative

Value for money. A large coffee has more caffeine than a small one. Which to buy depends on caffeine per pound spent, not caffeine per cup.

How it connects. It is marginal utility (entry 5) divided by price, and it falls as you buy more because of diminishing marginal utility (entry 6). Setting it equal across goods is the optimal consumption rule (entry 13).

Check yourself

12.1 Good A costs $5 and its next unit gives 40 utils. Good B costs $2 and its next unit gives 18 utils. Which dollar is better spent?

Show answer

A gives 40 ÷ 5 = 8 utils per dollar; B gives 18 ÷ 2 = 9. Spend on B, even though a unit of A delivers more than twice as much utility.

12.2 Why does marginal utility per dollar fall as you consume more of a good?

Show answer

Because the price stays the same while marginal utility falls. Dividing a shrinking number by a constant gives a shrinking number.

13

The optimal consumption rule

★ Exam
Definition

To maximise utility, a consumer equates the marginal utility per dollar spent on every good — while spending all their income.

Textbook page 520, margin definition and Equation 51-3. CBA-2.A.4, CED p. 39.

Your framework's wording is the one to memorise: CBA-2.A.4 — “Consumers allocate their limited income to purchase the combination of goods that maximises their utility by equating/comparing the marginal utility of the last dollar spent on each good.”

MUX ÷ PX  =  MUY ÷ PY

The argument for it is a single move. Suppose a dollar spent on clams buys 3 utils and a dollar spent on potatoes buys 1. Move a dollar from potatoes to clams: you gain 3 and lose 1, so you are 2 utils better off and you have spent exactly as much as before. Keep going. You can only stop improving when the two are equal — and that is the rule.

It generalises to any number of goods, and it is the same rule you will meet again in Unit 3 as marginal revenue = marginal cost and in Unit 6 as marginal social benefit = marginal social cost. Learn it once.

Watch out. Two things have to hold, not one. Equal marginal utility per dollar is necessary but not sufficient — you must also spend all your income. Your textbook's own review makes the point: at 4 pounds of clams and 10 pounds of potatoes the two figures are both exactly 0.75, and that bundle costs $36 on a $20 income. Equal, and unaffordable.

Textbook

The rule stated. “When a consumer maximizes utility in the face of a budget constraint, the marginal utility per dollar spent on each good or service in the consumption bundle is the same.”

Numeric

The AP Review's clean case. Snacks at $4, drinks at $2, income $20. At 2 snacks and 6 drinks, the second snack gives 10 utils → 2.50 per dollar, and the sixth drink gives 5 utils → 2.50 per dollar. Exactly equal, all $20 spent, total utility 72 — the maximum.

Illustrative

Levelling two buckets. Water flows from the fuller bucket to the emptier until the levels match. Dollars flow to whichever good is currently buying more utility, until neither is.

How it connects. It equates marginal utility per dollar (entry 12) across goods and finds the optimal consumption bundle (entry 11) without listing every possibility. It is the consumer’s version of marginal benefit = marginal cost (entry 15).

Check yourself

13.1 A consumer gets 30 utils from the last unit of X at $3 and 60 utils from the last unit of Y at $10. What should they do?

Show answer

X gives 10 utils per dollar, Y gives 6. Buy more X and less Y. As they do, X's marginal utility falls and Y's rises, until the two per-dollar figures meet.

13.2 Two goods have equal marginal utility per dollar, but the consumer has $8 left over. Are they at the optimum?

Show answer

No. The rule needs all income spent as well. Spending the $8 on either good adds utility, so the bundle can be improved.

14

Total benefit and total cost

★ ⚑
Definition

Total benefit is the whole benefit from a choice — for a consumer, that is total utility. Total cost is everything given up. Total net benefit is the difference, and it is maximised at the optimal choice.

The phrase “total benefit” occurs 0 times in this module. CBA-1.A.2, CBA-1.B.1, CBA-1.B.2, CED p. 38.

This is Topic 1.5's vocabulary, and your textbook never uses it. It works entirely in total utility — which is the same thing. Your framework says so outright:

CBA-1.A.2 — “Total benefits form the metric ‘utility’ for consumers and total revenue for firms.”

So every total-utility figure in this module is a total benefit. Table 51.2's column of 56.7, 68.2, 72.0, 67.8, 55.4 and 36.0 is a table of total benefits, and picking the largest is what CBA-1.B.1 means by “total net benefits … are maximised at the optimal choice.”

CBA-1.B.2 is the one to understand rather than memorise: some decisions can be taken at the margin, and some cannot be broken into increments at all and have to be judged on totals. Going to university is a totals decision — there is no such thing as one more marginal unit of a degree.

Watch out. If an exam question says total benefit and you have only ever seen total utility, they are the same thing for a consumer. Write either; recognise both. The whole gap between your textbook and your framework on Topic 1.5 is this one label.

Textbook

The words it uses instead. Table 51.2's last column is headed “Total utility (utils)”. Read it as “total benefit” and nothing about the arithmetic changes.

Numeric

Net benefit at the optimum. Sammy's total benefit at bundle C is 72.0 utils and his total cost is the $20 he spends. No other affordable bundle gives a larger benefit for that same cost, so net benefit is maximised there.

Illustrative

A decision with no margin. Your framework's own suggested activity: work out the total costs and benefits of a university degree and compare them. You cannot buy 3% of a degree, so marginal analysis has nothing to work on and totals are the only option.

How it connects. For a consumer it is total utility (entry 4), and maximising it subject to the budget constraint (entry 8) is exactly what the optimal consumption bundle (entry 11) does. Its marginal counterpart is marginal benefit and marginal cost (entry 15).

Check yourself

14.1 An exam question asks for the total benefit of a consumption bundle. Your table is headed “total utility”. What do you do?

Show answer

Use it directly — they are the same quantity. `CBA-1.A.2` states that total benefits are the metric utility for consumers.

14.2 Give a decision that cannot be analysed at the margin, and say why.

Show answer

Whether to take a degree, have an operation, or move house. Each is all-or-nothing: there is no “one more unit” to price, so you must compare total benefits with total costs.

15

Marginal benefit and marginal cost

★ ⚑
Definition

Marginal benefit is the additional benefit from one more unit of an activity; marginal cost is the additional cost. The optimal quantity is where MB = MC.

Neither phrase occurs in this module. CBA-2.B.1, CBA-2.B.3, CED p. 40.

Your framework defines marginal analysis in these words, not in utility: CBA-2.B.1 — “Marginal analysis involves comparing the additional benefit of increasing a given activity with the additional cost.” And CBA-2.B.3 gives the rule: “The optimal quantity is achieved when marginal benefit is equal to marginal cost or where total benefit is maximised.”

You have already done this. The translation is exact:

Your textbook, Module 51Your framework, CBA-2.B
Marginal utility of one more unit, MUMarginal benefit
The price you pay for it, PMarginal cost
MUX ÷ PX = MUY ÷ PYMB = MC, applied per dollar to each good
Total utility is maximisedTotal benefit is maximised

This rule outlives the module. It returns as MR = MC for a firm in Unit 3, as the hiring rule in Unit 5, and as MSB = MSC for society in Unit 6. It is the same sentence every time.

Watch out. MB = MC does not mean the benefit and the cost are equal in total. At the optimum you are usually far ahead overall — it is only the last unit on which benefit and cost have converged. Stopping there maximises the gap between the totals, which is the whole point.

Textbook

The same argument, in utils. Krugman's reasoning on page 520 — move a dollar to wherever it buys more utility, and keep going until neither is better — is MB = MC with utility as the benefit and price as the cost.

Numeric

Where to stop. A stall's next hour brings in $40 and costs $25 → work it. The hour after that brings $30 and costs $28 → work it. The next brings $22 and costs $31 → stop. The optimum is the last hour with MB ≥ MC.

Illustrative

Revision the night before. The first hour raises your mark a lot; the fifth barely at all, and costs you sleep. You stop when the next hour's gain no longer covers what it costs you.

How it connects. It is the general form of the optimal consumption rule (entry 13), with marginal utility (entry 5) as the benefit and price as the cost. Its totals counterpart is total benefit and total cost (entry 14).

Check yourself

15.1 Translate “MU per dollar is equal across goods” into marginal benefit and marginal cost.

Show answer

The marginal benefit of the last dollar spent is the same wherever you spend it. Since each dollar costs a dollar, marginal benefit equals marginal cost at the margin for every good.

15.2 At the optimal quantity, MB = MC. Does that mean the activity is barely worth doing?

Show answer

No. It means the last unit is barely worth doing. All the earlier units had marginal benefit well above marginal cost, so total net benefit is at its largest.

16

Sunk cost

★ ⚑
Definition

A sunk cost is a cost already incurred that cannot be recovered. The optimal quantity does not depend on it.

Does not occur in this module. CBA-2.B.2, CED p. 40.

Your framework requires this in Topic 1.6 and your textbook introduces it four hundred pages later, in Unit 3. It is one idea and it belongs with marginal analysis, so learn it here.

CBA-2.B.2 — “The optimal quantity at any point in time does not depend on fixed costs (sunk costs) or fixed benefits that have already been determined by past choices.”

The reason is simple once you see it. Marginal analysis compares what happens if you do one more with what happens if you do not. A cost that is the same either way cancels out of both sides. It cannot change the comparison, so it cannot change the answer.

Watch out. “I have already paid for it” is never a reason to consume more. The money is gone whatever you do next, so the only question is whether the next unit is worth its own cost. This is the error the rule exists to prevent, and it feels wrong to most people — which is why it is examinable.

Textbook

Where it turns up instead. Sunk costs appear in your book from page 563 onward — Modules 56 and 58–60, on firm costs and the shut-down decision. The idea is identical; only the setting changes.

Numeric

The all-you-can-eat buffet. You paid $18 to get in. The eighth clam is worth 1 util to you, the ninth is worth −1. The $18 does not appear in either figure, so it cannot make the ninth clam worth eating. Stop at eight.

Illustrative

The film you are not enjoying. The ticket cost $12 and you have watched twenty minutes. Staying costs you ninety minutes and gains you very little; leaving costs you nothing you have not already spent. The $12 is gone either way.

How it connects. It is a condition on how marginal benefit and marginal cost (entry 15) are computed: only costs that change with the decision belong in the comparison. It returns as the firm’s shut-down rule in Unit 3.

Check yourself

16.1 A concert ticket cost $60 and is non-refundable. On the night you would rather stay home. What should you do, and why?

Show answer

Stay home if that is what you prefer. The $60 is gone whether you go or not, so it belongs in neither the benefit nor the cost of going. Compare only the evening out against the evening in.

16.2 Why can a sunk cost never change the optimal quantity?

Show answer

Because marginal analysis compares doing one more against not doing it, and a sunk cost is identical in both cases. It cancels, so it cannot tip the comparison either way.

The five things to get right from Module 51

  1. Divide by the price. Marginal utility ranks units; marginal utility per dollar ranks purchases, and only the second answers “what should I buy next?” Every review question in this module has a distractor waiting for anyone who forgets.
  2. Total rises while marginal falls. Marginal utility is the slope of total utility. Total peaks exactly where marginal reaches zero, and falls once marginal goes negative.
  3. Equal MU per dollar is necessary, not sufficient. You must also spend all your income. Your textbook’s own review has a bundle with perfectly equal figures that costs $36 out of a $20 budget.
  4. Total utility is total benefit. Your framework says so; your textbook never uses the phrase. Recognise both, and remember that some decisions — a degree, an operation — have no margin at all and must be judged on totals.
  5. Sunk costs never change the answer. A cost that is identical whether or not you buy one more cancels out of the comparison. “I already paid” is not a reason.
