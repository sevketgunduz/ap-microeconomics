Efficiency and Deadweight Loss

Nineteen terms from Module 50 of Krugman’s Economics for AP. Why a competitive market cannot be improved on — and what a tax, or a subsidy, costs when a government changes it anyway.

Textbook pages 495–510·19 terms· Topics 2.6 and 2.8·2½ periods

How to use this guide

Read Module 50 in your textbook first. Entries 1 to 5 are efficiency; entries 6 to 15 are what a tax does; entries 16 to 19 are what your textbook leaves out and two side-costs worth knowing.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 50 of your textbook does not teach it. Entries 4, 16 and 17 carry the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. The competitive equilibrium makes total surplus as large as it can be. Anything that changes the quantity traded — a tax, a subsidy, a price control, a quota — makes it smaller, and the shortfall that nobody receives is the deadweight loss.

⚑ One big thing is missing. Your course names taxes and subsidies three times over, and subsidies come up on real exam papers as often as consumer surplus does. The word does not appear once in Module 50. Entries 16 and 17 supply the whole of it, on your textbook’s own hotel market.

Potterville, with and without a tax

Your textbook’s hotel market. Without intervention it clears at $80 a room and 10,000 rooms a night. Add a per-unit tax and the supply curve shifts up: buyers pay more, sellers receive less, fewer rooms are rented. Add a subsidy instead and everything runs the other way.

A per-unit tax, or a subsidy
Rooms per night5,000
Consumer surplus$50,000
Producer surplus$50,000
Government revenue$200,000
Deadweight loss$100,000

Two things to notice. The $60 tax raises less revenue than the $40 tax — $150,000 against $200,000 — while its deadweight loss more than doubles. And the subsidy costs the government $600,000, three times what the equivalent tax collects, because it pushes the quantity up rather than down.

The same tax, three different markets

All three markets start at $80 and 10,000 rooms, and all three get the same $40 per-unit tax. The only difference is how responsive buyers are. Watch where the tax lands.

Who bears a $40 tax?

The more inelastic side bears more. When demand is the inelastic side, buyers pay three-quarters of the tax; when demand is the elastic side, they pay a quarter. Nothing about the law changed — only the shape of the curves.

Revenue, deadweight loss, or neither?

Twelve descriptions. The distinction that matters: a transfer is received by somebody, a loss is received by nobody, and several of these are neither.

The nineteen terms

01

Total surplus

★ Exam
Definition

Consumer surplus plus producer surplus — the whole net gain to everyone from trading in a market. Your course calls it total economic surplus.

Textbook page 495.

Module 49 built two areas and stopped. This module adds them:

total surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus

In Potterville's hotel market the equilibrium is $80 a room and 10,000 rooms a night. Consumer surplus is $200,000 and producer surplus is $200,000, so total surplus is $400,000 a night — the value the market creates by existing.

That single number is what the rest of this module, and most of Unit 6, is about.

Watch out. Your textbook writes total surplus; your course writes total economic surplus. They are the same thing, and the longer phrase is the one on exam papers.

1

Potterville at $80. $200,000 + $200,000 = $400,000 a night.

2

A market with consumer surplus $4,200 and producer surplus $2,800. $7,000.

3

A market where nothing trades. Zero. No trades, no gains.

How it connects. It is what the competitive equilibrium (entry 2) maximises, it is what deadweight loss (entry 13) measures a fall in, and it is the number the whole account in the surplus account (entry 14) has to balance.

Check yourself

1.1 Consumer surplus is $900 and producer surplus is $1,400. What is total economic surplus?

Show answer

$2,300.

1.2 Why is total surplus a better measure of a market's value than consumer surplus alone?

Show answer

Because sellers gain too. Judging a market only by what buyers get would rate a market that ruins its sellers as a success.

02

Why the equilibrium maximises total surplus

★ Exam
Definition

In a competitive market with no market failures, the equilibrium produces the largest possible total surplus. That is what calling a market efficient means.

Textbook pages 495–498, Figures 50.1 to 50.4.

This is the central claim of the unit, and your textbook proves it rather than asserting it. It asks three questions, and the answer to each is “no”:

  • Could we do better by giving the good to different buyers?
  • Could we do better by having different sellers supply it?
  • Could we do better by trading more or fewer units?

Each change makes total surplus smaller. So the equilibrium has the right buyers, the right sellers and the right quantity — and nothing can improve on it.

Watch out. “Efficient” is a statement about the size of the pie, not about who gets the slices. A market can be perfectly efficient and wildly unequal.

1

Right buyers. The units go to the people who value them most.

2

Right sellers. They are supplied by the people who can give them up most cheaply.

3

Right quantity. Every trade worth making is made, and no trade that is not worth making happens.

How it connects. It is a claim about total surplus (entry 1), it is established by three experiments (entry 3), it is the content of allocative efficiency (entry 4), and it is the standard an equity argument (entry 5) is measured against.

Check yourself

2.1 A market is in competitive equilibrium. Is consumer surplus at its maximum?

Show answer

No — a lower price would give buyers more. It is total surplus that is maximised, which is why your textbook's own review question makes this exact distinction.

2.2 What does “in the absence of market failures” add to the claim?

Show answer

It limits it. When there are externalities, public goods or market power, the equilibrium can fail to maximise total surplus — which is the whole of Unit 6.

03

The three reallocation experiments

★ Exam
Definition

Move consumption, move sales, or move the quantity — total surplus falls every time.

Textbook pages 496–498, Figures 50.2, 50.3 and 50.4.

Reallocate consumption. Take a book from a buyer who values it at $35 and give it to one who values it at $25. Total surplus falls by $10.

Reallocate sales. Have a seller whose cost is $35 supply it instead of one whose cost is $25. Total surplus falls by $10 again.

Change the quantity. Force one extra trade and you are matching a buyer who values the good at less than the seller's cost — a loss. Prevent one trade and you lose a gain that was there for the taking.

Your textbook's own review question asks whether any of the three could increase total surplus at equilibrium. The answer is none of them.

Watch out. These experiments are about the total. Reallocating always makes somebody better off — that is not the test. The test is what happens to the sum.

1

A book moved to a buyer who values it less. The total falls by the difference.

2

A sale moved to a higher-cost seller. Same again, on the selling side.

3

One trade beyond the equilibrium quantity. The buyer values it at less than it costs the seller to supply, so the trade destroys value.

How it connects. They are the proof behind the efficiency claim (entry 2), and the third of them is what allocative efficiency (entry 4) and a deadweight loss (entry 13) both exploit.

Check yourself

3.1 Why does moving a sale from a low-cost to a high-cost seller reduce total surplus?

Show answer

Because the same unit now costs society more to produce, while the buyer's gain is unchanged. The difference is pure loss.

3.2 Which of the three experiments does a tax perform?

Show answer

The third: it changes the quantity traded. That is why a tax creates a deadweight loss.

04

Allocative efficiency

★ ⚑
Definition

Resources are allocated so that the goods produced are the ones society values most — which happens exactly when total surplus is maximised.

Not in Module 50 — or anywhere else in your textbook.

⚑ Not in Module 50 — learn it from here. Your course names this idea in so many words — and says that intervention in an efficient market “can only decrease allocative efficiency”. The word allocative does not appear once in your textbook's 927 pages. The economics is in Figures 50.2 to 50.4; only the label is missing.

Your course uses this term for the idea your textbook has just proved: at the competitive equilibrium, the last unit produced is worth exactly what it cost to produce, so nothing is made that is not worth making and nothing worth making is left unmade.

In diagram language: allocative efficiency holds where the demand curve crosses the supply curve, because the height of the demand curve is what buyers value the unit at and the height of the supply curve is what it costs.

Your course also states the consequence that this module spends ten pages on: intervening in an efficient market — by a tax, a subsidy, a price control or a quantity control — can only decrease allocative efficiency.

Watch out. Allocative efficiency is about which goods get produced and in what quantity. It is not the same as producing cheaply — that is a different idea you will meet in Unit 3.

1

The competitive equilibrium. Allocatively efficient: value equals cost on the last unit.

2

A binding price ceiling. Too few units trade, so trades worth making do not happen. Allocative efficiency falls.

3

A per-unit subsidy. Too many units trade — the last few cost more than they are worth. Allocative efficiency falls here too.

How it connects. It is the name for what the efficiency claim (entry 2) establishes and what the three experiments (entry 3) demonstrates, and it is what the equity trade-off (entry 5) measures the loss of.

Check yourself

4.1 A market trades fewer units than the equilibrium quantity. Is it allocatively efficient?

Show answer

No. There are units buyers value above what sellers would need to supply them, and those trades are not happening.

4.2 Why can intervening in an already-efficient market only make allocative efficiency worse?

Show answer

Because the equilibrium already maximises total surplus. Any change moves away from the maximum, in whichever direction it pushes the quantity.

05

Equity against efficiency

○ Background
Definition

Efficiency is about the size of the total surplus. Equity is about how fairly it is shared. They are different goals and they often conflict.

Textbook page 499.

A competitive market maximises total surplus. It says nothing at all about whether the resulting distribution is fair — and policies that make a distribution fairer very often shrink the total.

Your textbook's own review question asks which statement about equity and efficiency in competitive markets is true, and the answer is that there is often a trade-off between them.

You will not be asked to define equity. You may well be asked to recognise that an efficient outcome is not automatically a desirable one.

Watch out. Do not write that an efficient market is “fair” or that an inefficient one is “unfair”. The two words answer different questions.

1

A market that maximises total surplus. Efficient — and possibly one in which the poorest buy nothing at all.

2

A policy that redistributes income. May improve equity while reducing total surplus.

3

Organ allocation by expected gain in lifespan. Maximises the gain; many people think it unfair to the oldest patients.

How it connects. It is the limit of what allocative efficiency (entry 4) claims, it is the question the efficiency argument (entry 2) cannot settle on its own, and it is the heading progressive and regressive taxes (entry 19) belong under.

Check yourself

5.1 A government taxes a market and uses the money to help poorer households. Is this a good policy?

Show answer

Economics cannot answer that on its own. It can say the tax reduces total surplus, and that the transfer may improve equity. Weighing the two is a judgement, not a calculation.

5.2 Does an efficient outcome mean nobody can be made better off?

Show answer

It means nobody can be made better off without making someone else worse off. Redistribution is always possible; it just does not grow the total.

06

An excise tax, or per-unit tax

★ Exam
Definition

A fixed amount of tax charged on each unit sold.

Textbook page 499.

Your textbook calls it an excise tax: “a tax on sales of a good or service”. Your course and the exam call the same thing a per-unit tax. Learn both names — they appear in different places and mean exactly the same thing.

The worked example throughout this module is Potterville, a city that puts a $40 per night tax on hotel rooms. Before the tax the market clears at $80 a room and 10,000 rooms a night.

Watch out. A per-unit tax is a fixed number of pounds or dollars per unit, not a percentage of the price. Everything in this module assumes the per-unit kind.

1

$40 per hotel room per night. Potterville's tax, and this module's example.

2

A fuel duty of 58p a litre. A real per-unit tax.

3

A 20% sales tax. Not a per-unit tax — it is a percentage. The diagrams would need a supply curve that pivots rather than shifts.

How it connects. It is imposed either on sellers (entry 7) or on buyers (entry 8), and either way it opens a wedge (entry 9).

Check yourself

6.1 A government charges £2 on every pack of cigarettes sold. Is this a per-unit tax?

Show answer

Yes — a fixed amount on each unit, regardless of the price.

6.2 Why does the module use a per-unit tax rather than a percentage one?

Show answer

Because a per-unit tax shifts the curve by the same vertical distance at every quantity, which keeps the diagram simple and the arithmetic exact.

07

A tax on sellers shifts supply up

★ Exam
Definition

A per-unit tax collected from sellers shifts the supply curve up by the amount of the tax: sellers need that much more per unit to supply the same quantity.

Textbook pages 500–501, Figure 50.6.

Before the tax, Potterville's hotel owners will supply 5,000 rooms at $60. After a $40 tax they will still supply 5,000 rooms — but only if they receive $100, because $40 of it goes straight to the city.

That is true at every quantity, so the whole supply curve shifts vertically up by $40. The new equilibrium is at $100 a room and 5,000 rooms. Owners keep $60.

Notice what did not happen: the price did not rise by the full $40. It rose by $20.

Watch out. Measure the shift vertically, and make it exactly the size of the tax. A shift drawn “a bit to the left” cannot be used to read off the prices, and reading off the prices is what exam questions want.

1

The $40 tax at Potterville. Supply up $40; price to $100; quantity to 5,000.

2

A $10 tax in the same market. Supply up $10; price to $85; sellers keep $75; quantity 7,500 — wait, check it on the diagram above.

3

A tax on a perfectly inelastic supply. The curve is vertical, so shifting it up changes nothing about the quantity. Sellers bear the whole tax.

How it connects. It is one way of imposing a per-unit tax (entry 6), it gives the same answer as a tax on buyers (entry 8), and the gap it opens is reversed by a subsidy (entry 16).

Check yourself

7.1 A $6 per-unit tax is imposed on sellers. By how much does the supply curve shift, and in which direction?

Show answer

Up by $6, measured vertically at every quantity.

7.2 Why does the equilibrium price rise by less than the tax?

Show answer

Because the quantity falls as the price rises, so the market settles part-way. How far depends on the elasticities — entry 11.

08

A tax on buyers shifts demand down

★ Exam
Definition

A per-unit tax collected from buyers shifts the demand curve down by the amount of the tax — and produces exactly the same outcome as a tax on sellers.

Textbook page 502, Figure 50.7.

Suppose Potterville taxes the guests instead of the hotels. A guest willing to pay $100 in total can now only offer the hotel $60, because $40 goes to the city. So the demand curve shifts down by $40.

Work out the new equilibrium and you get: guests pay $100 in total, hotels receive $60, and 5,000 rooms are rented. That is the same answer as before, number for number.

The diagrams look different. The outcome is identical.

Watch out. The two diagrams are not interchangeable on an exam: if the question says the tax is on buyers, shift demand. But do not expect the answer to change — it will not.

1

The tax on hotels. Supply up $40. Guests pay $100, hotels keep $60.

2

The tax on guests. Demand down $40. Guests pay $100, hotels keep $60.

3

A payroll tax split between employer and employee. The same logic. How the law divides it does not decide who really pays.

How it connects. It is the other way of imposing a per-unit tax (entry 6), it shifts the curve that a tax on sellers (entry 7) does not, and together they make the point in who really pays (entry 10).

Check yourself

8.1 A $5 tax is placed on buyers. What happens to the demand curve?

Show answer

It shifts down by $5 vertically: at every quantity, the most buyers will now pay the seller is $5 less than before.

8.2 Two students draw the same tax, one shifting supply and one shifting demand. Who is right?

Show answer

Both, if each matched the diagram to who the tax was levied on — and they will get identical prices, quantities, revenue and deadweight loss.

09

The tax wedge

★ Exam
Definition

A per-unit tax drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive. The wedge is exactly the size of the tax.

Textbook page 501.

After Potterville's $40 tax there are two prices where there used to be one:

  • Guests pay $100 — the demand price of 5,000 rooms.
  • Hotels receive $60 — the supply price of 5,000 rooms.
  • The gap is $40: the tax.

Keep both prices on the diagram and label them. Every later quantity — revenue, the two surpluses, the deadweight loss — is read off one or the other.

Watch out. There is no single “price” after a tax. A question asking “what price do consumers pay?” and one asking “what price do producers receive?” have different answers, and they differ by exactly the tax.

1

$100 and $60 at Potterville. A $40 wedge.

2

A $10 tax with buyers paying $85. Sellers receive $75.

3

A tax that changes no quantity. The wedge is still the tax — it just does not cost anyone a trade.

How it connects. It is opened by the tax (entry 6), how it is shared is a separate question (entry 10), and its height is one side of both the revenue rectangle (entry 12) and the deadweight-loss triangle (entry 13).

Check yourself

9.1 Buyers pay $18 and sellers receive $13 after a per-unit tax. How big is the tax?

Show answer

$5 — the wedge is the tax.

9.2 Before a $40 tax the price was $80. Afterwards buyers pay $100. What do sellers get?

Show answer

$60. The wedge is $40, so the two prices sit $40 apart.

10

Who hands over the money does not decide who bears the tax

★ Exam
Definition

The incidence of a tax — who really bears it — is the same whether the government collects it from buyers or from sellers.

Textbook pages 502–503.

Entries 7 and 8 produced identical answers. That is not a coincidence in this example; it is a general result, and your textbook states it as one: the incidence of an excise tax “doesn't depend on who officially pays the tax.”

At Potterville the tax is levied on hotel owners, and yet guests end up paying $20 more a night. Half the tax has been passed on, without any law saying it should be.

So when a politician says a tax is “on business, not on families”, the claim is about paperwork, not about economics.

Watch out. Do not answer an incidence question by naming whoever the law names. The answer comes from the elasticities, which is the next entry.

1

Potterville's tax on hotels. Guests bear $20 of the $40 anyway.

2

The same tax on guests. Hotels still bear $20 of it.

3

Employer and employee payroll taxes. The split written into the law is not the split that happens.

How it connects. It follows from the two diagrams (entry 8) and the wedge they open (entry 9) giving the same answer, and what does decide the split is the elasticities (entry 11).

Check yourself

10.1 A tax is levied entirely on sellers. Can buyers end up bearing most of it?

Show answer

Yes — and they usually do when demand is more inelastic than supply. The law decides who writes the cheque, not who is poorer afterwards.

10.2 Why do the two diagrams give the same answer?

Show answer

Because both open the same wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers receive, and the market settles at the quantity where that wedge fits between the two curves.

11

Incidence depends on the elasticities

★ Exam
Definition

The more inelastic side of the market bears more of a per-unit tax.

Textbook pages 503–505, Figures 50.8 and 50.9.

Economists call this question tax incidence, and your course answers it in one line that covers subsidies as well as taxes: the incidence “depends on the elasticity of supply and demand”.

The intuition is escape. An elastic side can walk away — buyers switch to substitutes, sellers move resources elsewhere — so the market cannot put much of the tax on them. An inelastic side has nowhere to go and absorbs it.

  • Demand inelastic, supply elastic → buyers bear most of it. Petrol.
  • Demand elastic, supply inelastic → sellers bear most of it. Your textbook's example is parking spaces in a downtown area.
  • Equally elastic → an even split, as at Potterville.

Try the three markets below: the same $40 tax, the same starting equilibrium, three different splits.

Watch out. “Inelastic” means bearing more of the tax, not less. Students reason that an unresponsive side is protected; the opposite is true, because it cannot escape.

1

Petrol: inelastic demand. Buyers bear most of a fuel duty.

2

Downtown parking: inelastic supply. Owners bear most of a parking tax.

3

Perfectly inelastic supply. Sellers bear the whole tax, the quantity does not change, and there is no deadweight loss at all.

How it connects. It settles the question who really pays (entry 10) leaves open, it decides whether a deadweight loss (entry 15) arises at all, and because it is an elasticity statement it applies unchanged to a subsidy (entry 16).

Check yourself

11.1 A tax is placed on a good with very inelastic demand and very elastic supply. Who bears most of it?

Show answer

Buyers. They cannot easily do without the good, so the price they pay rises by most of the tax.

11.2 Why does a perfectly inelastic supply mean sellers bear the whole tax?

Show answer

Because the quantity cannot change, so the price buyers pay does not move at all. The entire tax comes out of what sellers receive.

12

Tax revenue is a rectangle

★ Exam
Definition

Government revenue = tax per unit × quantity traded after the tax. On the diagram it is the rectangle between the two prices, out to the new quantity.

Textbook page 505, Figure 50.10.

tax revenue = tax × quantity sold with the tax in place

At Potterville: $40 × 5,000 = $200,000 a night.

Two things make this harder than it looks. The height is the tax, not either price; and the width is the post-tax quantity, not the one the market started with. Using 10,000 rooms here is the commonest error on the whole topic.

And a warning the diagram makes obvious: raising the tax rate does not always raise revenue. At Potterville a $40 tax collects $200,000 and a $60 tax collects only $150,000, because the quantity falls faster than the rate rises.

Watch out. Use the new quantity. The rectangle sits to the left of where the market used to be, and its width is the quantity that still trades.

1

$40 × 5,000. $200,000 — your textbook's own figure.

2

$20 × 7,500. $150,000, from the same market at half the tax rate.

3

$60 × 2,500. $150,000 again — a higher rate raising the same money as a much lower one.

How it connects. Its height is the wedge (entry 9), it sits beside the deadweight-loss triangle (entry 13), it is the revenue half of the government's side of the account (entry 17), and for a subsidy it becomes a cost rather than a receipt (entry 16).

Check yourself

12.1 A $3 per-unit tax leaves 12,000 units trading, down from 15,000. What is the revenue?

Show answer

$3 × 12,000 = $36,000. The 15,000 is the pre-tax quantity and plays no part.

12.2 Why might a government raise a tax rate and collect less?

Show answer

Because the quantity traded falls as the rate rises. If it falls proportionally faster than the rate rises, revenue goes down.

13

Deadweight loss from a tax

★ Exam
Definition

The fall in total surplus caused by the tax, minus the revenue the government collects. On the diagram it is a triangle.

Textbook pages 506–507, Figures 50.11 and 50.12.

A tax takes surplus from buyers and sellers. Some of it reaches the government — that part is a transfer, not a loss to society. The rest reaches nobody, because the trades that would have generated it no longer happen.

deadweight loss = ½ × (fall in quantity) × tax

At Potterville: ½ × 5,000 × $40 = $100,000 a night. Five thousand room rentals that were worth more to guests than they cost hotels simply do not take place.

The triangle's base is the tax wedge and its height is the fall in quantity — so a bigger tax makes it bigger twice over.

Watch out. Deadweight loss is not the tax revenue, and it is not the total fall in surplus either. It is the part that nobody receives.

1

The $40 tax at Potterville. ½ × 5,000 × $40 = $100,000.

2

The $60 tax. ½ × 7,500 × $60 = $225,000 — more than twice the loss for one and a half times the rate.

3

A tax on a perfectly inelastic good. No fall in quantity, so no triangle. The tax is a pure transfer.

How it connects. It measures a loss of total surplus (entry 1), it is caused by the third of the three experiments (entry 3), its base is the wedge (entry 9) and its partner on the diagram is the revenue rectangle (entry 12), it is the part of the account (entry 14) nobody receives, and it vanishes in one special case (entry 15).

Check yourself

13.1 A $5 tax cuts the quantity traded from 900 to 700. What is the deadweight loss?

Show answer

½ × 200 × $5 = $500.

13.2 Why is the government's revenue not part of the deadweight loss?

Show answer

Because somebody receives it. It leaves buyers and sellers and arrives at the government, so it is a transfer within society, not a loss to it.

14

The full surplus account

★ Exam
Definition

Loss to buyers + loss to sellers = government revenue + deadweight loss. The account always balances.

Textbook pages 506–507, Figure 50.11.

Potterville's $40 tax, in full — numbers your textbook draws but never computes:

Before the taxAfter the taxChange
Consumer surplus$200,000$50,000−$150,000
Producer surplus$200,000$50,000−$150,000
Government revenue$0$200,000+$200,000
Total surplus$400,000$300,000−$100,000

Buyers and sellers lose $300,000 between them. The government gains $200,000 of it. The missing $100,000 is the deadweight loss — and it matches the triangle exactly.

Watch out. Count the government's revenue as part of the post-tax total. Leaving it out makes the “loss” look three times bigger than it is.

1

Buyers' loss. $150,000: $100,000 of it paid in tax, $50,000 of surplus simply gone.

2

Sellers' loss. The same, mirrored.

3

The balance. $300,000 lost = $200,000 collected + $100,000 destroyed.

How it connects. It is the bookkeeping behind total surplus (entry 1), it adds up the triangle (entry 13), it has the government's rectangle (entry 17) as one of its lines, and it is what makes the no-loss case (entry 15) a residual rather than a guess.

Check yourself

14.1 A tax cuts consumer surplus by $900 and producer surplus by $700, and raises $1,300. What is the deadweight loss?

Show answer

$1,600 − $1,300 = $300.

14.2 Why does the account have to balance?

Show answer

Because every pound of surplus either stays with buyers and sellers, moves to the government, or disappears with a trade that no longer happens. There is nowhere else for it to go.

15

When a tax causes no deadweight loss

★ Exam
Definition

If the tax does not change the quantity traded, the triangle has no height and the tax is a pure transfer.

Textbook page 507.

Your textbook makes the point explicitly: “if a tax does not discourage transactions, it causes no deadweight loss. In this case, the tax simply shifts surplus straight from consumers and producers to the government.”

When does that happen? When one side of the market is perfectly inelastic. A vertical supply curve — land, say — means the quantity cannot fall, so no trades are lost. The deadweight loss is zero and the sellers bear the whole tax.

It is the cleanest link between this module and the elasticities of Module 48.

Watch out. No deadweight loss does not mean nobody is worse off. Sellers in the land example lose the entire tax — they simply lose it as a transfer rather than as destroyed value.

1

A tax on land. Supply is vertical; quantity unchanged; no deadweight loss.

2

A tax on a good with very elastic demand. Quantity falls a lot, so the triangle is large.

3

A tiny tax on anything. A small fall in quantity and a very small triangle — the loss grows faster than the rate.

How it connects. It is the limiting case of deadweight loss (entry 13), it is the line in the account (entry 14) that goes to zero, it is why elasticity (entry 11) is the determining factor rather than the rate alone, and a subsidy (entry 16) can never reach it.

Check yourself

15.1 Supply is perfectly inelastic and a per-unit tax is imposed. What is the deadweight loss, and who bears the tax?

Show answer

Zero deadweight loss, and sellers bear all of it. The price buyers pay does not change at all.

15.2 Does a zero deadweight loss make a tax a good idea?

Show answer

It makes it efficient, which is not the same as desirable. Somebody still bears it, and whether that is fair is the equity question of entry 5.

16

Subsidies — the mirror image

★ ⚑
Definition

A per-unit subsidy is a payment on each unit sold. Supply shifts down by the subsidy, buyers pay less, sellers receive more, and the quantity rises above the efficient level.

Not in Module 50 — the word does not appear in it.

⚑ Not in Module 50 — learn it from here. Your course says governments use taxes and subsidies, that both affect government revenues or costs, and that the incidence of both depends on the elasticities. The word subsidy appears nowhere in Module 50 — while it appears 59 times across the released exam papers in this project. Everything above is the tax case with the signs reversed.

Your course names taxes and subsidies three times over, and the exam tests subsidies as often as it tests consumer surplus. Your textbook's module on intervention never mentions one. The economics is the tax case in reverse, and it is worth learning as exactly that.

Give Potterville's hotels a $40 per room subsidy instead of taxing them:

  • Supply shifts down by $40.
  • Guests pay $60; hotels receive $100 including the subsidy; the wedge is $40 again, but the other way up.
  • The quantity rises to 15,000 rooms — above the efficient 10,000.
  • The government has a cost of $40 × 15,000 = $600,000.
  • There is still a deadweight loss: ½ × 5,000 × $40 = $100,000, because the last 5,000 rooms cost more to supply than guests value them at.

Two things do not mirror: the government pays rather than collects, and the quantity ends up too high rather than too low.

Watch out. A subsidy is not “free money that makes everyone better off”. Consumer and producer surplus both rise, but by less than the subsidy costs — and the difference is the deadweight loss.

1

$40 subsidy at Potterville. Guests pay $60, hotels get $100, 15,000 rooms.

2

The government's bill. $40 × 15,000 = $600,000 — larger than the tax's revenue.

3

Who benefits more? The more inelastic side, exactly as with a tax. The incidence rule does not change.

How it connects. It reverses the supply shift of a tax (entry 7), it obeys the incidence rule (entry 11) unchanged, it turns the revenue rectangle (entry 12) into a government cost (entry 17), and like a tax it moves the market away from the quantity that avoids a deadweight loss (entry 15).

Check yourself

16.1 A $2 per-unit subsidy is given to sellers. What happens to the supply curve, and to the price buyers pay?

Show answer

Supply shifts down by $2, and the price buyers pay falls — by less than $2, with the rest showing up as a higher price received by sellers.

16.2 Why does a subsidy create a deadweight loss when it makes both buyers and sellers better off?

Show answer

Because it pushes the quantity past the efficient level. The extra units cost more to produce than they are worth to buyers, and the taxpayer covers the difference.

17

Government revenue, or government cost

★ ⚑
Definition

A tax raises revenue for the government. A subsidy costs it money. Both are rectangles: the per-unit amount × the quantity traded.

Not in Module 50 — it computes a tax's revenue and never a subsidy's cost.

⚑ Not in Module 50 — learn it from here. Your course's wording is “taxes and subsidies affect government revenues or costs”. Module 50 computes a tax's revenue carefully and never mentions that the other half of the pair runs the other way, because it never mentions subsidies at all.

Your course puts the two together in one sentence: taxes and subsidies “affect government revenues or costs”. The rectangle is drawn the same way in both cases:

Note the asymmetry in size: the subsidy's rectangle is wider, because a subsidy increases the quantity while a tax reduces it. Subsidies are expensive for exactly that reason.

Watch out. Use the post-intervention quantity for both. For a subsidy that is the larger quantity, which is why the government's bill grows faster than people expect.

1

A $40 tax. Revenue $40 × 5,000 = $200,000.

2

A $40 subsidy. Cost $40 × 15,000 = $600,000.

3

A subsidy on a good with elastic demand. Quantity rises a lot, so the bill is larger still.

How it connects. It is the government's line in the account (entry 14), it is the receipt side of a tax (entry 12), and the paying side of a subsidy (entry 16), with one cost the diagram never shows (entry 18) as its final entry.

Check yourself

17.1 A £1.50 per-unit subsidy raises the quantity traded from 40,000 to 52,000. What does it cost the government?

Show answer

£1.50 × 52,000 = £78,000. Use the quantity after the subsidy.

17.2 Why is a subsidy's rectangle usually bigger than a tax's at the same per-unit rate?

Show answer

Because the subsidy raises the quantity while the tax lowers it. The same per-unit amount is multiplied by a larger number.

18

Administrative costs

○ Background
Definition

The resources used by government to collect a tax and by taxpayers to pay it — over and above the tax itself.

Textbook page 508.

The deadweight-loss triangle is not the whole cost of a tax. There is also the time people spend filling in forms, the accountants they hire, the inspectors the government employs, and the resources spent avoiding the tax altogether.

Your textbook's familiar example is an income tax return: the hours and the accountants' fees are real resources that produce nothing. They are a cost of the tax that no diagram shows.

You will not be asked to compute them. You may be asked to recognise that a tax's total cost is bigger than the triangle.

Watch out. Administrative costs are not the tax, and they are not the deadweight loss on the diagram. They are a third thing, sitting outside the graph entirely.

1

Hours spent on a tax return. Real resources, no output.

2

An accountant hired to file it. Your textbook's point: that accountant could have been doing something else.

3

Resources spent evading a tax. Also administrative cost, legal or not.

How it connects. They are a cost that the government's rectangle (entry 17) never records.

Check yourself

18.1 Are administrative costs part of the deadweight-loss triangle?

Show answer

No. The triangle measures the value of trades that no longer happen. Administrative costs are additional, and the diagram does not show them.

18.2 Why does a simpler tax tend to cost society less?

Show answer

Because the administrative costs fall: less time, fewer accountants, less evasion. The triangle may be unchanged while the real cost drops.

19

Progressive and regressive taxes

○ Background
Definition

A progressive tax takes a larger share of a richer taxpayer's income; a regressive tax takes a smaller share.

Textbook page 499.

Your textbook introduces these terms while discussing equity, and the US tax system is described as “a mixture of progressive and regressive taxes, though it is somewhat progressive overall”.

They belong to the equity conversation, not the efficiency one: they describe how a tax burden is distributed across incomes, not how much surplus it destroys.

Neither term is part of your Microeconomics course. They appear here because your textbook raises them, and because per-unit taxes on everyday goods tend to be regressive — which is worth knowing when you argue about a policy.

Watch out. Progressive and regressive are about shares of income, not about amounts of money. A richer household can pay more in pounds under a regressive tax and still pay a smaller share.

1

An income tax with rising rate bands. Progressive.

2

A flat per-unit duty on fuel. Regressive — poorer households spend a larger share of their income on fuel.

3

A tax taking exactly 10% from everyone. Neither: proportional.

How it connects. They belong with the equity question (entry 5) rather than with efficiency.

Check yourself

19.1 A duty takes 3% of a poor household's income and 0.5% of a rich one's. Progressive or regressive?

Show answer

Regressive — the share falls as income rises, even though the rich household may pay more in cash.

19.2 Is a regressive tax necessarily inefficient?

Show answer

No. Efficiency is about the deadweight loss; progressivity is about who bears the burden. A tax can be regressive and efficient, or progressive and wasteful.

Things to get right

  • Equilibrium maximises the total, not consumer surplus and not producer surplus.
  • Shift by the size of the tax, vertically. Supply up for a tax on sellers, demand down for a tax on buyers, supply down for a subsidy.
  • Two prices after a tax. Say which one a question is asking for.
  • Revenue uses the post-tax quantity. The commonest arithmetic slip on this topic.
  • Revenue is a transfer; deadweight loss is not. Keep them apart.
  • The inelastic side bears more of a tax — and gains more from a subsidy.
  • A subsidy has a government cost, pushes quantity above the efficient level, and still creates a deadweight loss.
  • Check the account. Losses to buyers and sellers = government revenue + deadweight loss.
