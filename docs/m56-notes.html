The headline. Module 56 is well aligned on three of Topic 3.3's four statements, and the fourth is missing from the whole book. The module teaches that every input, and so every cost, is variable in the long run (PRD-1.A.9), defines all three returns to scale (PRD-1.A.10), and builds the long-run average total cost curve with its economies and diseconomies of scale (PRD-1.A.11) — on Selena's salsa, with the book's own numbers. PRD-1.A.12 is not in the book. "The minimum efficient scale plays a role in determining the concentration of firms in a market and the market structure." Minimum efficient scale occurs 0 times in all 927 pages and 0 times on the eight released papers; efficient scale, the CED's name for the flat bottom of LRATC in PRD-1.A.11, is also 0 and 0. The module never mentions market structure or concentration (0 times each in pp. 559–566). The link appears only in words, a module later: economies of scale as a barrier to entry (p. 571) and as "the most important source of oligopolies" (p. 573). The guide's entries 12, 13 and 14 supply all of it. One slip to correct. p. 562 says Selena's "decreasing returns to scale occur at output levels greater than 6 cases". The curve shows diseconomies of scale there; nothing in the example says that all inputs rose in the same proportion. Krugman's own next sentence makes the distinction.

The four essential knowledge statements this topic carries, verbatim (CED p. 64):

PRD-1.A.9 — "In the long run, firms can adjust all their inputs, and as a result, all costs become variable." PRD-1.A.10 — "The relationship between inputs and outputs in the long run is described by the scale of production--increasing, decreasing, or constant returns to scale." PRD-1.A.11 — "The long-run average total cost is characterized by economies of scale, diseconomies of scale, or constant returns to scale (efficient scale)." PRD-1.A.12 — "The minimum efficient scale plays a role in determining the concentration of firms in a market and the market structure."

The margin definitions, in Krugman's own words

Long-run average total cost curve (p. 561) — "The long-run average total cost curve shows the relationship between output and average total cost when fixed cost has been chosen to minimize average total cost for each level of output."
Economies of scale (p. 562) — "There are economies of scale when long-run average total cost declines as output increases."
Increasing returns to scale (p. 562) — "There are increasing returns to scale when output increases more than in proportion to an increase in all inputs."
Diseconomies of scale (p. 562) — "There are diseconomies of scale when long-run average total cost increases as output increases."
Decreasing returns to scale (p. 563) — "There are decreasing returns to scale when output increases less than in proportion to an increase in all inputs."
Constant returns to scale (p. 563) — "There are constant returns to scale when output increases directly in proportion to an increase in all inputs."
Sunk cost (p. 563) — "A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and is nonrecoverable. A sunk cost should be ignored in a decision about future actions."

Two points about the wording. Krugman keeps returns to scale (inputs and output) and economies of scale (cost) apart, and says how they connect: "Increasing returns to scale therefore imply economies of scale, although economies of scale exist whenever long-run average total cost is falling, whether or not all inputs are increasing by the same proportion" (p. 562). The CED's PRD-1.A.11 blurs them — "economies of scale, diseconomies of scale, or constant returns to scale" — so expect the exam to use constant returns to scale for the flat part of LRATC. And the CED's parenthesis, "(efficient scale)", names that flat part; the book does not.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Diseconomies of scale are not decreasing returns to scale

p. 562 says of Selena: "decreasing returns to scale occur at output levels greater than 6 cases". What the curve shows beyond 6 cases is diseconomies of scale: LRATC rising. Decreasing returns to scale is a claim about inputs — all of them up by a proportion, output up by less — and the example says nothing about inputs. Krugman's next sentence draws the right line: diseconomies "can result from" decreasing returns. Teach the pair as cause and effect, never as synonyms.

LRATC is U-shaped because of scale, not diminishing returns

Diminishing marginal returns need a fixed input; in the long run there is none. Students who explain the rising part of LRATC with "diminishing returns" have carried Module 54 into the wrong run. The causes are economies of scale (specialization, set-up costs) and diseconomies (coordination).

The long run is not a length of time

MCQ 2's key — no period "is always considered the long run" — is the point. A food truck reaches its long run in days, a steel mill in years.

Constant returns to scale is the CED's name for the flat part of LRATC

The CED writes "constant returns to scale (efficient scale)" in a list of cost behaviours. Accept it: on the exam, a flat stretch of LRATC is constant returns to scale, and its first point is the minimum efficient scale.

Fixed cost and sunk cost

The framework writes "fixed costs (sunk costs)" (CBA-2.B.2, CED p. 40) and tests one point with the pair: neither changes the best decision. Krugman separates them here (p. 563): a fixed cost does not change with output; a sunk cost cannot be recovered whatever the firm does. In the long run a firm can avoid a fixed cost — sell the plant — but never a sunk one. Teach both sentences; do not mark down a student who uses the framework's parenthesis.

Where a short-run curve touches LRATC

Each short-run curve touches LRATC at one output and lies above it everywhere else. Strictly, a plant built for an output on the falling part of LRATC touches it a little to the left of the short-run curve's own minimum, and on the rising part a little to the right; only at the bottom of LRATC do the two minimums coincide. The guide's diagram is drawn that way. Exam rubrics ask only that the short-run curves lie on or above LRATC and touch it, so do not mark down the simpler drawing in which each short-run minimum sits on LRATC.

third diagram (10 min).

  1. FRQ 1 (5 min, or homework).
