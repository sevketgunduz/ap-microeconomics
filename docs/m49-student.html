Consumer and Producer Surplus

Sixteen terms from Module 49 of Krugman’s Economics for AP. How much do buyers and sellers actually gain from a market? Two definitions, two areas, and the measure the rest of this course judges markets by.

Textbook pages 482–494·16 terms· Topic 2.6·2 periods

How to use this guide

Read Module 49 in your textbook first. Entries 1 to 6 are the buyers' side; entries 7 to 13 are the sellers' side, built the same way; entries 14 to 16 put the two together and show you how to compute them.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 49 of your textbook does not teach it. Entry 15 carries the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. A trade happens only when the buyer values the good more than the seller does. Everything between those two numbers is a gain, and the price decides how it is split: consumer surplus above the price, producer surplus below it.

⚑ Your textbook stops one step short. It computes consumer surplus and producer surplus and never adds them up — the phrase total economic surplus appears nowhere in its 927 pages, and the word efficient never appears in this module. Your course asks for both. Entry 15 supplies them, and the next module proves the argument.

The market this module is built on

Five students want a used textbook and five have one to sell. Each buyer has a maximum price and each seller a minimum:

Sellers — Andrew $5 · Betty $15 · Carlos $25 · Donna $35 · Engelbert $45

Pick a price. The blue blocks are what buyers gain, the green blocks are what sellers gain, and the dashed line is the price itself.

The sixteen terms

01

Willingness to pay

★ Exam
Definition

The most a buyer would pay for a good rather than go without it.

Textbook page 483.

Your textbook lines up five students who want the same used textbook and asks each the same question: what is the highest price at which you would still buy? Aleisha answers $59, Brad $45, Claudia $35, Darren $25 and Edwina $10.

At exactly that price a buyer is indifferent — buying and not buying are equally good. One penny more and they walk away.

Your course never uses the phrase, but exam questions do, and everything in this module is built on it.

Watch out. Willingness to pay is not what the buyer expects to pay, and it is not what they want to pay. It is the maximum — the point at which they stop caring either way.

1

Aleisha, $59. She buys at any price up to $59 and not a penny above.

2

Edwina, $10. The same book, the same shop — a very different number.

3

You, for a bottle of water after a long walk. Higher than it would be for the same bottle at home. Willingness to pay belongs to a person and a moment.

How it connects. It is what the demand curve (entry 2) is built from, it is one side of the subtraction in consumer surplus (entry 3), and its mirror image on the selling side is a seller's cost (entry 7).

Check yourself

1.1 Two people face the same price and one buys while the other does not. What does that tell you?

Show answer

That their willingnesses to pay differ, and that the price lies between them.

1.2 A buyer's willingness to pay is exactly the price. Do they buy?

Show answer

They are indifferent, so either answer is fine — and either way they gain nothing from the purchase. That case matters in entry 14.

02

The demand curve as a list of willingness to pay

★ Exam
Definition

Every point on a demand curve is some buyer's maximum price. The curve slopes downward because buyers are lined up from the most eager to the least.

Textbook page 484, Figure 49.1.

Sort the five students from highest willingness to pay to lowest and ask how many would buy at each price. At $59 only Aleisha. At $45 Aleisha and Brad. At $35, three of them. The answer is a step-shaped demand curve, one step per buyer, each step as high as that buyer's maximum price.

With five buyers the steps are obvious. With five million they are invisible, and the curve looks smooth — but it is still a list of maximum prices read from the top down.

Watch out. The height of the demand curve at a quantity is the willingness to pay of the last buyer served, not of the first. That is why the curve falls as you move right.

1

The first step, $59. Aleisha: the quantity demanded is 1 at any price up to $59.

2

The third step, $35. Claudia. At $35 three books are demanded.

3

A smooth demand curve for petrol. Millions of steps, each one a driver's maximum price. Same idea, finer grain.

How it connects. It is drawn from each buyer's maximum price (entry 1), the gap between it and the price is the consumer-surplus area (entry 5), and the selling side has exactly the same construction (entry 9).

Check yourself

2.1 A demand curve is at $12 when the quantity is 400. What does that say about the 400th buyer?

Show answer

Their willingness to pay is $12 — they are the last buyer willing to take a unit at that price.

2.2 Why is the demand curve a staircase here and a smooth line in most diagrams?

Show answer

Because there are only five buyers. With many buyers the steps become too small to see, and we draw the smooth line they add up to.

03

Individual consumer surplus

★ Exam
Definition

A buyer's net gain: willingness to pay minus the price actually paid.

Textbook pages 484–485, Table 49.1.

individual consumer surplus = willingness to pay − price paid

Set the price at $30 and look at what each student gains:

BuyerWillingness to payPrice paidConsumer surplus
Aleisha$59$30$29
Brad$45$30$15
Claudia$35$30$5
Darren$25—does not buy
Edwina$10—does not buy

Darren and Edwina are not worse off — they simply do not trade, so they gain nothing.

Watch out. Surplus is measured in money and it is a difference. If a question gives you a price and you answer with that price, you have reported what the buyer paid, not what they gained.

1

Aleisha at $30. $59 − $30 = $29.

2

Claudia at $30. $35 − $30 = $5. A real gain, just a small one.

3

Darren at $30. Nothing. His maximum is $25, so he does not buy — and he is no worse off for it.

How it connects. It is the difference between willingness to pay (entry 1) and the price, the sum of every buyer's is total consumer surplus (entry 4), and the seller's version of it is individual producer surplus (entry 10).

Check yourself

3.1 A buyer would have paid £40 and pays £26. What is their consumer surplus?

Show answer

£14.

3.2 Why does a buyer who does not buy have a consumer surplus of zero rather than a negative one?

Show answer

Because nobody is forced to trade. Not buying leaves you exactly where you started, so the gain is zero, never a loss.

04

Total consumer surplus

★ Exam
Definition

The sum of every buyer's individual consumer surplus in the market.

Textbook page 485.

At $30 the three buyers who trade gain $29, $15 and $5. Add them:

$29 + $15 + $5 = $49

That is the total consumer surplus in this market at a price of $30 — the whole gain to buyers from the market existing at all.

Your textbook warns that economists say “consumer surplus” for both the individual and the total, and leave it to context. On an exam it almost always means the total.

Watch out. Add up only the buyers who actually trade. Including Darren and Edwina adds nothing — they are at zero — but a student who writes “$59 + $45 + $35” has added willingness to pay instead of surplus.

1

Three buyers at $30. $29 + $15 + $5 = $49.

2

The same market at $45. Only Aleisha and Brad buy, and Brad gains nothing: $14 + $0 = $14.

3

The same market at $10. Everyone would buy, but only one book is for sale at $10.

How it connects. It sums the individual gains (entry 3), it is drawn as an area (entry 5), it is half of total economic surplus (entry 15), and its mirror image is total producer surplus (entry 11).

Check yourself

4.1 Four buyers gain $12, $8, $3 and $0. What is total consumer surplus?

Show answer

$23. The buyer gaining $0 is still a buyer; they just add nothing.

4.2 The price falls and a new buyer enters. What happens to total consumer surplus?

Show answer

It rises twice over: every existing buyer gains more, and the new buyer adds a gain of their own. That is entry 6.

05

Consumer surplus as an area

★ Exam
Definition

On a diagram, total consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price.

Textbook pages 485–487, Figures 49.2 and 49.3.

Aleisha's gain is a rectangle one book wide and $29 tall. Brad's sits beside it, $15 tall; Claudia's, $5 tall. Together they are a staircase — and the staircase is the area between the demand curve and the $30 line.

With many buyers the staircase becomes a smooth triangle:

consumer surplus = ½ × quantity × (demand intercept − price)

Your textbook states the rule as a general principle, and it is the sentence to memorise: consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve but above the price.

Watch out. Below demand, above price. Students who shade below the price have drawn what buyers spent, not what they gained.

1

The textbook market at $30. Three stacked rectangles: $29 + $15 + $5 = $49.

2

A straight-line demand curve from $80, with 200 bought at $40. ½ × 200 × $40 = $4,000 — your textbook's own review question.

3

A price so high that nobody buys. The area vanishes. Consumer surplus is zero.

How it connects. It is the picture of total consumer surplus (entry 4), it is bounded above by the demand curve (entry 2), it mirrors the producer-surplus area (entry 12), it changes when the price falls (entry 6), and it is computed in two shapes (entry 16).

Check yourself

5.1 A demand curve runs from $50 on the price axis; at a price of $20, 300 units are bought. What is consumer surplus?

Show answer

½ × 300 × ($50 − $20) = $4,500.

5.2 Why does the consumer-surplus triangle close to a point at the equilibrium quantity?

Show answer

Because the last buyer's willingness to pay is the price itself, so their gain is zero — the idea in entry 14.

06

A price fall raises consumer surplus

★ Exam
Definition

A lower price adds two pieces of surplus: a rectangle for buyers who were already buying, and a triangle for buyers who were not.

Textbook pages 487–488, Figures 49.4 and 49.5.

Drop the price of a used textbook from $30 to $20 and two different things happen.

  • Existing buyers — Aleisha, Brad and Claudia — each gain $10 more. Three buyers × $10 = a rectangle.
  • A new buyer — Darren, whose maximum is $25 — now trades and gains $5. That is the triangle.

A price rise does the reverse: existing buyers lose a rectangle, and the buyers squeezed out lose a triangle.

Watch out. Answer with both pieces. “Consumer surplus rises because the price is lower” collects the rectangle and misses the new buyers entirely, which is half the marks on a change-in-surplus question.

1

$30 → $20, existing buyers. Three buyers × $10 = $30 extra.

2

$30 → $20, the new buyer. Darren gains $25 − $20 = $5.

3

A rise from $30 to $40. Claudia drops out and loses her $5; Aleisha and Brad each lose $10.

How it connects. It moves the lower boundary of the consumer-surplus area (entry 5), and its exact mirror image on the selling side is a price rise raising producer surplus (entry 13).

Check yourself

6.1 A price falls and consumer surplus rises by a rectangle of $600 plus a triangle of $50. By how much did it rise?

Show answer

$650. Both pieces count.

6.2 Why is the piece belonging to new buyers a triangle rather than a rectangle?

Show answer

Because each new buyer gains a different amount — the first new buyer nearly as much as the old ones, the last one almost nothing. Stacked together they taper to a point.

07

A seller's cost

★ Exam
Definition

The lowest price at which a seller is willing to part with the good.

Textbook page 489.

The selling side of the same market. Five students each have a copy to sell, and each has a different minimum: Andrew $5, Betty $15, Carlos $25, Donna $35, Engelbert $45.

Your textbook calls this the seller's cost, and the word is deliberate. It is the exact mirror of willingness to pay: the price at which the seller is indifferent between selling and keeping.

Watch out. A seller's cost is a minimum price, not a bill they have paid. Nobody manufactured these textbooks — see the next entry.

1

Andrew, $5. He will sell at any price from $5 upwards.

2

Engelbert, $45. The same book, and he would rather keep it below $45.

3

A farmer's cost of a bushel of wheat. Here it really is a production cost — but the definition is the same: the lowest acceptable price.

How it connects. It mirrors willingness to pay (entry 1), it is an opportunity cost (entry 8) rather than a bill, the supply curve (entry 9) is built from it, and subtracting it from the price gives individual producer surplus (entry 10).

Check yourself

7.1 A seller's cost is $18 and the market price is $18. Do they sell, and what do they gain?

Show answer

They are indifferent, so either answer is fine — and their gain is zero either way.

7.2 Why is a seller's cost a minimum rather than a maximum?

Show answer

Because sellers are better off the higher the price. The binding question for them is how low they would go.

08

Cost means opportunity cost

○ Background
Definition

The seller's cost is what they give up by selling — not money they have spent.

Textbook page 489.

Your textbook meets the objection head-on. A student selling a used textbook spent nothing to make it available: the book already exists and is already theirs. So what is the “cost” of selling it?

It is not having it afterwards. The seller gives up a book they might have kept, lent or reread. That is an opportunity cost, and Unit 1's rule applies: the true cost of doing something is always what you must give up to do it.

In most real markets the seller is also the producer, so the cost includes money spent — but it still includes the opportunity costs on top.

Watch out. You will not be asked to define opportunity cost here. You will be asked why a supply curve slopes upward, and this is the answer: the sellers with the most to give up are the last to sell.

1

Andrew's $5. He barely wants the book. Giving it up costs him little.

2

Engelbert's $45. He values keeping it, so parting with it costs him a lot.

3

A bakery selling bread. Flour and labour are money costs; the oven time that could have baked cakes instead is an opportunity cost.

How it connects. It is what a seller's cost (entry 7) actually measures.

Check yourself

8.1 A gallery owner refuses £9,000 for a painting they bought for £2,000. What is their cost of selling?

Show answer

At least £9,000 — what they give up by no longer owning it. The £2,000 they paid is gone either way and does not enter the decision.

8.2 Why does a seller's cost not have to equal what they paid?

Show answer

Because the relevant cost is what they give up now, not what they spent then. Yesterday's price is sunk.

09

The supply curve as a list of costs

★ Exam
Definition

Every point on a supply curve is some seller's minimum price. The curve slopes upward because sellers are lined up from the cheapest to the dearest.

Textbook page 489, Figure 49.6.

Sort the five sellers from lowest cost to highest and ask how many would sell at each price. Below $5, nobody. Between $5 and $15, only Andrew. At $30, three of them. The result is a step-shaped supply curve, one step per seller, each step as high as that seller's cost.

It is the exact construction of entry 2, mirrored. Demand reads maximum prices from the top down; supply reads minimum prices from the bottom up.

Watch out. The height of the supply curve at a quantity is the cost of the last seller needed, not the cheapest one. That is why it rises as you move right.

1

The first step, $5. Andrew. One book is supplied at any price from $5.

2

The third step, $25. Carlos. At $25 three books are supplied.

3

A smooth supply curve for wheat. Thousands of farms, each with its own cost. The steps are invisible.

How it connects. It is built from sellers' costs (entry 7) exactly as the demand curve (entry 2) is built from willingness to pay, and the gap between it and the price is the producer-surplus area (entry 12).

Check yourself

9.1 A supply curve is at $8 when the quantity is 120. What does that say about the 120th unit?

Show answer

That the seller of that unit has a cost of $8 — they are the last seller worth bringing in at that price.

9.2 Why does a supply curve slope upward in this model?

Show answer

Because sellers are ordered by cost. Getting more units means reaching sellers who give up more, so the price has to rise.

Individual producer surplus

★ Exam
Definition

A seller's net gain: the price received minus their cost.

Textbook page 490, Table 49.2.

individual producer surplus = price received − seller's cost

At $30:

SellerCostPrice receivedProducer surplus
Andrew$5$30$25
Betty$15$30$15
Carlos$25$30$5
Donna$35—does not sell
Engelbert$45—does not sell

Watch out. Producer surplus is not profit. Nothing has been said about fixed costs, overheads or what the seller paid in the first place — only about the minimum price they would accept.

1

Andrew at $30. $30 − $5 = $25.

2

Carlos at $30. $30 − $25 = $5.

3

Donna at $30. Nothing — her minimum is $35, so she keeps her book.

How it connects. It subtracts the seller's cost (entry 7) from the price, it mirrors individual consumer surplus (entry 3) exactly, and the sum of all of them is total producer surplus (entry 11).

Check yourself

10.1 A seller would have accepted $14 and receives $22. What is their producer surplus?

Show answer

$8.

10.2 Two sellers receive the same price. Why might their producer surpluses differ?

Show answer

Because their costs differ. The seller who would have accepted less gains more from the same price.

11

Total producer surplus

★ Exam
Definition

The sum of every seller's individual producer surplus in the market.

Textbook page 490.

At $30 the three sellers who trade gain $25, $15 and $5:

$25 + $15 + $5 = $45

So at a price of $30 this market generates $49 of consumer surplus and $45 of producer surplus. Adding those two numbers is what the next module is about.

Watch out. As with buyers, count only the sellers who actually trade. Donna and Engelbert contribute zero, not a negative amount.

1

Three sellers at $30. $25 + $15 + $5 = $45.

2

The same market at $20. Only Andrew and Betty sell: $15 + $5 = $20.

3

The same market at $45. All five would sell — but only two buyers remain at that price.

How it connects. It sums the individual gains (entry 10), it mirrors total consumer surplus (entry 4), and it is drawn as an area (entry 12).

Check yourself

11.1 Three sellers gain $20, $9 and $0. What is total producer surplus?

Show answer

$29.

11.2 Why does total producer surplus rise when the price rises, while total consumer surplus falls?

Show answer

Because the price is the top of the sellers' gain and the bottom of the buyers'. Raising it enlarges one area and shrinks the other.

12

Producer surplus as an area

★ Exam
Definition

On a diagram, total producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the price.

Textbook pages 490–491, Figures 49.7 and 49.8.

Andrew's gain is a rectangle one book wide and $25 tall, sitting on top of his $5 step. Stack Betty's and Carlos's beside it and you have the area between the $30 line and the supply curve.

With many sellers it becomes a triangle:

producer surplus = ½ × quantity × (price − supply intercept)

Your textbook's wheat example: at $5 a bushel farmers supply 1 million bushels, and their producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below $5.

Watch out. Above supply, below price — the opposite of the consumer rule in both prepositions. Say the two rules together until they stop swapping.

1

The textbook market at $30. $25 + $15 + $5 = $45.

2

A supply curve from $20, with 50 sold at $60. ½ × 50 × $40 = $1,000 — your textbook's own review question.

3

A price below every seller's cost. Nothing is sold and producer surplus is zero.

How it connects. It is the picture of total producer surplus (entry 11), it mirrors the consumer-surplus area (entry 5), it is read off the supply curve (entry 9), it changes when the price rises (entry 13), and it is computed in two shapes (entry 16).

Check yourself

12.1 A supply curve starts at $6; at a price of $18, 400 units are sold. What is producer surplus?

Show answer

½ × 400 × ($18 − $6) = $2,400.

12.2 Which area is bigger in a market where the supply curve is very flat — consumer or producer surplus?

Show answer

Consumer surplus, usually. A flat supply curve leaves little room between it and the price, so sellers gain little and buyers keep most of the gain.

13

A price rise raises producer surplus

★ Exam
Definition

A higher price adds two pieces: a rectangle for sellers who were already selling, and a triangle for sellers newly drawn in.

Textbook page 492, Figure 49.9.

Your textbook's wheat market: the price rises from $5 to $7 a bushel and the quantity supplied rises from 1 million to 1.5 million.

  • Existing sellers gain $2 on every one of the 1 million bushels they were already growing — a rectangle of $2 million.
  • New sellers supply the extra 500,000 bushels and gain the difference between $7 and their own costs — a triangle.

A price fall runs the story backwards, and both pieces are losses.

Watch out. This is entry 6 with every sign reversed. If you find yourself learning two separate rules, you have learned one too many.

1

$5 → $7, existing sellers. $2 × 1 million = $2 million.

2

$5 → $7, new sellers. A triangle over the extra 500,000 bushels.

3

A fall from $7 to $5. The same two shapes, now losses: existing growers lose $2 a bushel, and some leave the market.

How it connects. It is the mirror image of a price fall raising consumer surplus (entry 6), and it moves the upper boundary of the producer-surplus area (entry 12).

Check yourself

13.1 Wheat rises from $4 to $6 and quantity supplied goes from 2 million to 2.4 million bushels. What is the rectangle worth?

Show answer

$2 × 2 million = $4 million, before adding the triangle for the extra 400,000 bushels.

13.2 A price rise brings in new sellers. Why do they gain less per unit than the existing ones?

Show answer

Because their costs are higher — that is why they were not selling before. The last one in gains almost nothing.

14

The buyer and seller at the margin gain nothing

★ Exam
Definition

The last buyer's willingness to pay equals the price, and the last seller's cost equals the price. Both gain exactly zero.

Textbook pages 485 and 490, and the review questions.

In your textbook's own review question, Josey's third pepper has a willingness to pay of $0.40 and the price is $0.40. She buys it and gains nothing. On the selling side, Jamie's third pepper costs him exactly $0.70 and the price is $0.70 — he sells and gains nothing.

This is not a quirk. It is why both surplus triangles come to a point at the equilibrium quantity, and it is the reason the next module can argue that the market stops in exactly the right place: the first trade not made would have been worth nothing to either side.

Watch out. Zero surplus is not a reason to stay out of the market. The marginal buyer is indifferent, not harmed — and including them changes no total.

1

Josey's third pepper at $0.40. Bought, and worth $0.00 to her.

2

Jamie's third pepper at $0.70. Sold, and worth $0.00 to him.

3

Brad at a price of $45. His maximum is exactly $45: he buys, and gains nothing.

How it connects. It is why the areas taper to a point, and it is the hinge of the argument in what surplus is for (entry 15), which you will finish in the calculation (entry 16).

Check yourself

14.1 Why does adding the marginal buyer not change total consumer surplus?

Show answer

Because their individual surplus is zero. They trade, but the gain they add is nothing.

14.2 What would it mean if the marginal buyer gained a lot?

Show answer

That the price is well below their willingness to pay — so there are buyers beyond them who would also trade, and the market has not reached the quantity where the two curves meet.

15

Total economic surplus, and what surplus is for

★ ⚑
Definition

Total economic surplus is consumer surplus plus producer surplus. Economists measure it to judge whether a market is efficient.

Not in Module 49 — your textbook computes the two halves and never adds them.

⚑ Not in Module 49 — learn it from here. Your course asks you to use consumer and producer surplus “to measure the benefits markets create … and understand market efficiency”, and says that market equilibrium maximises total economic surplus. Module 49 never adds the two surpluses together, never uses the word efficient, and the exact phrase total economic surplus appears nowhere in all 927 pages of your textbook. The next module supplies the whole argument.

At $30 the used-textbook market produces $49 of consumer surplus and $45 of producer surplus. The obvious next step is to add them:

total economic surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus = $49 + $45 = $94

That number is the whole gain the market creates — the value of every trade to everyone involved. Your course says markets should be judged by it: market equilibrium maximises total economic surplus, which is what it means to call a competitive market efficient.

Try it in the market above. Set the price to $25, $30 or $35 and total surplus is $94 every time, because three books trade in each case. Push the price outside that range and the total falls — not because the price is “wrong” for anyone in particular, but because fewer books change hands.

That is the whole idea, and the next module proves it properly.

Watch out. Total surplus depends on the quantity traded, not on how the gain is split. A price change inside the clearing range moves surplus between buyers and sellers without changing the total.

1

$30 in the textbook market. $49 + $45 = $94.

2

$35 in the same market. $34 of consumer surplus, $60 of producer surplus — still $94. The split moved; the total did not.

3

$45 in the same market. Only two books trade, and the total falls to $84. Two $10 gains have disappeared.

How it connects. It adds total consumer surplus (entry 4) to the producer side, the marginal trade adds nothing (entry 14) to it, and you compute it in the same two shapes (entry 16).

Check yourself

15.1 A market has consumer surplus of $320 and producer surplus of $180. What is total economic surplus?

Show answer

$500. Add the two; nothing else is needed.

15.2 Two prices give the same quantity traded. Will total surplus be the same?

Show answer

Yes. The price decides how the gain is divided between buyers and sellers; the quantity decides how big the gain is.

16

Calculating the areas

★ Exam
Definition

On a straight-line diagram each surplus is a triangle: ½ × base × height. On a table or a step diagram it is a sum of rectangles.

Textbook pages 486 and 491, and the review questions.

Your course asks you to calculate these areas, so the arithmetic matters.

On a graph. The base is the quantity traded; the height is the gap between the price and the curve's intercept.

  • Consumer surplus = ½ × quantity × (price intercept of demand − price)
  • Producer surplus = ½ × quantity × (price − price intercept of supply)

From a table. Work buyer by buyer or seller by seller, subtract, and add up. The textbook market is a table problem: $29 + $15 + $5.

A worked graph: demand from $80, supply from $10, 200 units traded at $40. Consumer surplus is ½ × 200 × $40 = $4,000; producer surplus is ½ × 200 × $30 = $3,000.

Watch out. Use the intercepts, not the axis ends. The height of the consumer triangle runs from the price up to where the demand curve meets the price axis — which is rarely the top of the drawn axis.

1

Demand from $80, price $40, 200 units. ½ × 200 × $40 = $4,000.

2

Supply from $10, price $40, 200 units. ½ × 200 × $30 = $3,000.

3

The textbook's five buyers at $30. Not a triangle at all — three rectangles: $29 + $15 + $5 = $49.

How it connects. It computes the consumer area (entry 5) and the producer area (entry 12), it is what makes total economic surplus (entry 15) a number rather than a picture, and the marginal trade contributes nothing (entry 14) to it.

Check yourself

16.1 Demand from $60, supply from $12, equilibrium at $30 with 400 units. Find both surpluses and the total.

Show answer

Consumer surplus ½ × 400 × $30 = $6,000. Producer surplus ½ × 400 × $18 = $3,600. Total economic surplus $9,600.

16.2 Why can you not use ½ × base × height on the five-buyer market?

Show answer

Because its demand curve is a staircase, not a straight line. Triangles are what staircases become when there are many buyers; with five, add the rectangles.

The two free-response questions

1. (6 points) A competitive market has a demand curve running from $80 on the price axis and a supply curve running from $10, crossing at a price of $40 with 200 units traded.

(a) Calculate consumer surplus. (b) Calculate producer surplus. (c) If supply increases, what happens to consumer surplus? Explain. (d) If demand decreases, what happens to producer surplus? Explain.

Show the mark scheme

1 point — consumer surplus $4,000: ½ × 200 × ($80 − $40).

1 point — producer surplus $3,000: ½ × 200 × ($40 − $10).

1 point — consumer surplus will increase.

1 point — because an increase in supply lowers the equilibrium price, and a lower price enlarges the area below the demand curve and above the price.

1 point — producer surplus will decrease.

1 point — because a decrease in demand lowers the equilibrium price, and a lower price shrinks the area above the supply curve and below the price.

Both explanations run through the price. A shift moves the equilibrium price, and the price is one side of each triangle. Say that sentence and the marks follow.

2. Draw a correctly labelled graph showing a competitive market in equilibrium. Clearly indicate and label the area of consumer surplus and the area of producer surplus.

Show the mark scheme

Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal. A downward-sloping demand curve labelled D and an upward-sloping supply curve labelled S, crossing at a labelled equilibrium price and quantity.

Consumer surplus shaded and labelled: the triangle between the demand curve and the equilibrium price, to the left of the equilibrium quantity.

Producer surplus shaded and labelled: the triangle between the equilibrium price and the supply curve.

Mark the two areas separately and name them. An unlabelled shading earns nothing, however correct the geometry.

Things to get right

  • Below demand, above price. Consumer surplus. Say it until it is automatic.
  • Above supply, below price. Producer surplus. Both prepositions flip.
  • Surplus is a difference, measured in money. Not a price, not a quantity, not spending.
  • ½ × base × height on a straight-line graph; a sum of rectangles from a table.
  • Use the intercepts for the height, not the top of the drawn axis.
  • A price change gives a rectangle and a triangle. Both count.
  • The marginal trader gains nothing, which is why each area tapers to a point.
  • Total economic surplus is the two added together — your course's phrase, and the thing efficiency is judged by.
