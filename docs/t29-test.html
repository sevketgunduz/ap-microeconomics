Questions 7 to 11 test what the reference book never says. POL-1.B.1 ends "Opening an economy to trade with other countries affects consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total economic surplus", and Hoang's Chapter 27 uses consumer surplus and producer surplus 0 times. Five of twenty-five items is the right weight for the half of the topic the book omits.
Vocabulary. Hoang writes welfare loss and social surplus; the exam writes deadweight loss and total economic surplus. Only the exam's words appear in the stems.

The market used throughout. Unless a question says otherwise, it refers to the market for rice in Freedonia, a small country that takes the world price as given. Domestic demand is Qd = 200 − 20P and domestic supply is Qs = 20P − 40, where P is dollars per kilogram and quantities are thousands of kilograms. Every area is therefore in thousands of dollars.

9. A politician says: "Our country should open its coffee market to trade. The world price is above ours, so we will export — and exporting makes everyone here better off, coffee drinkers included."

Which assessment is correct?

  • (A) The claim is right, because total economic surplus rises when a country opens to trade.
  • (B) The total does rise, but coffee drinkers lose: the domestic price rises to the world price, so they pay more and buy less. Growers gain more than drinkers lose.
  • (C) The claim is wrong, because exporting lowers total economic surplus.
  • (D) The claim is right, because export earnings are spent on imports that drinkers enjoy.
  • (E) The claim is wrong, because coffee growers lose when they sell abroad at the world price.

11. A student argues: "When a country starts importing, producers lose exactly what consumers gain — the lower price just moves money from one group to the other. So trade cannot raise the total."

What is wrong with the argument?

  • (A) Part of the consumers' gain is a transfer from producers, but consumers also gain on the extra units they now buy, and the country stops producing units that cost more than the world price. That triangle is a net gain.
  • (B) Nothing — opening to trade redistributes surplus but does not change the total.
  • (C) Producers do not lose, because they can export what they no longer sell at home.
  • (D) The total rises because the government collects revenue on imports.
  • (E) The total rises only if the country exports as well as imports.

24. Two students calculate how much consumer surplus Freedonia loses when the $1 tariff raises the price from $3 to $4.

  • Student X: ½ × 140 × ($10 − $3) − ½ × 120 × ($10 − $4) = $490 − $360.
  • Student Y: $1 × (140 + 120) ÷ 2.

Which is correct?

  • (A) Only X, because consumer surplus must be measured from the demand intercept.
  • (B) Both give $130. On a straight-line demand curve the loss is a trapezoid, and the two methods are the same area measured two ways.
  • (C) Only Y, because X uses the wrong intercept.
  • (D) Neither: the loss is $1 × 80 = $80, the tariff revenue.
  • (E) Both give $260, the sum of the two quantities.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Autarky · Application

Answer: (B)

Set domestic demand equal to domestic supply: 200 − 20P = 20P − 40, so 40P = 240, P = $6, and Q = 200 − 120 = 80 thousand kilograms.

  • (A) ✗ $10 is the demand curve's price intercept, where nothing is bought.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ At $8 quantity supplied is 120 and quantity demanded 40 — a surplus, not an equilibrium.
  • (D) ✗ $2 is the supply curve's price intercept.
  • (E) ✗ Right price, wrong quantity: 120 is what would be supplied at $8.

Question 2 — World price · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Only one comparison matters: the world price against the autarky price. Below it, the country imports; above it, it exports; equal, nothing changes.

  • (A) ✗ Reverses the rice and coffee cases.
  • (B) ✗ The size of the world market makes the country a price taker; it does not decide the direction of trade.
  • (C) ✗ Coffee's world price is above Freedonia's autarky price, so Freedonia exports it.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Rice $3 < $6, import; coffee $9 > $4, export; steel equal, no trade.
  • (E) ✗ Rice is imported. Producing a good at home does not mean exporting it.

Question 3 — A small country is a price taker · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The reference book's assumption, stated each time it draws the diagram (Hoang pp. 438 and 445): the world can supply all the country wants at the world price. That is a perfectly elastic supply — a horizontal line.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Perfectly inelastic supply is a vertical line, the opposite case.
  • (C) ✗ If foreign costs were the same, there would be no reason to trade.
  • (D) ✗ No agreement is assumed; the world price is simply beyond one small country's influence.
  • (E) ✗ The assumption concerns world supply, not Freedonia's demand, which slopes downward.

Question 4 — A world price below autarky · Application

Answer: (C)

At $3: Qd = 200 − 60 = 140; Qs = 60 − 40 = 20; imports = 140 − 20 = 120.

  • (A) ✗ The autarky position, before the market opens.
  • (B) ✗ Right quantities, but imports are only the gap, not the whole of quantity demanded.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Reverses demand and supply — this would be an exporting country.
  • (E) ✗ Domestic growers whose cost is below $3 keep supplying; production falls to 20, not to zero.

Question 5 — A world price above autarky · Analysis

Answer: (E)

A world price above autarky pulls the domestic price up to it. Growers supply more, buyers buy less, and the difference is exported.

  • (A) ✗ The importing case, which needs a world price below autarky.
  • (B) ✗ The classic error. Growers will not sell at home for $10 when they can get $14 abroad, so home buyers pay $14 too.
  • (C) ✗ Domestic production rises, not falls, when the price rises.
  • (D) ✗ Consumption falls when the price rises, and a country with a world price above autarky exports.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 6 — Trade fills the gap · Application

Answer: (A)

Imports are the gap: 900 − 300 = 600 hats. Spending on them is $7 × 600 = $4,200.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ $7 × 900 — the spending on all hats, domestic ones included.
  • (C) ✗ $7 × 300 — the spending on domestically made hats.
  • (D) ✗ $7 × 1,200 — adds the two quantities.
  • (E) ✗ The quantity imported, not the spending on it.

Question 7 — Who gains when a country imports · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The lower price helps buyers and hurts domestic growers. Buyers gain more than growers lose, so the total rises.

  • (A) ✗ Domestic producers sell less at a lower price; their surplus falls.
  • (B) ✗ Reverses the two groups, and the total does change.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Half right: right on the two groups, wrong on the total. Part of the gain is a transfer, but part is new.
  • (E) ✗ Jobs are not in the surplus account. In the model the total rises.

Question 8 — Who gains when a country imports · Application

Answer: (E)

Autarky: consumer surplus ½ × 80 × ($10 − $6) = $160; producer surplus ½ × 80 × ($6 − $2) = $160. At $3: consumer surplus ½ × 140 × ($10 − $3) = $490; producer surplus ½ × 20 × ($3 − $2) = $10. So consumer surplus rises by $330 and producer surplus falls by $150. The net gain is $180.

  • (A) ✗ $180 is the net gain to the country, not the consumers' gain; and producers lose.
  • (B) ✗ $490 is the new level of consumer surplus, not the change; producer surplus falls by $150, not $160.
  • (C) ✗ Assumes consumers gain exactly what producers lose — the error in Question 11.
  • (D) ✗ Producer surplus falls to $10; it does not rise by $10.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 9 — Who gains when a country exports · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

When a country exports, the domestic price rises to the world price. Growers gain; home buyers lose; the total rises. The politician is right about the total and wrong about "everyone".

  • (A) ✗ Right conclusion about the total, wrong reasoning about the group. A rise in the total does not mean nobody loses — the error this item is built on, and one an AP-aligned textbook makes in print.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Opening to trade raises the total in either direction.
  • (D) ✗ The foreign-exchange story is outside this model; in the coffee market, drinkers lose.
  • (E) ✗ Growers gain: they sell more at a higher price.

Question 10 — Total economic surplus rises with trade · Application

Answer: (D)

At $8: Qd = 40, Qs = 120, exports 80. The gain is the triangle ½ × 80 × ($8 − $6) = $80. Check: consumer surplus ½ × 40 × $2 = $40, producer surplus ½ × 120 × $6 = $360, total $400 against $320 under autarky.

  • (A) ✗ $40 is the new consumer surplus.
  • (B) ✗ 80 × $2 without the ½.
  • (C) ✗ $320 is the autarky total, not the change.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ $360 is the new producer surplus.

Question 11 — Total economic surplus rises with trade · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The lower price on the units still produced at home is a transfer from producers to consumers. But consumers also buy units that were never worth producing domestically, and the high-cost domestic units are replaced by cheaper imports. Those gains form the triangle — the net gain from trade.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The student's error.
  • (C) ✗ In the importing case the world price is below autarky; exporting would earn even less.
  • (D) ✗ Under free trade there is no tariff, so no revenue.
  • (E) ✗ Either direction raises the total on its own.

Question 12 — Tariff · Analysis

Answer: (E)

A tariff is a tax on imported goods (Hoang p. 437). Only one option taxes goods crossing the border.

  • (A) ✗ A quota.
  • (B) ✗ A per-unit subsidy to domestic producers.
  • (C) ✗ A per-unit tax on domestic output — the Module 50 case, not a tariff.
  • (D) ✗ An administrative barrier.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 13 — What a tariff does to prices and quantities · Application

Answer: (B)

The domestic price rises by the full tariff, to $4. Qd = 200 − 80 = 120; Qs = 80 − 40 = 40; imports = 80.

  • (A) ✗ Free trade, before the tariff.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The price rises but the quantities are left at their free-trade values.
  • (D) ✗ Subtracts the tariff instead of adding it.
  • (E) ✗ Right price and quantities, but imports are the new gap, 120 − 40, not the old one.

Question 14 — What a tariff does to prices and quantities · Analysis

Answer: (C)

A tariff stops imports once it raises the domestic price to the autarky price, $6, where domestic supply meets domestic demand. That takes $6 − $3 = $3.

  • (A) ✗ Leaves 80 thousand kilograms still imported.
  • (B) ✗ The autarky price itself, not the gap between it and the world price.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The demand intercept; far more than is needed.
  • (E) ✗ A tariff of $3 or more is prohibitive.

Question 15 — Tariff revenue · Application

Answer: (D)

Revenue is the tariff times the imports that remain: $1 × 80 = $80.

  • (A) ✗ Uses the free-trade imports, 120 — the commonest error on this topic.
  • (B) ✗ Uses the fall in imports, 40, which is the base of the two deadweight triangles.
  • (C) ✗ Uses the free-trade quantity demanded.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The autarky total surplus; unrelated.

Question 16 — Tariff revenue · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

At $2.50 the price is $5.50: Qd = 90, Qs = 70, imports 20. Revenue is $2.50 × 20 = $50, down from $80. Revenue is the rate times a base that shrinks as the rate rises.

  • (A) ✗ Multiplies the new rate by the old imports.
  • (B) ✗ Domestic production pays no tariff; rising production shrinks the revenue base.
  • (C) ✗ Revenue depends on both the rate and the imports.
  • (D) ✗ The prohibitive tariff is $3; at $2.50 some imports remain.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 17 — The tariff's surplus account · Application

Answer: (A)

Consumers lose $130. Of that, $30 goes to domestic producers and $80 to the government. The remainder, $130 − $30 − $80 = $20, goes to nobody.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The whole consumer loss, most of which is transferred.
  • (C) ✗ Subtracts only the revenue.
  • (D) ✗ Subtracts only the producer gain.
  • (E) ✗ Adds the three lines.

Question 18 — The tariff's surplus account · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The 2012 released free-response question asks exactly this. Every positive tariff creates two deadweight triangles, so the sum is largest with no tariff at all.

  • (A) ✗ The prohibitive tariff; it gives back the whole gain from trade.
  • (B) ✗ Raises revenue but loses $20 of surplus.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Revenue is only one part of the sum; and the $1 and $2 tariffs raise the same $80.
  • (E) ✗ Ignores the deadweight loss.

Question 19 — Deadweight loss of a tariff · Application

Answer: (B)

Domestic production rises from 20 to 40 and consumption falls from 140 to 120. Each triangle is ½ × 20 × $1 = $10, so the deadweight loss is $20. Check: $490 − $360 = $130 lost by consumers; $30 gained by producers, $80 by the government; $130 − $110 = $20.

  • (A) ✗ One triangle only.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Drops the ½.
  • (D) ✗ The tariff revenue.
  • (E) ✗ How the revenue is spent does not restore the lost trades.

Question 20 — Deadweight loss of a tariff · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The supply-side triangle is the extra domestic production the tariff encourages: units that cost domestic growers more than the world price to produce, which the country could have imported more cheaply. The demand-side triangle is the consumption that no longer happens.

  • (A) ✗ Domestic production pays no tariff.
  • (B) ✗ That is the demand-side triangle.
  • (C) ✗ The transfer to producers is the area above the supply curve, and it is not lost.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Foreign producers are outside the domestic surplus account.

Question 21 — Quota · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The framework asks exactly this: how quotas "alter quantities produced". With imports limited, the domestic price rises until the gap between demand and domestic supply equals the quota, so domestic firms produce more at a higher price. No graph is needed.

  • (A) ✗ Both directions reversed.
  • (B) ✗ Domestic output responds to the higher price.
  • (C) ✗ A binding quota stops the world price from applying at home.
  • (D) ✗ Production rises when the price rises.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 22 — What a quota does · Application

Answer: (A)

The price rises until imports equal the quota: (200 − 20P) − (20P − 40) = 80, so P = $4 — the same as the $1 tariff. The rectangle ($4 − $3) × 80 = $80 goes to whoever holds the import licences, as quota rent.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A binding quota raises the price.
  • (C) ✗ The reference book's claim (Hoang p. 446), which holds only if the government sells the licences. In the AP model a quota raises no government revenue.
  • (D) ✗ Wrong price, and producers' gain is a different area.

Question 25 — Protectionism and other barriers · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Tariffs, quotas and bans all work by raising the domestic price. A subsidy to domestic producers lowers their costs instead: they supply more at the world price, imports fall, and consumers still pay the world price. The government pays for it, and it creates a deadweight loss of its own — the Module 50 subsidy, applied to trade.

  • (A) ✗ Raises the domestic price.
  • (B) ✗ Raises the domestic price.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Raises the price all the way to autarky.
  • (E) ✗ A producer subsidy is the exception.
