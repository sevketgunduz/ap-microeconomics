The headline. Module 49 is the best-taught idea in Section 9. It builds consumer surplus and producer surplus from first principles — five named buyers, five named sellers, a price, and arithmetic anyone can follow — and then shows both as areas. Everything MKT-4.A(a) and (c) ask for is here, with figures. What is missing is the point of it. MKT-4.A.4 says economists use consumer and producer surplus "to measure the benefits markets create to buyers and sellers and understand market efficiency", and MKT-4.A.5 says market equilibrium "maximizes total economic surplus … meaning that perfectly competitive markets are efficient." In pp. 482–494, efficien occurs 0 times, total surplus occurs 0 times, and the phrase total economic surplus occurs 0 times in all 927 pages. Module 49 computes the two surpluses and never adds them up or says what they are for. Module 50 does both — so teach the pair together, and do not test Topic 2.6 after Module 49 alone. One vocabulary note that runs the other way. Willingness to pay is Krugman's organising term, 28 times book-wide and 5 in this module, and it occurs 0 times in the Micro CED — but 10 times across the nine released papers. The framework does not name it; the exam uses it. Teach it.

The essential knowledge statements this topic carries, verbatim:

MKT-4.A.4 (CED p. 53) — "Economists use consumer surplus and producer surplus to measure the benefits markets create to buyers and sellers and understand market efficiency."
MKT-4.A.5 (CED p. 53) — "Market equilibrium maximizes total economic surplus in the absence of market failures, meaning that perfectly competitive markets are efficient."

MKT-4.A.5 is Topic 2.6 and lives in Module 50. That is the single most important sentence in these notes for planning purposes. The framework files efficiency under the same topic as consumer and producer surplus; Krugman splits the two across a module boundary.

Krugman's three objectives (p. 482):

  1. The meaning of consumer surplus and its relationship to the demand curve
  2. The meaning of producer surplus and its relationship to the supply curve
  3. How to calculate consumer surplus and producer surplus

All three are MKT-4.A(a) and (c). Objective 3 is the calculate verb, and it is the reason this module needs a full period of practice rather than a reading.

The margin definitions, in Krugman's own words

Willingness to pay (p. 483) — "A consumer's willingness to pay for a good is the maximum price at which he or she would buy that good."
Individual consumer surplus (p. 485) — "Individual consumer surplus is the net gain to an individual buyer from the purchase of a good. It is equal to the difference between the buyer's willingness to pay and the price paid."
Total consumer surplus (p. 485) — "Total consumer surplus is the sum of the individual consumer surpluses of all the buyers of a good in a market."
A seller's cost (p. 489) — "A seller's cost is the lowest price at which he or she is willing to sell a good."
Individual producer surplus (p. 490) — "Individual producer surplus is the net gain to an individual seller from selling a good. It is equal to the difference between the price received and the seller's cost."
Total producer surplus (p. 490) — "Total producer surplus in a market is the sum of the individual producer surpluses of all the sellers of a good in a market."

Krugman adds, for both: "Economists often use the term consumer surplus to refer to both individual and to total consumer surplus … it will always be clear in context." Exam questions almost always mean the total.

The two rules the whole topic reduces to

Consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price. Producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the price.

Krugman states each as "a general principle" (pp. 487, 491). Students who can say these two sentences and compute a triangle have most of what Topic 2.6 asks for.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Surplus is a gain, not a price and not a quantity

Both surpluses are measured in money per period, and both are differences. Consumer surplus is what a buyer would have paid minus what they did pay. A student who answers "the consumer surplus is $30" when $30 is the price has not yet separated the two.

The buyer at the margin gains nothing

In Krugman's own CYU 1, Josey's third pepper has a willingness to pay of exactly $0.40 and the price is $0.40: she buys it and gains zero surplus. This is the single most useful case in the module — it is why the triangle closes to a point at the equilibrium quantity, and it is the idea Module 50 needs to argue that equilibrium is efficient.

Cost means opportunity cost, not money spent

Krugman is careful here and students are not. The student selling a used textbook spends nothing to make it available; the cost is not having the book afterwards. Keep the definition — "the lowest price at which a seller is willing to sell" — on the board, and connect it to Unit 1's opportunity cost.

Consumer surplus is below demand; producer surplus is above supply

Get the two prepositions right from the first lesson, because every later diagram in the course depends on them — price controls, quotas, taxes, monopoly, tariffs. A quick drill: name an area and have students say "below demand, above price" or "above supply, below price" in chorus.

A price change produces a rectangle and a triangle

Both Figure 49.5 and Figure 49.9 split the change into two pieces: the gain to those already trading (a rectangle) and the gain to those newly drawn in (a triangle). Students who see only the rectangle undercount; students who see only the triangle undercount more. The AP rubric for a "by how much does surplus change" question expects both.

The area is a triangle only because the curve is a straight line

On the step-shaped diagrams the areas are rectangles stacked like a staircase. The triangle is what that staircase becomes when there are many buyers. Say it once, so that a question with a step-shaped or table-based market does not throw them.

"Total economic surplus" is the framework's phrase

Krugman writes total surplus. The CED and the released papers write total economic surplus. They are the same thing. Use both names in class for a week.

7. Suggested allocation — two periods

Period 1 — consumer surplus

  1. Willingness to pay, with the five named buyers; build the step-shaped demand curve (10 min).
  2. Set the price at $30 and compute each buyer's gain. Total $49 (10 min).
  3. The same $49 as the area of three rectangles, then the smooth-curve triangle (10 min).
  4. The general rule: below the demand curve, above the price (5 min).
  5. A price fall: the rectangle for existing buyers plus the triangle for new ones (10 min).
  6. MCQ 1 and the triangle formula (5 min).

Period 2 — producer surplus, and both together

  1. A seller's cost as an opportunity cost, with the five named sellers (10 min).
  2. Producer surplus at $30: total $45; then the rule — above the supply curve, below the price (10 min).
  3. The wheat diagram and a price rise from $5 to $7 (10 min).
  4. Both areas on one diagram, at equilibrium. Note that the market clears at three books, and that

the two triangles meet at the equilibrium quantity (10 min).

  1. FRQ 1 in class; set CYU 1 and CYU 2 for homework (10 min).

Then go straight into Module 50 — it opens by adding the two areas together.
