Part 1 — examinable content (Questions 1–17)

1. A hospital administrator faces four decisions. Which one is not an economic question?

  • (A) Whether to use the remaining equipment budget on a scanner or on twelve monitors.
  • (B) Whether to print the hospital's name on signage in one typeface or another, at identical cost and identical legibility.
  • (C) Whether to schedule the one available operating theatre for orthopaedics or cardiology on Thursday.
  • (D) Whether to hire one senior nurse or two junior nurses for the same salary budget.
  • (E) Whether to keep the pharmacy open at weekends, which would require paying staff overtime.

2. Four inputs used by a shipping company are described below. Which one is scarce in the economic sense but would not be described as rare in ordinary English?

  • (A) A collection of antique navigation instruments displayed in the head office, which nobody uses.
  • (B) A rare-earth magnet used in one specialised sensor, of which the firm owns a large unused stock.
  • (C) A commemorative coin issued in 1910, of which fewer than 200 exist.
  • (D) The loading dock, which the morning and the afternoon shift both need at 1 p.m.
  • (E) A decommissioned crane awaiting disposal.

3. A government minister says: "Every economic resource is scarce, so every input a firm uses must be rationed." Which of the following is the strongest objection?

  • (A) No objection is needed; scarcity applies to all resources without exception.
  • (B) Resources are scarce only in poor countries; in wealthy ones most inputs are abundant.
  • (C) Scarcity applies to goods but not to services, so services need no rationing.
  • (D) Rationing is a policy choice, so scarcity has no bearing on whether it is needed.
  • (E) Most resources are scarce, but some are not: established knowledge is non-rival, so one firm's use of a formula does not prevent another's, and nothing is rationed by using it.

4. A vineyard lists what it used last season: the hillside it occupies; the pickers' labour; the press and the storage tanks; the owner's decision to switch to a new grape variety and bear the risk; and the 40,000 bottles it sold. Classify these as land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship and output.

  • (A) Hillside = capital · pickers = labour · press and tanks = land · switch = entrepreneurship · bottles = output
  • (B) Hillside = land · pickers = labour · press and tanks = capital · switch = labour · bottles = output
  • (C) Hillside = land · pickers = labour · press and tanks = capital · switch = entrepreneurship · bottles = output
  • (D) Hillside = land · pickers = entrepreneurship · press and tanks = capital · switch = labour · bottles = output
  • (E) All five are resources, since all five were used by the business during the season.

5. A bakery owner tells a lender: "We need capital to expand." An economist would point out that

  • (A) what the owner needs is money, and the capital is the ovens and premises the money would buy.
  • (B) capital and money are the same thing in economics, so the statement is correct as it stands.
  • (C) capital refers only to financial assets, so ovens are not capital.
  • (D) bread is capital, because it is produced by the business.
  • (E) the owner means labour, since expansion requires hiring.

6. A student has four free afternoons a week and does not sign up for the debating club, telling a friend "I haven't decided yet." Which statement is correct?

  • (A) No choice has been made, so no opportunity cost has been incurred.
  • (B) A choice has been made only if the sign-up deadline has already passed.
  • (C) The opportunity cost is zero, because the student is not doing anything with the afternoons.
  • (D) Not deciding is only a choice if the student is aware of the alternatives.
  • (E) Not signing up is itself the choice not to join, and it carries the same forgone alternative as an explicit refusal.

7. A student is given a free ticket to a four-hour concert. The ticket cannot be resold. The only alternative use of that time is a shift paying $18 an hour. Travel to the concert costs $6. What is the opportunity cost of attending?

  • (A) $6
  • (B) $78
  • (C) $72
  • (D) $0, because the ticket was free
  • (E) $90

8. On a Saturday a student could work a shift worth $90, revise for an exam, or visit family. She judges the shift the most valuable of the three and the revision second. She chooses to visit family. What is the opportunity cost of that visit?

  • (A) Nothing, because visiting family has no monetary price.
  • (B) The revision, because it was the option she ranked immediately below the visit.
  • (C) The shift and the revision together, because both were given up.
  • (D) The shift, because it was the single best alternative she gave up.
  • (E) $90 plus the value of the revision plus the value of the visit.

9. An analyst earning $52,000 a year is offered $58,000 elsewhere. The new job adds 30 minutes of commuting each way, 5 days a week, 46 weeks a year. She values her own time at $15 an hour and nothing else differs. What is the annual net gain from moving?

  • (A) $2,550
  • (B) $6,000
  • (C) $3,450
  • (D) $9,450
  • (E) $0 — the two jobs are equivalent once commuting is counted

10. Two students each pay $40 for a ticket to the same event on the same evening. One would otherwise have worked a shift paying $95; the other would otherwise have stayed home watching television, which they value at $10. Which statement is correct?

  • (A) The opportunity cost is $40 for both, because that is what each paid.
  • (B) The opportunity cost is higher for the second student, who gains less from attending.
  • (C) The opportunity cost is $135 for the first and $50 for the second: the same price, very different total economic costs.
  • (D) Opportunity cost cannot be compared between two people.
  • (E) The opportunity cost is $95 for the first and $10 for the second; the $40 is not part of it, since both paid it.

11. A city wants households to reduce food waste. It is choosing between a leaflet campaign explaining the environmental damage and a charge of $2 per bag of unsorted waste collected. An economist would expect

  • (A) the charge to work better, because it changes what a household gains by sorting, whereas the leaflet changes only what it knows.
  • (B) both to work equally, since each communicates that waste is undesirable.
  • (C) neither to work, because household waste decisions are habitual rather than economic.
  • (D) the leaflet to work better, because households who understand the damage will act on it.
  • (E) the charge to work better, because $2 is a large sum relative to a household's weekly budget.

12. A country has enough engineers to staff either a new rail line or a flood-defence programme, but not both. The government funds the rail line, and a commentator writes: "The flood defences cost nothing — no money was spent on them." Which response is correct?

  • (A) The commentator is right: a project that is not undertaken has no cost.
  • (B) The commentator has confused cost with expenditure. The engineers are scarce, so funding the rail line meant forgoing the flood defences, and that forgone programme is the opportunity cost of the rail line.
  • (C) The commentator is right, because opportunity cost applies only to individuals, not to governments.
  • (D) The commentator is wrong, because the flood defences will eventually be built and so cost the same amount later.
  • (E) The commentator is wrong, because the rail line will generate revenue that offsets its cost.

13. A theatre with 200 empty seats is deciding whether to sell one more ticket at $12. The building costs $900 a night whether or not the seat is sold; cleaning and printing for one extra attendee cost $3. Should the seat be sold, and why?

  • (A) No, because $12 does not cover the seat's share of the $900 building cost.
  • (B) Yes, because any revenue is better than none regardless of costs.
  • (C) No, because selling below average cost is loss-making.
  • (D) Yes: the marginal cost of that seat is $3 and the marginal benefit is $12, so the sale adds $9. The $900 does not change with the extra ticket and so does not enter the decision.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without knowing the total number of seats sold.

14. In a market economy, a prolonged shortage of a good normally ends without anyone being instructed to produce more. Which mechanism explains this best?

  • (A) A central authority observes the shortage and issues revised production targets.
  • (B) Consumers reduce demand voluntarily once they learn a shortage exists.
  • (C) The price rises, which both rations the existing supply and rewards whoever produces more — so producers move toward the good without being told to.
  • (D) Producers have a legal obligation to meet consumer demand.
  • (E) The government subsidises production until the shortage clears.

15. A country's government owns the electricity grid and the railways, sets a minimum wage and funds universal healthcare. All other industry is privately owned and firms decide what to produce and at what price. Is this a command economy?

  • (A) Yes, because the state owns major industries.
  • (B) No. A command economy requires public ownership of industry and a central authority making the production and consumption decisions. Here firms still decide what to produce.
  • (C) Yes, because a minimum wage is a central decision about the labour market.
  • (D) No, because command economies no longer exist.
  • (E) It cannot be classified without knowing the share of GDP the government spends.

16. A country's firms decide what to produce and what to charge. Its government owns the rail network, funds schools and hospitals out of taxation, sets a minimum wage and taxes carbon emissions. Asked to classify it using the economic systems named in the AP Microeconomics framework, four students answer. Which answer is fully correct?

  • (A) A market economy, because firms make the production decisions and that is the only test.
  • (B) A command economy, because the state owns a major industry and intervenes in the labour market.
  • (C) None of the three. The categories describe historical extremes and no longer classify real countries.
  • (D) A mixed economy — though the term is a useful convenience rather than required content.
  • (E) A mixed economy, and the term is required: MKT-1.B.2 names command, market and mixed as the systems that influence resource allocation. It is also the one of the three the textbook never defines.

17. Two neighbouring stretches of forest are logged. One is owned by a company that expects to harvest the same land again in twenty years; the other is open to anyone. The owned stretch is replanted and cut selectively; the open one is cleared. Which explains the difference best?

  • (A) The owner bears the future cost of clearing the land, so avoids it; on the open land nobody bears that cost, so no one has reason to leave a tree standing.
  • (B) The company is better regulated than the individuals logging the open land.
  • (C) Open-access land is usually of lower quality, so clearing it is more efficient.
  • (D) Individuals are less concerned about the environment than companies are.
  • (E) The difference is coincidental; ownership does not affect how a resource is used.

Part 2 — background terms (Questions 18–20)

These three questions test the only Module 1 concepts with no anchor anywhere in the AP Microeconomics framework. Score them separately if you are reporting an exam-relevant figure.

18. Which sentence uses economy and economics correctly?

  • (A) She is studying economy at university, and Japan's economics grew last year.
  • (B) Economy is the study of scarcity and choice, and a market economics uses prices to coordinate.
  • (C) He teaches economy, and the national economics grew quickly last year.
  • (D) She is studying economics at university, and Japan's economy grew last year.
  • (E) Both words are interchangeable, so all of the above are acceptable.

19. A national price index rises 2% over a year. Over the same year residential rents rise 12% and consumer electronics fall 8%. A commentator concludes that "prices were roughly stable."

Which statement best describes what the index is and what it misses?

  • (A) The index is wrong, since neither of the two markets moved by 2%.
  • (B) The index shows that price changes are a microeconomic rather than a macroeconomic matter.
  • (C) It is an aggregate: a single number summarising many markets, which is what makes it a macroeconomic measure — and which is also why it hides two large movements in opposite directions, in exactly the two markets a microeconomist would want to explain.
  • (D) The index proves that one of the two sectoral figures was measured incorrectly.
  • (E) The index should be replaced by the larger of the two movements, since a 12% rise matters more than an 8% fall.

20. Two economists agree that a proposed carbon tax would reduce emissions by about 8% and reduce manufacturing employment by about 1%. One supports the tax and the other opposes it. Which statement best describes the disagreement?

  • (A) They disagree on a positive question, and further data would settle it. Models exist precisely to resolve disputes like this.
  • (B) One of them must have made an analytical error, since they agree on the numbers.
  • (C) The disagreement shows that economic models are unreliable and should not inform policy.
  • (D) The disagreement is about definitions and would disappear if the terms were clarified.
  • (E) They agree on the positive question — what the tax would do — and disagree on the normative one, whether it should be adopted. No amount of additional data settles a question that turns on values.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Economics · Analysis

Answer: (B)

A decision is an economic question when something must be given up. Option B has no forgone alternative: identical cost, identical legibility, so nothing is sacrificed either way. It is a question of taste.

  • (A) ✗ Scanner and monitors compete for the same budget; every pound on one is unavailable for the other.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. No trade-off, so no economic question, however official the decision feels.
  • (C) ✗ One theatre, two specialties, one Thursday. Whichever is scheduled, the other's use of that theatre is given up.
  • (D) ✗ The salary budget is fixed, so one senior nurse is chosen instead of two junior ones.
  • (E) ✗ Overtime pay is money that then cannot be used elsewhere, and staff time likewise.

Question 2 — Scarcity · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Scarcity is about competing uses, not rarity. The loading dock is an ordinary facility, in no way rare, and two shifts need it at the same moment — so using it one way prevents the other. That is the definition.

  • (A) ✗ Nobody uses them and there are no competing uses, so they are not scarce however old they are.
  • (B) ✗ Rare in the ordinary sense, but the firm holds more than it needs, so no use competes with another.
  • (C) ✗ The clearest case of rare but not scarce — rarity is not the test.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Plentiful in kind, scarce in the economic sense, because two uses compete for it.
  • (E) ✗ Awaiting disposal means nothing wants it. No competing uses, no scarcity.

Question 3 — Scarcity · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Enduring Understanding MKT-1 says "Most resources are scarce", and MKT-1.A.2 names the exception outright: "some factors of production (such as established knowledge) may not be scarce due to their non-rival nature." Krugman never makes the point — non-rival occurs 0 times in the book. A non-rival resource — established knowledge, a formula, a language — is not used up by being used, so one firm applying it does not stop another. Nothing about it needs rationing.

  • (A) ✗ Restates the minister's error. The framework's own wording is most.
  • (B) ✗ Wealth changes how binding scarcity is, not whether resources have competing uses. A rich economy still cannot use the same engineer twice.
  • (C) ✗ Services are as scarce as goods — a surgeon's hour has competing uses like any other.
  • (D) ✓ Contains something true — rationing is a policy choice — but reaches the wrong conclusion. Scarcity is exactly what makes some allocation mechanism necessary; it does not follow that scarcity is irrelevant.
  • (E) ✓ Correct. The exception the word most exists to preserve.

Question 4 — Resources · Application

Answer: (C)

Land is what comes from nature — the hillside. Labour is the effort of workers — the pickers. Capital is manufactured goods used to make other goods — the press and tanks. Entrepreneurship is risk-taking and organising production — the decision to switch variety. The 40,000 bottles are output, not a resource: resources are inputs.

  • (A) ✗ Swaps land and capital. A hillside is not manufactured; a press is.
  • (B) ✗ Correct except that the variety switch is labelled labour. Bearing risk and reorganising production is entrepreneurship, not effort.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. One caveat for marking: MKT-1.A.2 names only

land, labor and capital, and entrepreneurship occurs 0 times in the Micro CED. The four-way classification is standard economics and your textbook’s own rubric awards it, but the framework’s list is three.

  • (D) ✗ Swaps labour and entrepreneurship. Picking grapes is effort; deciding to change the business is not.
  • (E) ✗ The distractor for students who forget that output is not an input. The bottles are what the resources produced.

Question 5 — Resources · Analysis

Answer: (A)

In economics capital is manufactured goods used to produce other goods — ovens, mixers, premises. Money is not capital; it is what buys capital. The owner's sentence is ordinary English, not economic vocabulary.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. The money is finance; the capital is what the finance would purchase.
  • (B) ✗ States the confusion the question is built on.
  • (C) ✗ Exactly inverted: financial assets are the everyday meaning, and physical productive goods are the economic one.
  • (D) ✗ Bread is output, not capital. Capital produces; bread is produced.
  • (E) ✗ Expansion may well require hiring, but "capital" does not mean labour under any reading.

Question 6 — Individual choice · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Individual choice is "decisions about what to do, which necessarily involve decisions about what not to do". Declining to act is a decision with the same forgone alternatives. Delay does not remove a choice; it makes it by default.

  • (A) ✗ The most common error on this term. Not choosing is choosing.
  • (B) ✗ A deadline changes when the choice becomes irreversible, not whether one is being made.
  • (C) ✗ The afternoons are being used for something, and even idleness has an alternative use.
  • (D) ✗ Awareness affects how well the choice is made, not whether it is one.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 7 — Opportunity cost · Application (numeric)

Answer: (B)

Explicit cost: $6 travel. Implicit cost: 4 hours × $18 = $72 of forgone earnings. Total opportunity cost = 6 + 72 = $78. "Free" tickets are not free.

  • (A) ✗ $6 counts only the money paid and ignores the forgone shift — the module's central error.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. $6 explicit + $72 implicit.
  • (C) ✗ $72 is the implicit cost alone; the travel is a real cash outlay and belongs in the total.
  • (D) ✗ The ticket price is zero; the cost of attending is not. This is the distractor the word "free" is in the stem to attract.
  • (E) ✗ $90 would be 5 hours of forgone earnings, or four hours plus an $18 error. The concert is four hours.

Question 8 — Opportunity cost · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Opportunity cost is the single best alternative forgone. Of the two options given up, the shift was ranked highest, so it is the opportunity cost. The others are not added in.

  • (A) ✗ Absence of a price does not mean absence of a cost. The whole point of opportunity cost is that the cost need not be monetary.
  • (B) ✗ The next-best forgone option is the shift, not the revision. Ranking below the chosen option is not the test.
  • (C) ✗ The standard error: summing everything given up. Only one alternative could have been taken instead.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Adds the value of the option actually chosen, which is a benefit rather than a cost, and double-counts besides.

Question 9 — Opportunity cost · Application (numeric)

Answer: (A)

Extra commuting: 30 minutes each way = 1 hour a day × 5 days × 46 weeks = 230 hours. Valued at $15 an hour = $3,450 of implicit cost. Gross gain = 58,000 − 52,000 = $6,000. Net gain = 6,000 − 3,450 = $2,550.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ $6,000 is the salary difference alone, ignoring the implicit cost of the commute — the same error as option A in Question 7.
  • (C) ✗ $3,450 is the commuting cost by itself, not the net effect.
  • (D) ✗ $9,450 adds the commuting cost to the salary gain instead of subtracting it.
  • (E) ✗ Concludes the two are equivalent, which would require the commute to cost exactly $6,000. It costs $3,450, so moving is still worth $2,550.

Question 10 — Opportunity cost · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

Total economic cost is explicit plus implicit. Both students pay $40 explicitly. The first gives up a $95 shift, so bears $135; the second gives up $10 of television, so bears $50. The same price can carry very different opportunity costs, which is why price alone never measures cost.

  • (A) ✗ Counts only the explicit cost, and so misses the entire point of the comparison.
  • (B) ✗ Reverses it. The second student gives up less, so bears the lower cost.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. $40 + $95 = $135 and $40 + $10 = $50.
  • (D) ✗ Opportunity costs are routinely compared across people; that is how the question is even interesting.
  • (E) ✓ Right that the implicit costs differ — but wrong to exclude the $40. A cost is excluded when it is the same whichever option is chosen, not when two different people happen to pay it. Each student pays $40 only if they attend, so it is part of each one's cost.

Question 11 — Incentives · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

An incentive changes what someone gains from an action. The leaflet changes what households know; the charge changes what sorting is worth to them. Krugman's own example is the same shape: a voluntary appeal to cut pollution is unlikely to work where a financial incentive is. MKT-3.A.2 states it as a bare fact — "Economic agents respond to incentives" — and MKT-3.A.3 adds the qualification about prices and constraints. Together they are the law of demand, which is Topic 2.1.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Payoff against information.
  • (B) ✗ Both communicate a preference; only one changes a payoff.
  • (C) ✗ Habits are precisely what a price change disturbs; this option denies the mechanism the question asks about.
  • (D) ✗ Assumes information is sufficient. Economists are, in Krugman's words, sceptical of attempts to change behaviour that do not change incentives.
  • (E) ✓ Picks the right option for the wrong reason. The size of $2 relative to a budget is not the argument — a much smaller charge would still work through the same mechanism, and a large leaflet campaign would not.

Question 12 — Integrated · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Scarcity (one pool of engineers), choice (fund one project), and opportunity cost (the forgone project) in one case. Cost is what is given up, not what is paid out, and MKT-1.A.1 frames scarcity against "society's wants and needs", not only an individual's.

  • (A) ✗ States the confusion of cost with expenditure, which is the error in the stem.
  • (B) ✓ Correct. The flood-defence programme is the cost of the rail line.
  • (C) ✗ Opportunity cost applies to societies explicitly. MKT-1.A.1: "Economic

trade-offs arise from the lack of sufficient resources (scarcity) to meet society’s wants and needs."

  • (D) ✗ Deferral changes when the resources are used, not whether choosing the rail line forwent something now.
  • (E) ✗ Revenue affects whether the choice was a good one. It does not change what was given up to make it.

Question 13 — Marginal benefit, cost and analysis · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Marginal analysis compares the additional benefit with the additional cost of one more unit. Marginal benefit $12, marginal cost $3, so the sale adds $9. The $900 is incurred whether or not the seat sells and therefore does not change with the decision.

  • (A) ✗ Allocates a share of a cost that does not vary with the extra ticket. Average cost is the wrong comparison.
  • (B) ✗ Reaches the right decision by a rule that would also sell a $1 ticket costing $3. Revenue must beat marginal cost, not zero.
  • (C) ✗ Average cost again. A seat that would otherwise be empty adds $9 whatever the average is.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The number already sold does not change the marginal cost of one more.

Question 14 — Market economy · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Coordination without instruction is the defining feature. A rising price simultaneously rations the existing quantity and rewards additional production, so producers move toward the shortage because they gain by it.

  • (A) ✗ Describes a command economy.
  • (B) ✗ Information alone rarely changes behaviour; the price change is what does it. Compare Question 11.
  • (C) ✓ Correct. Signal and incentive in the same number.
  • (D) ✗ No such obligation exists in a market economy.
  • (E) ✗ A subsidy is government action. The point is that the shortage clears without it.

Question 15 — Command economy · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Two conditions, both required: industry publicly owned and a central authority making production and consumption decisions. Here firms decide what to produce, so the second fails.

  • (A) ✗ Ownership of some industries is not ownership of industry, and it is only half the test anyway.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ A minimum wage is a regulation within a market, not central determination of what gets produced.
  • (D) ✗ Factually shaky and irrelevant — the question is about classification, not existence.
  • (E) ✗ Government spending share does not enter the definition.

Question 16 — Mixed economy · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Two things have to be right: the classification and the status of the term. The country has private production decisions and substantial government allocation, which is the definition of a mixed economy — and MKT-1.B.2 names all three systems, so mixed is examinable here even though mixed economy occurs 0 times in Krugman's 927 pages.

  • (A) ✗ "Little government involvement in the decisions" is part of the definition of a market

economy. A government that owns the railways and sets wages is making allocation decisions.

  • (B) ✗ A command economy needs public ownership of industry and a central authority making

the production and consumption decisions. Here firms still decide what to produce, so the second condition fails.

  • (C) ✗ The extremes are limiting cases, which is exactly what makes the middle category useful.

Abolishing the classification loses the comparison the framework asks for.

  • (D) ✓ Reaches the right classification by the wrong route. This is the AP

Macroeconomics position: there, none of the three systems appears in the framework at all. In AP Microeconomics MKT-1.B.2 names them, so the term is required content. A student who has taken both courses, or read a Macro study guide, will choose this.

  • (E) ✓ Correct. Right classification, and right about the framework.

Question 17 — Property rights · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Ownership decides who bears the consequences. An owner who will harvest the same land in twenty years loses by clearing it now, so does not. On open-access land nobody bears that future cost, so nobody gains by leaving a tree.

  • (A) ✓ Correct. Who bears the cost is the whole mechanism. MKT-3.A.1 makes it

a Unit 2 requirement — "A well-defined system of property rights is necessary for the market system to function well" — and POL-3.A.2 makes its absence the source of externalities in Unit 6.

  • (B) ✗ Assumes an enforcement difference the stem does not give. The outcome follows from ownership alone.
  • (C) ✗ Invents a quality difference, and it would not explain replanting on one side.
  • (D) ✗ Attributes the difference to attitude rather than to incentive. The same people would behave differently under different ownership.
  • (E) ✗ Denies the effect the stem describes.

Question 18 — Economy · Analysis (background)

Answer: (D)

Economics is the discipline; an economy is the system being studied. Only one option gets both right. The quickest test is the article: "Japan's" and "a market" need a countable noun, so those take economy; "studying" takes no article, so it takes economics.

  • (A) ✗ Both reversed.
  • (B) ✗ Both reversed.
  • (C) ✗ Both reversed.
  • (D) ✓ Correct. Studying economics; Japan's economy grew.
  • (E) ✗ They are not interchangeable, and the distinction shows up in written answers.

Question 19 — Micro, macro and aggregates · Analysis (background)

Answer: (C)

An aggregate summarises many markets into one number. That is what makes it a macroeconomic measure and also what makes it lossy. Aggregate occurs 0 times in the Micro CED and 177 times in the Macro one, and the reason is in this question: microeconomics studies the individual markets that the index averages away.

  • (A) ✗ An average need not equal any of the values it averages. The figure is incomplete, not

wrong.

  • (B) ✗ Exactly inverted. The single economy-wide index is the macroeconomic object; the rent

market and the electronics market are the microeconomic ones.

  • (C) ✓ Correct. Both halves: what an aggregate is, and what it conceals.
  • (D) ✗ Nothing suggests measurement error. A 12% rise and an 8% fall in two markets are entirely

consistent with a small overall index move.

  • (E) ✓ Right that the two movements matter more than the average — but the remedy is wrong.

Replacing an average with its largest component is not a better aggregate, it is a worse one. The answer is to look at the two markets separately, which is what this course does.

Question 20 — Positive, normative and models · Synthesis (background)

Answer: (E)

They agree on the positive question — what the tax would do — and disagree on the normative one, whether it should be adopted. Trading 1% of manufacturing employment for an 8% emissions cut is a judgement about values, and no further data settles it.

  • (A) ✗ Gets the category backwards. They already agree on the positive question; more data would change nothing.
  • (B) ✗ Agreement on the facts and disagreement on the policy is the normal case, not evidence of an error.
  • (C) ✗ The models did their job — both economists reached the same estimates. The disagreement is downstream of them.
  • (D) ✗ No definitional ambiguity is present; both figures are stated plainly.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 17 concept entries appear. Weighting follows the framework rather than the module: opportunity cost — the only concept CBA-1.A(c) asks students to calculate — carries four items, and the three economic systems carry three between them because Topic 1.2 is a quarter of what this module owes Unit 1 and Krugman's own AP Review contains no item on it at all.

Why Part 2 is only three questions. Twelve of Module 1's seventeen concepts are examinable in AP Microeconomics and two more (incentives, property rights) are examinable in Unit 2, so almost nothing here is genuinely off-syllabus. The AP Macroeconomics version of this paper has an eight-question background section for the same module. That gap is the clearest single measure of how differently the two frameworks treat this chapter.

Bloom levels. Application 3 · Analysis 11 · Synthesis 6, a ratio of 1 : 3.7 : 2 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The deviation is deliberate and is explained under the blueprint: only opportunity cost supports calculation in this module, so application is capped at three genuine items and the rest would have been invented.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: counting only explicit cost (7A, 7D, 9B); summing every forgone option instead of taking the best (8C); confusing capital with money (5B); treating rarity as scarcity (2C); reading output as an input (4E); and assuming information changes behaviour the way a payoff does (11A, 14B).

Four distractors are correct-conclusion-by-wrong-reasoning, flagged in the solutions: 3D, 10E, 11E and 16D. Students who choose them hold a true fact and a broken argument, and need different feedback from students who simply did not know.

Answer distribution. A 4 · B 4 · C 4 · D 4 · E 4 — exactly even.

Question 16 is the one to discuss aloud. Its option D is the AP Macroeconomics answer: in that framework no economic system is named at all, so mixed economy really is a convenience there. Here MKT-1.B.2 names all three, and the term is required precisely because the textbook omits it. A student who has seen a Macro study guide — or who is taking both courses — will pick D and be half right, which is the most useful kind of wrong answer this module produces.
