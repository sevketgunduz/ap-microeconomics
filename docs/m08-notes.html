The headline. Module 8 teaches price ceilings and floors thoroughly and leaves out the one thing the exam tests hardest about them. Topic 2.8's statements POL-1.A.4 and POL-1.A.5 (CED p. 55) say that a control on an efficient market "can only decrease allocative efficiency" and that deadweight loss measures the loss. Deadweight loss occurs 0 times in pp. 77–87, and the book never draws or computes it for a price control anywhere — it says once, in the Section 2 summary (p. 95), that price controls "lead to deadweight loss". Allocative occurs 0 times in the whole book. Meanwhile deadweight loss is the most frequent of all the Topic 2.8 terms on the released papers in this project (34 hits). The module spends most of its length instead on four kinds of inefficiency — misallocation, wasted resources, quality distortion, black markets — none of which the framework or the released papers name. Teach them as the reason a control is costly; examine deadweight loss.

The enduring understanding, verbatim (CED p. 55):

POL-1 — "Government policies influence consumer and producer behavior and therefore affect market outcomes."

Krugman's four objectives (p. 77):

  1. The meaning of price controls, one way government intervenes in markets
  2. How price controls can create problems and make a market inefficient
  3. Why economists are often deeply skeptical of attempts to intervene in markets
  4. Who benefits and who loses from price controls, and why they are used despite their well-known

problems

Objective 1 is POL-1.A.1; objective 2 is POL-1.A.4 in substance; objective 4 is the consumer-and-producer-surplus reasoning of LO POL-1.A(c). Objective 3 is commentary.

Verdict on alignment. Strong on the mechanics (ceiling → shortage, floor → surplus, binding or not), with Krugman's own schedules that make the shortage and surplus exact. Weak on exactly the two statements the exam leans on, deadweight loss and allocative efficiency, which must be supplied. Over-long on the four named inefficiencies, which are worth teaching as reasons and not as vocabulary. The student guide marks them ○ Background.

The knowledge elements, in the CED's own words

Learning objective POL-1.A (CED p. 55):

a. Define forms of government price and quantity intervention. b. Explain (using graphs where appropriate) how government policies alter consumer and producer behaviors that influence incentives and therefore affect outcomes. c. Calculate (using data from a graph or table where appropriate) changes in market outcomes resulting from government policies.
POL-1.A.1 — "Some government policies, such as price floors, price ceilings, and other forms of price and quantity regulation, affect incentives and outcomes in all market structures." (CED p. 55)
POL-1.A.4 — "Government intervention in a market producing the efficient quantity through taxes, subsidies, price controls, or quantity controls can only decrease allocative efficiency." (CED p. 55)
POL-1.A.5 — "Deadweight loss represents the losses to buyers and sellers as a result of government intervention in an efficient market." (CED p. 55)

The other three statements of the topic — POL-1.A.2 (taxes and subsidies shift curves), POL-1.A.3 (they affect government revenue or costs) and POL-1.A.6 (incidence depends on elasticity) — are not this module's job: tax, subsid and incidence each occur 0 times in pp. 77–87.

Note the condition in POL-1.A.4 and POL-1.A.5: "in an efficient market". Krugman makes the same assumption explicitly on p. 78 — "the markets in question are efficient before price controls are imposed" — and notes that in a monopolised market a control can move it closer to efficiency. That caveat is Unit 6 content (Topic 6.4); flag it and move on.

The CED's own sample question

Micro CED sample question 5 (Exam Information p. 128; key E, Skill 3.A, POL-1.A, Unit 2, p. 135) sets a price floor on a lettered graph and asks for the resulting consumer surplus, producer surplus and deadweight loss as sums of areas. It cannot be reproduced without its graph, but it is the clearest evidence of what the topic examines: a floor read in surplus terms, deadweight loss included. The test's question 21 is built on the same pattern with Krugman's butter numbers.

Both schedules are exactly linear, which is what lets the student guide compute the deadweight loss Krugman never draws:

  • Apartments: quantity demanded = 3.0 − P/1,000 and quantity supplied = 1.0 + P/1,000 (millions).

Under the $800 ceiling 1.8 million are rented; buyers value the 1.8 millionth apartment at $1,200 and sellers would supply it for $800, so the deadweight loss is ½ × 0.2 million × $400 = $40 million a month.

  • Butter: quantity demanded = 15 − 5P and quantity supplied = 10P (millions of pounds). Under the

$1.20 floor 9 million pounds are sold; buyers value the 9 millionth pound at $1.20 and sellers would supply it for $0.90, so the deadweight loss is ½ × 1 million × $0.30 = $150,000.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

A ceiling is below equilibrium, a floor above — and the names mislead

"Ceiling" sounds high and "floor" low, and both are true of the limit: the ceiling caps the price from above, the floor props it from below. A binding ceiling sits below equilibrium; a binding floor sits above. Students who place them by the sound of the word draw non-binding controls and get no shortage or surplus at all. MCQ 1 is built on it.

Both controls reduce the quantity traded

The most counter-intuitive line in the module, and Krugman says it outright (p. 84): "You might be tempted to think that a price floor and a price ceiling have opposite effects, but both have the effect of reducing the quantity of a good bought and sold." The quantity traded is always the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied — under a ceiling the supply, under a floor the demand.

Deadweight loss is not the shortage

Students compute the 400,000-apartment shortage and call it the loss. The shortage is a horizontal distance at the controlled price; deadweight loss is the triangle between the curves, from the quantity actually traded to the equilibrium quantity. Under the $800 ceiling, 200,000 apartments that would have been rented are not — that 0.2 million is the base of the triangle, not the 0.4 million.

A surplus is not good, and "surplus" is not consumer surplus

Under a floor the surplus is unsold butter; total economic surplus has fallen. Say the two words together once, so students hear the clash.

In Micro, say "surplus of labor"

Krugman's MCQ 5 asks how many workers are "unemployed". Unemploy- occurs 0 times in the Micro CED; the framework's language for a minimum wage above equilibrium is a surplus in the labour market. Accept unemployed, model surplus of labor.
