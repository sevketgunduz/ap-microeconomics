Price Controls: Ceilings and Floors

Sixteen terms from Module 8 of Krugman’s Economics for AP and two your course adds. What happens when the law stops a price from reaching equilibrium — and how to measure what that costs.

Textbook pages 77–87·16 terms· Topic 2.8·2 periods

How to use this guide

Read Module 8 in your textbook first. Entries 1 to 8 are what a ceiling and a floor do; entries 9 to 11 are how to measure the cost; entries 12 to 16 are your textbook’s other costs and reasons, marked background.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 8 of your textbook does not teach it. Entries 9 and 10 carry the flag — and entry 10 is the one released exams ask about most.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. A ceiling below equilibrium causes a shortage; a floor above equilibrium causes a surplus. Either way less is traded than at equilibrium, and the value of the lost trades is the deadweight loss.

⚑ Your textbook leaves out the measure. Module 8 describes what price controls cost in words — misallocation, waste, quality, black markets — but never measures it. Your course measures it as deadweight loss, a triangle on the diagram. Entries 9 and 10 show you how, on your textbook’s own numbers.

The 16 terms

01

Price controls

★ Exam
Definition

Legal restrictions on how high or low a market price may go. They take two forms: a price ceiling and a price floor.

Textbook page 77.

A market left alone moves to the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. That price does not always please everyone. Buyers can make a case that it is too high — rents a working family cannot afford — and sellers that it is too low — wages below the poverty line. Price controls are what a government does when it agrees.

This module is about what happens next. The answer is the same every time: the price stops doing its job of matching buyers to sellers, and somebody pays for that.

Watch out. A price control does not move a curve. Demand and supply stay exactly where they were; the law only stops the price from reaching the crossing.

Textbook

Rent control in New York. A legacy of the Second World War, kept and extended after it.

Textbook

The minimum wage. A floor on the price of an hour of labor, in force in the United States and many other countries.

Illustrative

Price caps after a disaster. Governments often cap the prices of fuel or bottled water after a hurricane, when prices would otherwise jump.

How it connects. The two forms are the price ceiling (entry 2) and the price floor (entry 5). Why governments use them anyway is the last entry (entry 16).

Check yourself

1.1 Does a price control shift the demand curve or the supply curve?

Show answer

Neither. Both curves stay where they are. The control only forbids some prices.

1.2 Name the two forms of price control and say which one protects buyers.

Show answer

A price ceiling (a legal maximum) protects buyers; a price floor (a legal minimum) protects sellers.

02

Price ceiling

★ Exam
Definition

A maximum price sellers are allowed to charge for a good or service.

Textbook pages 77–79, Figures 8.1 and 8.2.

In your textbook’s model of New York apartments the market rent is $1,000 a month and 2 million apartments are rented. A ceiling of $800 makes landlords offer fewer apartments and makes more people want one.

To matter at all, a ceiling has to be set below the equilibrium price. Then the price is stuck at the ceiling and cannot rise to clear the market.

Watch out. The name misleads. A ceiling is a maximum, so it does its work when it is low — below equilibrium. A ceiling set high is above the market price and changes nothing.

Textbook

The $800 ceiling. Your textbook’s Figure 8.2: rent capped at $800 when the market rent would be $1,000.

Numeric

Reading the schedule. At $800, quantity demanded is 2.2 million apartments and quantity supplied is 1.8 million.

Illustrative

Concert tickets. A rule that tickets may not be resold above face value is a ceiling on the resale price.

How it connects. It is one of the two price controls (entry 1); whether it does anything depends on whether it is binding (entry 3), and if it is, it creates a shortage (entry 4). It helps some buyers (entry 11) and brings low quality (entry 14) and black markets (entry 15).

Check yourself

2.1 The equilibrium price of a good is $50. Which of these is a binding price ceiling: $40 or $60?

Show answer

$40. A ceiling must be below the equilibrium price to stop the price from reaching it. $60 is above the market price and has no effect.

2.2 Under a binding ceiling, which is larger: quantity demanded or quantity supplied?

Show answer

Quantity demanded. The low price makes more people want the good and fewer sellers offer it.

03

Binding and non-binding controls

★ Exam
Definition

A control is binding when it actually constrains the market — a ceiling below the equilibrium price, or a floor above it. A non-binding control has no effect.

Textbook pages 80 and 83.

Your textbook’s test is simple: a ceiling of $1,200 on apartments whose market rent is $1,000 does nothing, because nobody was paying more than $1,000 anyway. A floor of $0.80 on butter that sells for $1.00 does nothing for the same reason.

So the first thing to do with any price control is compare it with the equilibrium price. Only then is there anything to draw.

Watch out. “Binding” is about position, not about the size of the law or how strictly it is enforced. A strictly enforced ceiling above the market price still binds nobody.

Textbook

A $1,200 rent ceiling. Above the $1,000 equilibrium, so it is not binding and has no effect.

Numeric

A $0.80 butter floor. Below the $1.00 equilibrium, so it is not binding: 10 million pounds are still sold at $1.00.

Illustrative

Your textbook’s multiple-choice 1. “To be effective, a price ceiling must be set above the equilibrium price” is false — it must be set below.

How it connects. It decides whether a price ceiling (entry 2) or a price floor (entry 5) matters at all.

Check yourself

3.1 The market wage is $10 an hour. A minimum wage of $8 is passed. What happens?

Show answer

Nothing. A floor below the equilibrium price is not binding; workers are already paid $10.

3.2 A ceiling and a floor are both set at exactly the equilibrium price. Is either binding?

Show answer

No. The market already clears at that price, so neither constrains anyone.

04

The shortage from a price ceiling

★ Exam
Definition

A binding ceiling creates a persistent shortage: quantity demanded minus quantity supplied at the ceiling price.

Textbook pages 78–79, Figure 8.2.

At $800, landlords supply 1.8 million apartments (point A) and 2.2 million people want one (point B). The shortage is 2.2 − 1.8 = 0.4 million apartments: 400,000 people who want an apartment at the legal rent and cannot get one.

It is persistent because the usual cure — the price rising — is illegal. In an uncontrolled market a shortage lasts only until the price goes up.

Watch out. Subtract in the right order, and from the right curves. The shortage is measured at the ceiling price, between the demand curve and the supply curve — not between either curve and the equilibrium quantity.

Textbook

Apartments. 2.2 million demanded − 1.8 million supplied = a shortage of 400,000.

Numeric

A lower ceiling. At $700 the schedule gives 2.3 demanded and 1.7 supplied: a shortage of 0.6 million. The lower the ceiling, the bigger the shortage.

Textbook

Game-day parking. Your textbook’s Check Your Understanding 1: a $7 cap on driveway parking worth $11 cuts spaces offered by 400 and raises spaces wanted by 400 — a shortage of 800.

How it connects. It is what a binding price ceiling (entry 2) produces; the quantity actually traded is the smaller number, which is why the quantity falls (entry 8) and a deadweight loss (entry 10) appears. It also leads to misallocation (entry 12) and wasted resources (entry 13).

Check yourself

4.1 A ceiling of $40 is set where quantity demanded is 500 and quantity supplied is 320. What is the shortage, and how many units are bought?

Show answer

Shortage = 500 − 320 = 180. Only 320 are bought — buyers cannot buy what sellers do not offer.

4.2 Why does the shortage not disappear on its own?

Show answer

Because the price is not allowed to rise. In a free market the shortage would push the price up until it vanished.

05

Price floor

★ Exam
Definition

A minimum price buyers are required to pay for a good or service.

Textbook pages 77 and 82–83, Figures 8.3 and 8.4.

In your textbook’s butter market the equilibrium is $1.00 a pound and 10 million pounds. To help dairy farmers the government sets a floor of $1.20. Farmers want to sell more and buyers want to buy less.

To matter, a floor has to be set above the equilibrium price. Floors have been used for wheat, milk, sugar and butter, and for airfares and trucking before the 1970s.

Watch out. A floor is a minimum, so it does its work when it is high — above equilibrium. A floor below the market price changes nothing.

Textbook

Butter at $1.20. Your textbook’s Figure 8.4: a floor $0.20 above the $1.00 equilibrium.

Numeric

Reading the schedule. At $1.20, quantity supplied is 12 million pounds and quantity demanded is 9 million.

Textbook

School lunches. The surplus food the government buys up under agricultural floors is given to schools as “bonus foods”.

How it connects. It is the other price control (entry 1); it matters only if binding (entry 3), and then it creates a surplus (entry 6). The minimum wage (entry 7) is the best-known example. It helps some sellers (entry 11) and pushes quality (entry 14) too high.

Check yourself

5.1 The equilibrium price of wheat is $5 a bushel. Is a $6 floor binding? A $4 floor?

Show answer

$6 is binding — it is above equilibrium. $4 is below the market price and has no effect.

5.2 Under a binding floor, which is larger: quantity demanded or quantity supplied?

Show answer

Quantity supplied. The high price draws out more supply and puts buyers off.

06

The surplus from a price floor

★ Exam
Definition

A binding floor creates a persistent surplus: quantity supplied minus quantity demanded at the floor price.

Textbook pages 83–84, Figure 8.4.

At $1.20, producers want to sell 12 million pounds (point B) and consumers want to buy 9 million (point A). The surplus is 12 − 9 = 3 million pounds of butter that nobody buys at the legal price.

What happens to it depends on the government. With farm products it usually buys the surplus, stores it, gives it away or destroys it — or pays farmers not to produce. The European Union once owned a “butter mountain”.

Watch out. This “surplus” is unsold goods. It has nothing to do with consumer surplus or producer surplus — in fact a floor reduces total surplus.

Textbook

Butter. 12 million pounds supplied − 9 million demanded = a surplus of 3 million pounds.

Numeric

A higher floor. At $1.40 the schedule gives 14.0 supplied and 8.0 demanded: a surplus of 6 million pounds.

Textbook

Your textbook’s multiple-choice 2. A $5 floor where buyers want 100 units and sellers offer 200 creates a surplus of 100 units.

How it connects. It is what a binding price floor (entry 5) produces. In the labor market it is the surplus the minimum wage (entry 7) creates. The quantity sold is the smaller number, so the quantity falls (entry 8) and there is a deadweight loss (entry 10). Sales go to the wrong sellers: inefficient allocation (entry 12).

Check yourself

6.1 A floor of $12 is set where quantity supplied is 900 and quantity demanded is 650. What is the surplus, and how many units are sold?

Show answer

Surplus = 900 − 650 = 250. Only 650 are sold — sellers cannot sell what buyers will not buy.

6.2 Is a surplus under a floor a sign that the market is doing well?

Show answer

No. It is output nobody wants at the legal price, and the trades the floor prevents are a loss to buyers and sellers.

07

Minimum wage

★ Exam
Definition

A legal floor on the wage rate, which is the market price of labor.

Textbook pages 82 and 84.

Read the labor market like any other: the wage is on the vertical axis, firms are the buyers (demand) and workers are the sellers (supply). A minimum wage above the equilibrium wage is a binding price floor, so more people want to work than firms will hire: a surplus of labor.

Two numbers come out of every minimum-wage question: how many are hired at the new wage (the quantity demanded), and how many want to work but cannot find a job (the surplus).

Watch out. Your textbook calls the people who cannot find jobs “unemployed”. In this course say a surplus of labor: it is the same subtraction, in the language of supply and demand.

Textbook

Your textbook’s free-response 1. Equilibrium wage $6 and 1,800 workers. At an $8 minimum, 2,600 want to work and 1,000 are hired: a surplus of 1,600 workers.

Numeric

Multiple-choice 4 and 5. A $10 minimum where firms hire 50 and 110 want to work: 50 benefit from the higher wage and 60 cannot find work.

Textbook

Rosetta. In the Belgian film your textbook describes, an eager worker loses her job to the owner’s reluctant son, because hiring her for less than the minimum wage is illegal.

How it connects. It is a price floor (entry 5) in the labor market, so it creates the surplus (entry 6) of entry 6 — here, a surplus of labor.

Check yourself

7.1 The equilibrium wage is $9 and 500 workers are hired. A $11 minimum wage makes 420 hired and 580 willing to work. How many lose their jobs, and what is the surplus of labor?

Show answer

80 lose their jobs (500 − 420). The surplus of labor is 160 (580 − 420).

7.2 Who benefits from a binding minimum wage?

Show answer

The workers who are still hired: they earn more. The workers who would have been hired at the equilibrium wage and now are not lose.

08

Inefficiently low quantity

★ Exam
Definition

Both a binding ceiling and a binding floor reduce the quantity bought and sold below the equilibrium quantity.

Textbook pages 81 and 84.

The quantity traded is always the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied: you cannot buy what nobody sells, or sell what nobody buys. Under a ceiling that is the quantity supplied; under a floor it is the quantity demanded. Either way it is less than at equilibrium.

Your textbook says it outright: you might think a floor and a ceiling have opposite effects, but both reduce the quantity of a good bought and sold.

Watch out. A floor does not raise the quantity sold just because it raises the quantity supplied. Supplied is not sold.

Textbook

Apartments. The $800 ceiling cuts apartments rented from 2.0 million to 1.8 million.

Textbook

Butter. The $1.20 floor cuts butter sold from 10 million pounds to 9 million.

Illustrative

Both directions, one result. Push the price below equilibrium or above it — either way you move away from the crossing, and the quantity traded falls.

How it connects. It follows from the shortage (entry 4) and the surplus (entry 6), it is a move away from allocative efficiency (entry 9), and it is the reason for the deadweight loss (entry 10).

Check yourself

8.1 Under a binding ceiling, is the quantity traded the quantity demanded or the quantity supplied?

Show answer

The quantity supplied — the smaller of the two. Buyers cannot buy more than sellers offer.

8.2 A floor raises the price of butter to $1.20. Does more or less butter change hands than at equilibrium?

Show answer

Less: 9 million pounds instead of 10. The extra butter supplied is not bought.

09

Allocative efficiency

★ ⚑
Definition

A market is allocatively efficient when it produces the quantity at which the last unit is worth to buyers exactly what it costs sellers to supply — where demand meets supply.

Not in this module. Your textbook’s nearest phrase is “efficient in allocation”, in Module 3 (page 18).

⚑ Not in Module 8 — learn it from here. Your course says a price control on an efficient market “can only decrease allocative efficiency”. The word allocative does not appear anywhere in your textbook.

At the equilibrium quantity every unit that buyers value more than it costs to supply is traded, and no unit is traded that costs more than it is worth. That is as much value as the market can create. A competitive market left alone gets there.

So a price control on an efficient market can only make things worse: it moves the quantity away from that point. Your course states it as a rule — government intervention in a market that produces the efficient quantity can only decrease allocative efficiency.

Watch out. The rule is about a market that was efficient to begin with. Your textbook notes that in a market dominated by a single seller a price control can sometimes move it closer to efficiency — a case for later in the course.

Textbook

Your textbook’s assumption. Module 8 assumes the markets it studies are efficient before the controls are imposed (page 78).

Numeric

The 1.8 millionth apartment. Under the ceiling, a renter would pay $1,200 for it and a landlord would supply it for $800. A trade worth $400 to both together does not happen.

Illustrative

Measuring the loss. The value of all the trades a control prevents is the deadweight loss of entry 10.

How it connects. It is what the inefficiently low quantity (entry 8) departs from, and the deadweight loss (entry 10) measures how far.

Check yourself

9.1 Why can a price control on an efficient market never raise allocative efficiency?

Show answer

Because the market was already trading exactly the units worth more than they cost. A control can only prevent some of those trades, or force others.

9.2 At the quantity traded under a binding ceiling, compare what buyers would pay for one more unit with what sellers need for it.

Show answer

Buyers would pay more than sellers need — so that unit should be traded and is not. That gap is the sign of inefficiency.

10

Deadweight loss

★ ⚑
Definition

The loss of total surplus — to buyers and sellers together — from the trades a price control prevents. On the diagram it is the triangle between the demand and supply curves, from the quantity actually traded to the equilibrium quantity.

Not in this module. Your textbook first draws a deadweight loss in Module 9, for a quota; for price controls it only mentions one, on page 95.

⚑ Not in Module 8 — learn it from here. Your course defines deadweight loss as the losses to buyers and sellers from government intervention in an efficient market, and released AP exams ask for it more than any other idea in this topic. Module 8 never uses the phrase.

Each unit between the quantity traded and the equilibrium quantity is worth more to a buyer than it costs a seller. Every one of those trades is lost. Add up the lost gains and you get a triangle.

Your textbook’s schedules are straight lines, so the triangle can be computed exactly. Under the $800 ceiling, 1.8 million apartments are rented; buyers would pay $1,200 for the last of them and sellers would supply it for $800. The deadweight loss is ½ × 0.2 million × $400 = $40 million a month. Under the $1.20 butter floor, 9 million pounds are sold; buyers value the last pound at $1.20 and sellers would supply it for $0.90. The deadweight loss is ½ × 1 million × $0.30 = $150,000.

Watch out. Deadweight loss is not the shortage or the surplus. The shortage is a horizontal gap at the controlled price; the triangle’s base is the gap between the quantity traded and the equilibrium quantity — 0.2 million apartments, not 0.4.

Numeric

Apartments. ½ × (2.0 − 1.8) million × ($1,200 − $800) = $40 million a month.

Numeric

Butter. ½ × (10 − 9) million × ($1.20 − $0.90) = $150,000.

Textbook

Misallocation makes it worse. Your textbook’s Lees would pay $1,500 for an apartment that George, who values it at under $850, keeps. If the wrong renters get the apartments, the true loss is bigger than the triangle.

How it connects. It measures how far the market has moved from allocative efficiency (entry 9); its base is the lost quantity (entry 8), which comes from the shortage (entry 4) or the surplus (entry 6). Who gains and who loses (entry 11) is how the rest of the surplus is shared out.

Check yourself

10.1 A ceiling cuts the quantity traded from 100 to 80. At 80 units buyers would pay $30 and sellers need $20. What is the deadweight loss?

Show answer

½ × (100 − 80) × ($30 − $20) = ½ × 20 × $10 = $100.

10.2 On a diagram with a binding floor, where is the deadweight loss triangle?

Show answer

Between the demand and supply curves, from the quantity buyers purchase at the floor to the equilibrium quantity, with its point at the equilibrium.

11

Who gains and who loses

★ Exam
Definition

Every price control has winners and losers. A ceiling helps the buyers who still get the good at the lower price; a floor helps the sellers who still sell at the higher price.

Textbook pages 81–82 and 85.

Under the rent ceiling the tenants who hold an apartment pay $800 instead of $1,000 — a gain taken from landlords. But landlords lose, the 400,000 who cannot find an apartment lose, and the deadweight loss is lost to everyone.

Under the butter floor the farmers who sell get $1.20 instead of $1.00, paid by buyers; buyers who are priced out lose, and taxpayers pay for any surplus the government buys. Your textbook’s explanation of why controls survive is that the winners are often better organised than the losers.

Watch out. A ceiling does not help “buyers” as a group. It helps the ones who still manage to buy; the ones squeezed out by the shortage are worse off.

Textbook

Rent control. Hurts most residents but gives a small minority much cheaper housing, and that minority is vocal.

Textbook

Your textbook’s Check Your Understanding 2. A ceiling below equilibrium makes some people who want the good worse off, and all producers worse off.

Illustrative

A minimum wage. Workers who keep their jobs gain; workers who would have been hired at the market wage, and the firms that would have hired them, lose.

How it connects. The gains and losses are shares of the total surplus the deadweight loss (entry 10) shrinks. They follow from a ceiling (entry 2) or a floor (entry 5), and they explain why governments use controls (entry 16).

Check yourself

11.1 Under a binding price floor on milk, who gains?

Show answer

The farmers who still sell, at the higher price. Buyers lose, and so do farmers who cannot sell all they produce unless the government buys the surplus.

11.2 A rent ceiling transfers money from landlords to some tenants. Is that transfer the deadweight loss?

Show answer

No. The transfer is a gain to one side and a loss to the other. The deadweight loss is the value lost to both sides from the apartments that are no longer rented.

12

Inefficient allocation

○ Background
Definition

The good goes to the wrong people. Under a ceiling, some buyers who value it little get it while others who value it highly do not; under a floor, the sellers who would sell most cheaply are not always the ones who make the sale.

Textbook pages 80 and 84.

With a shortage, the price no longer decides who gets the apartments, so luck and connections do. Your textbook’s example: the Lees would pay $1,500 and cannot find a flat, while George, who would give his up for $850, keeps one at $800.

It is marked background: a real cost of price controls and a good explanation, but not a phrase the exam asks for. Use it to explain why the loss can be larger than the deadweight-loss triangle.

Watch out. This is a different cost from the lower quantity. Even the apartments that are rented go to the wrong people.

Textbook

The Lees and George. A sublease at $1,200 would make both better off; rent control makes it illegal.

Textbook

Rosetta. Under a minimum wage, the eager worker loses her job to the reluctant one.

Illustrative

Game-day parking. Friends of homeowners get the cheap spaces while serious fans give up.

How it connects. It comes with the shortage (entry 4) and the surplus (entry 6), and it is one reason people spend resources (entry 13) coping with them — or turn to black markets (entry 15).

Check yourself

12.1 Why does a shortage under rent control lead to misallocation?

Show answer

Because the price can no longer ration apartments, so something else does — luck, connections, or who already has one.

12.2 Why is this entry background?

Show answer

It explains a cost of price controls, but the framework measures that cost as deadweight loss and does not ask for this term.

13

Wasted resources

○ Background
Definition

People spend money, effort and time coping with the shortage or surplus a control creates.

Textbook pages 80 and 84–85.

In 1979 controls on petrol prices caused shortages, and millions of Americans spent hours a week queuing at petrol stations. The time had an opportunity cost: wages not earned, leisure not enjoyed. Surplus farm products that are destroyed, or rot in storage, are waste too.

Watch out. The waste is real even though no money changes hands. Time spent in a queue has an opportunity cost.

Textbook

Petrol lines, 1979. Hours each week spent queuing because prices were held down.

Textbook

The Lees’ search. Months of spare time spent hunting for an apartment.

Textbook

Surplus food. Stored produce that goes “out of condition” and is thrown away.

How it connects. It follows from inefficient allocation (entry 12) and the shortage (entry 4).

Check yourself

13.1 What is the cost of waiting two hours in a petrol queue?

Show answer

The opportunity cost of the time — what you would have earned or enjoyed instead.

13.2 Name one way a price floor wastes resources.

Show answer

Surplus output the government buys and then destroys, or that spoils in storage; or workers spending hours searching for jobs.

14

Inefficient quality

○ Background
Definition

A ceiling leads sellers to offer inefficiently low quality; a floor leads them to offer inefficiently high quality.

Textbook pages 81 and 85.

Under rent control landlords cannot raise rents to pay for repairs and can find tenants anyway, so rent-controlled flats are notoriously badly maintained. Under a floor, sellers who cannot compete on price compete on extras instead: when transatlantic fares were fixed high, airlines served lavish meals that went uneaten.

Watch out. Higher quality is not always better. It is inefficient if buyers would rather have the lower price than the extra quality.

Textbook

Rent-controlled flats. Rarely painted, frequent electrical and plumbing problems.

Textbook

The Scandinavian Sandwich. An airline’s towering snack under fixed fares, which led to a conference to define a sandwich.

Textbook

After deregulation. Fares fell, in-flight service got worse, and the number of passengers grew several hundred per cent.

How it connects. Quality falls under a price ceiling (entry 2) and rises under a price floor (entry 5).

Check yourself

14.1 Why do landlords under rent control let buildings decay?

Show answer

They cannot charge more for improvements, and tenants are easy to find at the low rent.

14.2 Why is very high quality under a floor a waste?

Show answer

Buyers would rather pay less and get less. The extra quality costs more than it is worth to them.

15

Black markets

○ Background
Definition

A black market is one in which goods or services are bought and sold illegally — because selling them at all is illegal, or because the price charged breaks a price control.

Textbook pages 81 and 85.

A shortage creates buyers willing to pay more than the legal price, so some sellers will take it under the table: illegal sublets, cash on the side. Under a floor, workers desperate for jobs may work “off the books” for less than the minimum wage — “black labor” in southern Europe.

Watch out. Black-market prices are not a guide to the market price. They are usually higher than the price an uncontrolled market would set.

Textbook

Illegal subletting. New York tenants who live elsewhere sublet rent-controlled flats at two or three times the controlled rent.

Textbook

Cash on the side. A landlord asks for a few hundred extra each month in cash.

Textbook

Black labor. Work off the books below the minimum wage, common in Italy and Spain.

How it connects. It grows out of the price ceiling (entry 2)’s shortage and the misallocation (entry 12) it causes.

Check yourself

15.1 Why does a binding ceiling create a black market?

Show answer

Some buyers value the good above the legal price, and some sellers are willing to break the law to collect the difference.

15.2 Why might renters overestimate what rents would be without control?

Show answer

Because the black-market prices they hear about are higher than the price a free market would set.

16

Why governments use price controls

○ Background
Definition

Despite their costs, price controls persist because they benefit some buyers or sellers who are well organised, because people do not see what prices would be without them, and because officials do not always understand supply and demand.

Textbook pages 81–82 and 85.

Your textbook is blunt: it is a great mistake to suppose that economic policies in the real world are always sensible or well informed. But the main reason is the one in entry 11 — every control has winners, and they defend it.

Watch out. Explaining why a policy exists is not the same as defending it. You can be asked to describe a control’s effects without being asked whether it is a good idea.

Textbook

Rent control. A small, vocal group of tenants benefits a great deal.

Textbook

Farm price supports. Introduced in the 1930s to help farmers, and still in force for sugar and dairy products.

Illustrative

A debate. Should the federal government impose a $15 minimum wage? Argue each side using a supply-and-demand diagram and its deadweight loss.

How it connects. It rests on who gains and who loses (entry 11), and it is why the price controls (entry 1) of this module exist at all.

Check yourself

16.1 Give one reason a costly price control might survive.

Show answer

The people who gain from it are better organised and more vocal than the people it harms.

16.2 Why might voters support rent control even though it creates a shortage?

Show answer

They may not realise how much lower the market rent would be than the black-market prices they hear about, and the tenants who benefit are visible.

  5. 800 newly want to work: 2,600 − 1,800.
  6. 800 lose their jobs: 1,800 − 1,000.

A seventh point your course would add: the surplus of labor is 2,600 − 1,000 = 1,600, and the deadweight loss is the triangle between the curves from 1,000 workers to 1,800.

The five things to get right from Module 8

  1. Check whether it binds. Ceiling below equilibrium, floor above — otherwise nothing happens.
  2. Ceiling → shortage, floor → surplus, measured at the controlled price.
  3. Both cut the quantity traded. It is always the smaller of Qd and Qs.
  4. Deadweight loss is the triangle, from the quantity traded to the equilibrium quantity — not the shortage or the surplus.
  5. Say who gains and who loses. Every control has both.
