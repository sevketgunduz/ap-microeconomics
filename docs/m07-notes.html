The headline. Module 7 delivers half of the Micro statement it serves, and delivers it completely. MKT-4.B.2 (CED p. 54) says a shift changes price, quantity, consumer surplus, producer surplus and total economic surplus, and that "the impact of the change depends on the price elasticities of demand and supply". Krugman's six pages give all eight price-and-quantity results and nothing else: consumer surplus, producer surplus, total surplus, elastic and elasticity each occur 0 times in pp. 71–76. They are in the book — Modules 46–49 — but 400 pages on. The student guide supplies the shift version of both as two flagged entries. The second thing to know: the Macro edition of these notes is built around the Macro CED's sample question on the "indeterminate" equilibrium price. The Micro CED has no such question — indeterminate, ambiguous and simultaneous each occur 0 times in it. The double shift is still examined in practice (section 1), but on the evidence of released exams, not of the framework.

The enduring understanding, verbatim (CED p. 54):

MKT-4 — "Although equilibria are stable, an economy can move from one equilibrium to another if market conditions change."

That is Module 7 in one sentence: Module 6 was the "stable", this is the "move from one equilibrium to another".

Krugman's two objectives (p. 71):

  1. How equilibrium price and quantity are affected when there is a change in either supply or demand
  2. How equilibrium price and quantity are affected when there is a simultaneous change in both

Both are on-syllabus.

Verdict on alignment. Complete on price and quantity, silent on surplus and elasticity. The two missing clauses are not gaps in the book — Module 49 shows how a price change affects consumer and producer surplus (pp. 486–492), and Modules 46–48 are elasticity — but in Krugman's order a student reaches Module 7 without either. In the CED's order Topics 2.3–2.6 (elasticity, equilibrium surplus) come before Topic 2.7, so a class following the framework has the tools already. Either way, the student guide's entries 15 and 16 give the shift version of each.

The knowledge elements, in the CED's own words

Learning objective MKT-4.B (CED p. 54):

a. Define a surplus and shortage. b. Explain (using graphs where appropriate) how changes in underlying conditions and shocks to a competitive market can alter price, quantity, consumer surplus, and producer surplus. c. Calculate (using data from a graph or table as appropriate) changes in price, quantity, consumer surplus, and producer surplus in response to changes in market conditions or market disequilibrium.
MKT-4.B.1 — "Whenever markets experience imbalances—creating disequilibrium prices and quantities, surpluses, and shortages—market forces drive price and quantity toward equilibrium." (CED p. 54)
MKT-4.B.2 — "Factors that shift the market demand and market supply curves cause price, quantity, consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total economic surplus (within that market) to change. The impact of the change depends on the price elasticities of demand and supply." (CED p. 54)

Set it beside the Macro statement for the same module, MKT-2.G.1 (Macro CED p. 39): "Changes in the determinants of supply and/or demand result in a new equilibrium price and quantity." The Micro sentence names five variables against two, adds elasticity, and — note — drops the "and/or". The simultaneous case is implied by "the market demand and market supply curves", not stated.

The CED's own sample question — a different one from Macro's

The Micro CED's sample question 3 (Exam Information p. 127; key E, Skill 2.A, MKT-4.B, Unit 2, p. 135) is the only published item for this learning objective:

Which of the following occurs if the price of oranges is below the equilibrium price? (A) There is a surplus of oranges. (B) The quantity supplied of oranges is greater than the quantity demanded. (C) The quantity supplied of oranges is greater than the quantity sold. (D) The quantity purchased of oranges is greater than the quantity sold. (E) The quantity demanded of oranges is greater than the quantity supplied.

It tests MKT-4.B.1 — Module 6's shortage — not the shift. Use it here anyway, as the bridge: the shortage it describes is the step in Module 7's adjustment chain that makes the price rise after an increase in demand. The student guide's FRQ 3 and the test's question 13 reproduce it verbatim.

Neither is absent from the book. But note what Module 49 does and does not do: it shows how a change in price affects each surplus along a fixed curve, and shift occurs 0 times in it. The step "a supply shift moves the price along an unchanged demand curve, so consumer surplus moves opposite to the price" is the bridge between Module 7 and Module 49, and it is written nowhere in the book. Entry 15 supplies it:

  • When supply shifts, consumer surplus certainly moves opposite to the price (buyers slide along

their unchanged demand curve).

  • When demand shifts, producer surplus certainly moves with the price (sellers slide along

their unchanged supply curve).

  • The other side's surplus depends on how the shifted curve moved; with straight lines shifted in

parallel it moves with the equilibrium quantity, and so does total surplus.

The guide checks this on its own diagram (D: P = 14 − ½*Q*, S: P = 2 + ½*Q*): at the start consumer and producer surplus are 36 each; after demand rises by 2, both are 49.

Entry 16 uses one demand shift (P = 14 − ½*Q* to P = 19 − ½*Q*) against a steep supply curve (P = 2*Q* − 16: price 8 → 12, quantity 12 → 14) and a flat one (P = 6.5 + Q/8: price 8 → 9, quantity 12 → 20). Same directions, very different sizes — which is all MKT-4.B.2 asks at this point.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

All five of the Macro reading's corrections hold here unchanged — a curve, not a price; one curve unless two are moved; ambiguous is not unchanged; say which variable is indeterminate; draw the second panel. Three Micro additions:

"Surplus" now means two things in the same topic

Topic 2.7's learning objective says "define a surplus and shortage" in part (a) and "consumer surplus, and producer surplus" in part (b). Students meet both senses in one sentence of the framework. Say it outright: a surplus is unsold goods above equilibrium; consumer and producer surplus are gains from trade. A market with a surplus in the first sense has less total surplus in the second.

The surplus you can be sure of belongs to the curve that did not move

Students guess, or say "consumers lose" for every price rise. Give them the rule and the reason: whichever curve did not shift is the one its owners slide along, so their surplus is the one that is settled. Supply shifts → consumers' surplus is settled. Demand shifts → producers' surplus is settled.

Slope changes the size, never the direction

A student who has just met steep curves sometimes "corrects" a direction: "supply is inelastic, so the price won't rise". It will rise — by more. Keep the rule from entry 16 on the board: elasticity decides how much, the shift decides which way.
