This paper covers the measurement, not the verdict. Module 49 builds consumer and producer surplus; Module 50 adds them up and uses the total to judge whether a market is efficient. Topic 2.6 needs both, so set this paper between the two modules rather than at the end of the topic.

1. A student says: "I'd have paid up to £28 for that concert ticket. I got it for £19 and I'd have been just as happy to skip it at £29."

What is this student's willingness to pay, and what does the £29 remark tell you?

  • (A) £19, because that is what the ticket cost.
  • (B) £29, because that is the price at which they would refuse.
  • (C) £28 — the maximum price at which they would still buy. The £29 remark confirms it: one penny above their maximum and the purchase stops being worth making.
  • (D) £9, the difference between the two figures.
  • (E) It cannot be determined without knowing the student's income.

2. A market demand curve sits at $14 when the quantity demanded is 800 units.

What does that tell you about the 800th buyer?

  • (A) That the 800th buyer's willingness to pay is $14 — they are the last buyer willing to take a unit at that price, and every earlier buyer would have paid more.
  • (B) That the 800th buyer gains $14 of consumer surplus.
  • (C) That total spending by buyers is $14 × 800.
  • (D) That the 800th buyer is the one with the highest willingness to pay in the market.
  • (E) That every buyer values the good at $14.

7. The price of a good falls and consumer surplus rises. A student explains: "Consumer surplus rose because everyone who was already buying now pays less."

What is missing from the explanation?

  • (A) Nothing — that is the whole of it.
  • (B) It should mention that producer surplus rose too.
  • (C) It should mention that total spending fell.
  • (D) It should mention that willingness to pay rose when the price fell.
  • (E) The new buyers. The gain has two parts: a rectangle for buyers who were already trading, and a triangle for buyers drawn in by the lower price. The explanation collects only the rectangle.

8. At a price of $30, 400 units are bought. The price falls to $24 and 500 units are bought.

By how much does consumer surplus rise?

  • (D) $3,000
  • (E) $300

9. A collector refuses $400 for a signed photograph but says she would let it go at $450.

What is her cost, in the sense this module uses the word?

  • (A) Whatever she originally paid for the photograph.
  • (B) $50, the gap between the two figures.
  • (C) $400, because that is the offer on the table.
  • (D) $450 — the lowest price at which she is willing to sell.
  • (E) Nothing, since she spent no money producing it.

10. A student sells a used textbook for $30. She bought it new for $110 two years ago, and she spends nothing to make it available for sale.

What is her cost of selling, and why?

  • (A) $110, the price she paid.
  • (B) Zero, because she spends no money to sell it.
  • (C) $80, the fall in the book's value.
  • (D) $140, the two figures added.
  • (E) The opportunity cost of no longer having the book — the lowest price at which she would part with it. What she paid two years ago is sunk and does not enter the decision at all.

  • (D) That sellers receive $9 of producer surplus on that unit.

16. The price of wheat rises from $5 to $7 a bushel, and the quantity supplied rises from 1 million to 1.5 million bushels.

Which description of the gain in producer surplus is complete?

  • (A) $2 on each of the original 1 million bushels, plus a triangle for the extra 500,000 bushels whose sellers gain the difference between $7 and their own costs.
  • (B) $2 × 1.5 million, since every bushel now sells for $2 more.
  • (C) $2 × 1 million, and nothing else.
  • (D) The extra 500,000 bushels × $7.
  • (E) Nothing — a price rise transfers surplus from producers to consumers.

17. In a market that clears, the last buyer served has a willingness to pay of exactly the market price.

What is that buyer's consumer surplus, and why does it matter?

  • (A) Equal to the price, since they pay full value.
  • (B) Negative, since they barely wanted the good.
  • (C) Undefined, because indifferent buyers cannot be modelled.
  • (D) It cannot be determined without their income.
  • (E) Zero — willingness to pay minus price is nothing. It matters because it is why the consumer-surplus area tapers to a point at the equilibrium quantity, and because it is the first step in showing that the market stops trading in exactly the right place.

18. Why do both the consumer-surplus and producer-surplus areas come to a point at the equilibrium quantity rather than ending in a vertical edge?

  • (A) Because at that quantity the last buyer's willingness to pay and the last seller's cost have both converged on the price, so each of them gains nothing and the areas close.
  • (B) Because the curves are drawn as straight lines.
  • (C) Because beyond that quantity consumer surplus becomes negative.
  • (D) Because the two areas must be equal in size.
  • (E) Because the diagram is cut off at the equilibrium quantity by convention.

19. A competitive market generates $4,200 of consumer surplus and $2,800 of producer surplus.

What is total economic surplus, and what is it used for?

  • (A) $1,400 — the difference, which measures the gap between buyers' and sellers' gains.
  • (B) $4,200, since consumer surplus is what economists mean by the gain from a market.
  • (C) It cannot be computed; the two are measured in different units.
  • (D) $7,000 — the two added together. Economists use it to judge whether a market is efficient: a competitive equilibrium maximises it.
  • (E) $7,000, which measures the total revenue earned in the market.

20. In a five-buyer, five-seller market, three units trade at a price of $25, and three units also trade at a price of $35. At $25 consumer surplus is $64 and producer surplus is $30; at $35 consumer surplus is $34 and producer surplus is $60.

What does the comparison show?

  • (A) That $25 is the better price, because buyers gain more.
  • (B) That total economic surplus is $94 at both prices: the price decides how the gain is divided between buyers and sellers, while the quantity traded decides how big the gain is.
  • (C) That the market is inefficient at both prices, since neither maximises consumer surplus.
  • (D) That producer surplus and consumer surplus always sum to a constant, whatever the quantity.
  • (E) That the two prices cannot both be equilibria, so one of the figures must be wrong.

21. In a competitive market, demand meets the price axis at $100, supply meets it at $20, and the market clears at a price of $60 with 500 units traded.

Calculate consumer surplus, producer surplus and total economic surplus.

  • (A) $20,000 · $20,000 · $40,000
  • (B) $40,000 · $40,000 · $80,000
  • (C) $10,000 · $10,000 · $20,000
  • (D) $10,000 · $20,000 · $30,000
  • (E) $30,000 · $10,000 · $40,000

22. A question gives a table of five named buyers with their willingness to pay and asks for consumer surplus at a stated price.

Why can the formula ½ × base × height not be used here?

  • (A) Because the formula only works for producer surplus.
  • (B) Because the demand curve is a staircase, not a straight line. With five buyers the area is a stack of rectangles — subtract the price from each buyer's willingness to pay and add. The triangle is what that staircase becomes when there are many buyers.
  • (C) Because a price must be an equilibrium price before any area can be computed.
  • (D) Because consumer surplus is undefined when there are few buyers.
  • (E) Because the table gives willingness to pay rather than prices.

23. A graph shows a competitive market in which the supply and demand curves cross at price P2 and quantity Q2. P3 lies above P2 and P1 below it; Q1 is smaller than Q2 and Q3 larger.

Total economic surplus will be maximized at which of the following price and quantity combinations?

  • (A) P3 and Q3
  • (B) P3 and Q1
  • (C) P1 and Q1
  • (D) P2 and Q2
  • (E) P1 and Q3

24. Three figures are reported for the same market: the price × quantity sold, the gain to buyers over what they paid, and the gain to sellers over their costs.

Which is which?

  • (A) They are three names for total surplus.
  • (B) Price × quantity is consumer surplus; the other two are producer surplus and profit.
  • (C) Price × quantity is total revenue; the gain to buyers is consumer surplus; the gain to sellers is producer surplus. None of the three is profit, which would require subtracting all of a firm's costs.
  • (D) All three are measures of efficiency.
  • (E) Price × quantity is producer surplus; the gain to sellers is profit.

25. A fall in the cost of producing a good shifts supply to the right. The equilibrium price falls and the quantity rises.

What happens to each surplus, and why?

  • (A) Both fall, because the price is lower.
  • (B) Consumer surplus rises — the lower price enlarges the area below demand and above the price, and more buyers now trade. Producer surplus may rise or fall: sellers receive less per unit but sell more, and the supply curve itself has moved, so the area has to be measured against the new curve rather than reasoned from the price alone.
  • (C) Consumer surplus falls and producer surplus rises, as always when price changes.
  • (D) Consumer surplus rises and producer surplus necessarily falls, because the price fell.
  • (E) Neither changes, because the two effects cancel.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Willingness to pay · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Willingness to pay is the maximum price at which someone still buys. The student has named it twice: £28 is acceptable, £29 is not.

  • (A) ✗ £19 is the price paid. The difference between the two is this student's consumer surplus.
  • (B) ✗ £29 is the first price they would refuse, which is one penny above the maximum.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ £9 is neither — it is the gap between the maximum and the first refusal.
  • (E) ✗ Income shapes willingness to pay, but the student has already told you the number.

Question 2 — The demand curve as willingness to pay · Analysis

Answer: (A)

The height of a demand curve at a quantity is the willingness to pay of the marginal buyer — the last one served. Everyone to the left would have paid more.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Their surplus is willingness to pay minus price. At a price of $14 that buyer gains

nothing.

  • (C) ✗ That is total spending, a different quantity entirely.
  • (D) ✗ The highest willingness to pay is at the left-hand end of the curve.
  • (E) ✗ Then the curve would be horizontal. It slopes down precisely because buyers differ.

Question 3 — Individual consumer surplus · Application

Answer: (E)

$72 − $45 = $27.

  • (A) ✗ The seller's cost belongs to producer surplus. Consumer surplus needs only the buyer's

two numbers.

  • (B) ✗ The price paid.
  • (C) ✗ The willingness to pay.
  • (D) ✗ The two added, which measures nothing.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 4 — Total consumer surplus · Application

Answer: (D)

Only buyers whose willingness to pay is at least $36 trade: $50 − $36 = $14, $42 − $36 = $6, and $36 − $36 = $0. The $28 buyer does not buy. Total: $20.

  • (A) ✗ The four willingnesses to pay added together.
  • (B) ✗ Counts the $28 buyer, who does not trade, as though they gained nothing yet still appear.
  • (C) ✗ Uses only the second buyer's gain plus a miscount.
  • (D) ✓ Correct, including the buyer whose gain is exactly zero.
  • (E) ✗ The three traders' willingness to pay added, with the price subtracted once.

Question 5 — Consumer surplus as an area · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Krugman states it as a general principle: consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve but above the price.

  • (A) ✗ Below the price is what buyers spent.
  • (B) ✗ Both prepositions reversed.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ That is producer surplus.
  • (E) ✗ The rectangle is total revenue.

Question 6 — Consumer surplus as an area · Application

Answer: (B)

½ × 600 × ($90 − $30) = $18,000.

  • (A) ✗ Uses a height of $30 rather than $60.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The rectangle 600 × $90, with no halving.
  • (D) ✗ base × height without the ½. The area is a triangle.
  • (E) ✗ 600 × $45 — a height taken from the middle rather than the intercept.

Question 7 — A price fall and consumer surplus · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

A change in surplus has two parts, and the explanation given covers only the rectangle.

  • (A) ✗ It is half the answer, and worth half the marks.
  • (B) ✗ Producer surplus falls when the price falls.
  • (C) ✗ Total spending may rise or fall depending on elasticity, and it is not what the question

asks.

  • (D) ✗ Willingness to pay is a property of the buyer and does not move when the price moves.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, naming both pieces.

Question 8 — A price fall and consumer surplus · Application

Answer: (A)

Existing buyers: 400 × $6 = $2,400. New buyers: ½ × 100 × $6 = $300. Total rise: $2,700.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The rectangle alone — the error diagnosed in question 7.
  • (C) ✗ 100 × $6, treating the new buyers' gain as a rectangle.
  • (D) ✗ 500 × $6, as though every buyer including the new ones gained the full $6.
  • (E) ✗ The triangle alone.

Question 9 — A seller's cost · Analysis

Answer: (D)

A seller's cost is the lowest price at which they are willing to sell. She has named it: $450.

  • (A) ✗ What she paid is sunk and irrelevant — see question 10.
  • (B) ✗ $50 is the gap between the rejected offer and her minimum.
  • (C) ✗ $400 is an offer she turned down, so it is below her cost.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Spending no money does not mean giving nothing up.

Question 10 — Cost as opportunity cost · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Krugman's point exactly: the cost of selling a used textbook is not having it afterwards. The purchase price is sunk.

  • (A) ✗ The original price is gone whether she sells or keeps the book.
  • (B) ✗ Reaches a conclusion from the right observation — no money is spent — but misses the

opportunity cost entirely.

  • (C) ✗ Depreciation is an accounting idea and is not the seller's cost.
  • (D) ✗ Adds two numbers that do not belong together.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, and it names the sunk cost for what it is.

Question 11 — The supply curve as a list of costs · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The height of a supply curve at a quantity is the cost of the marginal seller. Everyone to the left has a lower cost.

  • (A) ✗ Then the curve would be horizontal.
  • (B) ✗ The cheapest seller is at the left-hand end.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Producer surplus is price minus cost; the curve's height is the cost itself.
  • (E) ✗ Total revenue is price × quantity.

Question 12 — Individual producer surplus · Application

Answer: (D)

$26 − $18 = $8.

  • (A) ✗ The seller's cost.
  • (B) ✗ The price received.
  • (C) ✗ The two added.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The buyer's willingness to pay belongs to consumer surplus.

Question 13 — Total producer surplus · Application

Answer: (E)

Only sellers whose cost is at most $20 trade: $20 − $5 = $15, $20 − $11 = $9, and $20 − $20 = $0. The $26 seller stays out. Total: $24.

  • (A) ✗ The second seller's gain alone.
  • (B) ✗ The four costs added together.
  • (C) ✗ Counts the $26 seller as trading at a loss, which nobody does voluntarily.
  • (D) ✗ The first seller's gain alone.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 14 — Producer surplus as an area · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Above the supply curve and below the price — both prepositions the reverse of the consumer rule.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ That is consumer surplus.
  • (C) ✗ The rectangle is total revenue.
  • (D) ✗ Below the supply curve measures nothing.
  • (E) ✗ Above both is outside the traded region altogether.

Question 15 — Producer surplus as an area · Application

Answer: (B)

½ × 300 × ($26 − $8) = $2,700.

  • (A) ✗ base × height without the ½.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ 300 × $26 — total revenue.
  • (D) ✗ Uses a height of $8 rather than $18.
  • (E) ✗ 300 × $13, halving the quantity's partner instead of the product.

Question 16 — A price rise and producer surplus · Analysis

Answer: (A)

A rectangle for the sellers already in the market, plus a triangle for those the higher price draws in. The mirror image of question 7.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The new 500,000 bushels do not each gain $2 — their sellers' costs are higher, so they

gain less.

  • (C) ✗ The rectangle alone.
  • (D) ✗ Multiplies the new quantity by the whole price rather than by a gain.
  • (E) ✗ A price rise raises producer surplus. Consumer surplus is what falls.

Question 17 — The marginal trader · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Willingness to pay minus price is zero. The buyer trades and gains nothing — and that is exactly why the area closes to a point.

  • (A) ✗ Paying full value means gaining nothing, not gaining the price.
  • (B) ✗ Nobody is forced to trade, so no buyer ends up worse off.
  • (C) ✗ Indifference is a perfectly ordinary case and the model handles it.
  • (D) ✗ Income does not enter; the two relevant numbers are equal by assumption.
  • (E) ✓ Correct, with both consequences.

Question 18 — The marginal trader · Analysis

Answer: (A)

At the equilibrium quantity the demand curve, the supply curve and the price all meet, so the last buyer and the last seller each gain nothing and both areas narrow to nothing.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Straight lines make the areas triangles, but step-shaped curves also close to a point.
  • (C) ✗ Consumer surplus never goes negative; those buyers simply do not trade.
  • (D) ✗ The two areas are usually different sizes.
  • (E) ✗ Not a convention — it is where the curves cross.

Question 19 — Total economic surplus · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Add the two: $4,200 + $2,800 = $7,000. That total is what efficiency is judged by, and a competitive equilibrium maximises it.

  • (A) ✗ Subtracts rather than adds, and the gap between the two has no standard use.
  • (B) ✗ Consumer surplus is one half of the gain.
  • (C) ✗ Both are measured in money, which is what makes adding them meaningful.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Right number, wrong meaning — total revenue is price × quantity and has nothing to do

with these two figures.

Question 20 — Total economic surplus · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

$64 + $30 = $94 and $34 + $60 = $94. The split moves a long way; the total does not move at all, because the same three units trade.

  • (A) ✗ "Better" for buyers, certainly. Efficiency is judged on the total, which is the same.
  • (B) ✓ Correct, with the general principle stated.
  • (C) ✗ Efficiency is not about maximising consumer surplus; it is about maximising the total.
  • (D) ✗ They sum to a constant only while the quantity is unchanged. Change the quantity and

the total changes.

  • (E) ✗ In a small market with step-shaped curves, a range of prices can clear the same quantity.

Both sets of figures are consistent.

Question 21 — Calculating the areas · Application

Answer: (C)

Consumer surplus ½ × 500 × ($100 − $60) = $10,000. Producer surplus ½ × 500 × ($60 − $20) = $10,000. Total $20,000.

  • (A) ✗ Doubles both triangles by dropping the ½.
  • (B) ✗ Uses the full height of the diagram, $80, for each area.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Computes the producer side with the wrong intercept.
  • (E) ✗ Measures the consumer triangle from $20 instead of $60.

Question 22 — Calculating the areas · Analysis

Answer: (B)

The triangle formula is for straight-line curves. Five buyers make a staircase, so the area is a stack of rectangles.

  • (A) ✗ It works for both areas, when the curves are straight.
  • (B) ✓ Correct, with the method that does apply.
  • (C) ✗ Surplus can be computed at any price, equilibrium or not.
  • (D) ✗ Consumer surplus is perfectly well defined with five buyers; it is simply computed

differently.

  • (E) ✗ Willingness to pay is exactly what is needed.

Question 23 — The published sample question · Application

Answer: (D)

Total economic surplus is largest at the competitive equilibrium, where the two curves cross: price P2 and quantity Q2. Any other quantity leaves mutually beneficial trades undone or forces trades whose cost exceeds their value.

  • (A) ✗ Q3 is beyond the equilibrium quantity: those extra units cost more to produce than buyers

value them, so the total falls.

  • (B) ✗ P3 with Q1 is the price-floor outcome — fewer trades, and a smaller total.
  • (C) ✗ P1 with Q1 restricts quantity below equilibrium, so gains from trade go unrealised.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Q3 cannot be supplied at the low price P1; and more than Q2 would reduce total surplus in

any case.

Question 24 — Surplus against revenue and profit · Analysis

Answer: (C)

Three different quantities with three different definitions, and none of them is profit.

  • (A) ✗ Only two of the three are components of total surplus.
  • (B) ✗ Price × quantity is revenue, not consumer surplus.
  • (C) ✓ Correct, including the warning about profit.
  • (D) ✗ Revenue measures no efficiency at all.
  • (E) ✗ Price × quantity is revenue; the gain over cost in this module is producer surplus, which

is not profit because fixed costs have not been deducted.

Question 25 — What a shift does to each area · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Consumer surplus unambiguously rises. The producer side is genuinely ambiguous, because the curve that bounds the area has itself moved.

  • (A) ✗ A lower price raises consumer surplus; it does not lower it.
  • (B) ✓ Correct, and it is honest about the half that cannot be settled by the price alone.
  • (C) ✗ True when the price changes because demand shifts. Here supply moved, so the

producer-surplus area is measured against a new curve.

  • (D) ✗ Reaches the right answer on the consumer side and overstates the producer side. A

rightward supply shift often raises producer surplus despite the lower price, because costs are lower and more units are sold.

  • (E) ✗ Both areas change.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 16 concept entries appear. The two areas and their arithmetic take nine items (3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 13, 15, 21 and 23), matching the calculate verb in MKT-4.A(c). The mirrored structure of the module is mirrored in the paper: questions 1 to 8 are the buyers' side and 9 to 16 the sellers', in the same order.

Bloom levels. Application 9 · Analysis 10 · Synthesis 6, a ratio of 1 : 1.1 : 0.7 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. This is the most application-weighted paper in Unit 2, and the justification is the learning objective: Topic 2.6 ends with "Calculate … areas of consumer surplus and producer surplus at equilibrium", and the published sample question for the topic is itself a graph-reading item.

Questions 19, 20 and 23 are the ones to discuss aloud. All three use the framework's phrase total economic surplus, which does not appear anywhere in the textbook. Question 20 is the key one: it shows two prices with wildly different splits and an identical total, which is the fact Module 50 turns into the efficiency argument.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: the price reported in place of the surplus (3B, 12B); willingness to pay or cost summed without subtracting (4A, 13B); the ½ dropped from the triangle (6D, 15A); total revenue offered as a surplus (5E, 14C, 19E, 24E); only the rectangle counted after a price change (8B, 16C); and the two prepositions swapped (5C, 14D).

Three distractors reach a right-looking conclusion by wrong reasoning, flagged in the solutions: 10B, 19E and 25D. The most useful is 25D — it gets the consumer side right and over-claims on the producer side, which is exactly the half-right answer a strong student gives.

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 5 · D 5 · E 5 — exactly even, with no letter repeating in consecutive questions.

What this paper deliberately does not test. Deadweight loss, taxes, and the proof that equilibrium maximises total surplus are all Module 50. Questions 19, 20 and 23 go as far as naming the total and observing that it depends on the quantity — and stop there.
