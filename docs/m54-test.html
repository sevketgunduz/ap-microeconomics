Solutions — every option explained

Maple Street Bakery's marginal products, from the table, are 12, 20, 28, 32, 23, 17, 15 and 13 loaves for the first to the eighth baker. Its average products are 12, 16, 20, 23, 23, 22, 21 and 20.

Question 1 — Production function · Analysis

Answer: (B)

A production function relates the quantity of an input to the quantity of output a firm produces from it. The bakery's is the relationship between bakers and loaves.

  • (A) ✗ Revenue is loaves times price; this relates output to money, not inputs to output.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The wage bill is a cost: it relates an input to the money paid for it.
  • (D) ✗ Price and quantity describe the market, not the firm's use of inputs.
  • (E) ✗ Cost as output varies is a cost relationship, built from the production function later.

Question 2 — Fixed input and variable input · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The lease fixes the oven for the period in question. Bakers can be hired each morning and flour is bought each morning, so both can be varied at any time.

  • (A) ✗ Reversed: the oven cannot be changed and the bakers can.
  • (B) ✗ The owner can hire or release bakers each morning.
  • (C) ✗ That describes the long run, when the oven can be varied too.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Flour is bought fresh each day, so it is variable.

Question 3 — Short run and long run · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The short run is defined by there being at least one fixed input, not by a number of months. Temporary staff can be added in the short run; the press, which cannot be changed until it arrives, is varied only in the long run, when every input can be varied.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The short run is a condition on inputs, not a length of time.
  • (C) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: the roles are reversed — the staff are variable and the press is fixed.
  • (D) ✗ Only at least one input need be fixed; the staff can be varied.
  • (E) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: adding a worker is a short-run adjustment; the long run needs every input to be variable.

Question 4 — Total product · Application

Answer: (E)

Total product rises from 115 to 147, so the two new bakers add 147 − 115 = 32 loaves (17 + 15).

  • (A) ✗ 147 is total product with 7 bakers, not the rise.
  • (B) ✗ 15 is only the seventh baker's marginal product.
  • (C) ✗ 23 + 17 = 40 adds the fifth and sixth bakers' marginal products, which belong to the hires before.
  • (D) ✗ 147 ÷ 7 = 21 is the average product of 7 bakers.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — Marginal product · Application

Answer: (C)

MPL = ΔQ ÷ ΔL = (1,600 − 1,300) ÷ (50 − 40) = 300 ÷ 10 = 30 bushels per added worker.

  • (A) ✗ 300 is the change in output, not divided by the change in workers.
  • (B) ✗ 1,600 ÷ 50 = 32 is the average product at 50 workers.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ 1,300 ÷ 40 = 32.5 is the average product at 40 workers.
  • (E) ✗ 300 ÷ 40 = 7.5 divides by the starting number of workers, not the change.

Question 6 — The slope of the total product curve · Application

Answer: (B)

Slope is rise over run: (84 − 75) ÷ (6 − 5) = 9 bushels per worker, the sixth worker's marginal product.

  • (A) ✗ 84 is the height of the curve at 6 workers.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ 84 ÷ 6 = 14 is the slope of a line from the origin: the average product.
  • (D) ✗ 75 − 64 = 11 is the slope of the segment before.
  • (E) ✗ 75 is the height of the curve at 5 workers.

Question 7 — Plotting marginal product between quantities · Application

Answer: (A)

The fifth worker's 11 bushels is the change as employment goes from 4 to 5, so the textbook plots it halfway, at 4.5.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ That is the convention for a level, such as a total, not for a change.
  • (C) ✗ 11 is the height of the point, not its position.
  • (D) ✗ 5.5 is where the sixth worker's marginal product, 9, is plotted.
  • (E) ✗ The textbook plots halfway, not at the lower number.

Question 8 — Diminishing marginal returns · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Marginal products are 1,000, 800, 600 and 400 pounds per kilowatt-hour. Each added kilowatt-hour adds less than the one before, while total ice still rises from 1,000 to 2,800.

  • (A) ✗ Total ice rises, but by less each time; diminishing marginal returns concern the added ice.
  • (B) ✗ The machine is the fixed input; it is the electricity that is increased.
  • (C) ✗ Increasing marginal returns would need rising marginal products.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Each marginal product is below the one before from the second kilowatt-hour on.

Question 9 — Average product · Application

Answer: (C)

Average product is total product divided by workers: 132 ÷ 6 = 22 loaves per baker.

  • (A) ✗ 17 is the sixth baker's marginal product, 132 − 115.
  • (B) ✗ 132 is total product.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ 23 is the average product of 5 bakers, 115 ÷ 5.
  • (E) ✗ 21 is the average product of 7 bakers, 147 ÷ 7.

Question 10 — Increasing marginal returns · Analysis

Answer: (E)

With two bakers the work can be divided, so each baker specializes and the pair does more than twice what one did before; the oven is not yet a constraint. Marginal product rises from 12 to 20.

  • (A) ✗ The bakers are equally skilled by assumption.
  • (B) ✗ Diminishing marginal returns would mean a lower marginal product, not a higher one.
  • (C) ✗ The oven and the technology are unchanged; this is a movement along the curve.
  • (D) ✗ The oven is the same, and it is shared.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 11 — Increasing marginal returns · Application

Answer: (A)

Marginal products rise 12, 20, 28, 32 and then fall to 23 with the fifth baker. Diminishing marginal returns set in with the fifth baker.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The fourth baker has the largest marginal product, 32: the last baker of the rising phase.
  • (C) ✗ The sixth is where average product first falls, 22 against 23, not where marginal product first falls.
  • (D) ✗ The first baker adds 12, the least of the first four.
  • (E) ✗ The eighth baker adds the least of the bakers since the peak, but the fall began three bakers earlier.

Question 12 — Marginal product pulls average product · Application

Answer: (D)

Average product with 6 bakers is 132 ÷ 6 = 22. With 7 it is 147 ÷ 7 = 21. The seventh baker's 15 is below the average, so it pulls the average down.

  • (A) ✗ Total product rises, but the average falls whenever the new baker adds less than the average.
  • (B) ✗ It would stay at 22 only if the seventh baker added exactly 22.
  • (C) ✗ 15 is the marginal product, not the average.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The simple average of 22 and 15 ignores that the 22 stands for six bakers.

Question 13 — Marginal product pulls average product · Analysis

Answer: (B)

An average rises only if the added unit is above it. The fourth baker's marginal product must exceed the old average of 20; it is in fact 92 − 60 = 32.

  • (A) ✗ Average product can rise while marginal product is falling, as long as it stays above the average.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Nothing makes the marginal product equal the new average; here it is well above it.
  • (D) ✗ A marginal product below the average would lower it.
  • (E) ✗ The new average lies between the old average and the marginal product, so the marginal product is above 23.

Question 14 — Marginal product pulls average product · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Diminishing marginal returns set in with the fifth baker, whose marginal product, 23, equals the average of the first four, 23. Average product therefore stays at 23 and falls only with the sixth baker, whose 17 is below it. Marginal product must fall to the average before it can pull it down.

  • (A) ✗ The fifth baker's marginal product equals the average, so it does not pull it down.
  • (B) ✗ They move apart: marginal product peaks at the fourth baker and falls while the average is still rising.
  • (C) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: average product does fall while total product rises, as it does with the sixth baker.
  • (D) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: average product rises up to the fourth baker.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 15 — Marginal product pulls average product · Application

Answer: (C)

Total product with 10 workers is 10 × 40 = 400, and the eleventh adds 40: 440. Average product is 440 ÷ 11 = 40. A marginal product equal to the average leaves the average unchanged.

  • (A) ✗ Divides the new total by the old number of workers.
  • (B) ✗ Leaves out the eleventh worker's output.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Adds the marginal product to the average, not to the total.
  • (E) ✗ Takes the marginal product as the total.

Question 16 — A bigger fixed input shifts the curves · Application

Answer: (A)

With the 10-ton machine the third kilowatt-hour adds 2,400 − 1,800 = 600 pounds. With the 15-ton machine it adds 4,800 − 3,600 = 1,200. More of the fixed input shifts the marginal product curve up.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The curve keeps its downward slope but moves up; the value at each quantity changes.
  • (C) ✗ 2,400 and 4,800 are total product, not marginal product.
  • (D) ✗ More of the fixed input raises the marginal product of the variable one.
  • (E) ✗ 800 and 1,600 are the second kilowatt-hour's marginal products.

Question 17 — A bigger fixed input shifts the curves · Analysis

Answer: (E)

Every number of workers now produces more, so the total product curve shifts up. Each worker also adds more, so the marginal product curve shifts up. With acreage still fixed, it still slopes downward.

  • (A) ✗ A movement along the curve would be a change in the number of workers.
  • (B) ✗ If total product is higher at every quantity, marginal product is higher too.
  • (C) ✗ Diminishing marginal returns remain, so the curve still slopes downward.
  • (D) ✗ Output rose, so the curves move up.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 18 — Diminishing returns raise marginal cost · Application

Answer: (B)

One more pizza needs 1/6 of a worker's day: $60 ÷ 6 = $10.

  • (A) ✗ $60 is the cost of a worker, not of a pizza.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ $60 ÷ 12 = $5 is the cost with the third worker.
  • (D) ✗ $60 ÷ 10 = $6 is the cost with the fourth worker.
  • (E) ✗ Multiplies $60 by 6, instead of dividing.

Question 19 — Diminishing returns raise marginal cost · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The extra cost of one more pizza is the wage divided by the marginal product. With the wage constant, a falling marginal product makes each added pizza cost more.

  • (A) ✗ Every worker is paid the same.
  • (B) ✗ Fixed cost does not change with output.
  • (C) ✗ Total product keeps rising; it is marginal product that falls.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Right pattern, wrong channel: a worker costs $60 whatever the average product.

Question 20 — Diminishing returns are not diseconomies of scale · Application

Answer: (C)

Every input was doubled, so a doubling of output would be 400. Output rose to 360, a factor of 360 ÷ 200 = 1.8: less than the inputs. That is decreasing returns to scale.

  • (A) ✗ Diminishing marginal returns hold the other inputs fixed; here every input rose.
  • (B) ✗ Increasing returns to scale would give more than 400.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Constant returns to scale would give exactly 400.
  • (E) ✗ A short-run curve holds the machines fixed, and here they doubled.

Question 21 — Malthus and technology · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

Technology and capital raise the whole production function. Diminishing returns is a statement about one input at a given technology and given other inputs, and it survives: each added farmer on a given farm still adds less than the one before.

  • (A) ✗ Output per person can rise if the production function moves up faster than workers are added.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: diminishing returns apply in the short run, with an input fixed.
  • (D) ✗ Farms still have fixed inputs, such as the acreage of a given farm.
  • (E) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: output per person has risen.

Question 22 — Diminishing marginal returns · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

The seventh worker adds 91 − 84 = 7 bushels and the eighth adds 96 − 91 = 5. Marginal product is falling but positive, so total product keeps rising; it would fall only if marginal product were negative.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A falling marginal product does not make total product fall; it needs to turn negative.
  • (C) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: this farm shows diminishing marginal returns.
  • (D) ✗ Average product is 96 ÷ 8 = 12, above the eighth worker's 5, but that only pulls the average down.
  • (E) ✗ Total product is 96 with 8 workers and 91 with 7.

Question 23 — The slope of the total product curve · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The slope of each segment is the marginal product of the baker added: 12, 20, 28, 32, 23, 17, 15 and

  1. The steepest is the fourth baker's, 32, from 3 to 4 bakers.
  • (A) ✗ The fifth baker's 23 is the first fall from the peak.
  • (B) ✗ The last segment's slope, 13, is among the flattest.
  • (C) ✗ The second baker's 20 is below the third's and fourth's.
  • (D) ✗ The first baker's 12 is the smallest of the first four, not the largest.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 24 — Short run and long run · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The long run is a period in which all inputs can be varied, so the bakery could change its oven as well as its bakers.

  • (A) ✗ No fixed length of time defines it.
  • (B) ✗ Hiring bakers is a short-run adjustment; the oven is still fixed.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ A year is not the dividing line; the lease just happens to run for one.
  • (E) ✗ Output may keep changing in the long run; what changes is which inputs can be varied.

Question 25 — Diminishing returns are not diseconomies of scale · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

Hiring more bakers with the oven fixed varies one input: output rises by less each time, which is diminishing marginal returns. Scale refers to the long run, where every input rises together.

  • (A) ✗ They are different ideas: one input varies, versus every input.
  • (B) ✗ The oven is also an input; it is held fixed.
  • (C) ✗ Hiring more bakers with a fixed oven raises costs per loaf; it does not lower long-run average cost.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: output rising by less each time is diminishing, not increasing.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 15 concept entries appear. The setting (entries 1 to 3) carries four items; total and marginal product, the slope and the plotting convention (entries 4 to 7) five; diminishing and increasing marginal returns (entries 8 and 10) four; average product and its link to marginal product (entries 9 and 11) five; and shifts, cost, scale and Malthus (entries 12 to 15) seven. The weight follows the learning objective, which asks students to calculate the various measures of productivity, and the three flagged entries (9 to 11) carry nine items between them because the book does not teach them.

Bloom levels. Application 11 · Analysis 9 · Synthesis 5, a ratio of 1 : 0.8 : 0.5 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The paper is more numerical than the standard because Topic 3.1 is a table and a rule, and the released papers test it from tables.

Questions 11 to 15 are the ones to discuss aloud. Question 11 asks where diminishing marginal returns set in, which a farm that starts at its peak never asks. Question 14 is the trap in the CED's sample question 7: the option "average product of labor begins to decline" is wrong, and the key is that marginal cost begins to increase. Question 15 is the boundary case, where the marginal product equals the average and the average does not move.

Question 22 is Krugman's MCQ 4 argued from the other side. The textbook keys a total product curve with a positive, decreasing slope; question 22 shows the student who reads diminishing as falling.
