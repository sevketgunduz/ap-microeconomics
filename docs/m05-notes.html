The headline, before anything else. The same eleven pages serve both courses, and the two frameworks ask very different things of them. AP Macroeconomics covers demand in two essential knowledge statements, and Module 5 over-delivers on both. AP Microeconomics covers it in seven (MKT-3.A.1–MKT-3.A.6 and MKT-3.B.1, CED pp. 46–47), and Module 5 delivers only two of them in full. Property rights, incentives and constraints, the income and substitution effects, and diminishing marginal utility are all required in Topic 2.1, and every one of them occurs 0 times in pp. 48–58. None is missing from the book: each has a home in Module 1, 46 or 51. The teaching problem here is the reverse of the Macro one. There it was proportion; here it is assembly. See section 3.

The enduring understanding, quoted verbatim (CED p. 46):

MKT-3 — Individuals and firms respond to incentives and face constraints.

Note what that sentence is about. The Macro enduring understanding for the same chapter (MKT-2, Macro CED p. 37) is about competitive markets determining price. The Micro one is about behaviour — incentives and constraints — and the demand curve is presented as the consequence of that behaviour. That is why the Micro topic carries three statements about property rights and incentives before it reaches the law of demand, and why it asks why the curve slopes down rather than only that it does.

Krugman's four stated objectives for the module (p. 48):

  1. What a competitive market is and how it is described by the supply and demand model
  2. What the demand curve is
  3. The difference between movements along the demand curve and changes in demand
  4. The factors that shift the demand curve

All four are on-syllabus. Objectives 2 and 3 are MKT-3.A; objective 4 is MKT-3.B; objective 1 belongs to Topic 2.6, where MKT-4.A.1 and MKT-4.A.2 name the supply-demand model and the competitive market (CED p. 53).

Verdict on alignment. Two statements are delivered in full and well — MKT-3.A.4 (own price moves you along the curve) and MKT-3.B.1 (determinants shift it). Figure 5.3 is the best single exhibit in Section 2 for them. MKT-3.A.6 is delivered in substance by Figure 5.5 on p. 55, which is the horizontal summation the framework asks for. MKT-3.A.4 — "The law of demand suggests that a change in the own-price causes a change in quantity demanded in the opposite direction and a movement along a demand (marginal benefit) curve." (CED p. 46)

MKT-3.A.6 — "The market demand curve (schedule) is derived from the summation of individual demand curves (schedules)." (CED p. 46)

Learning objective MKT-3.B (CED p. 47):

Explain (using graphs as appropriate) buyers' responses to changes in incentives and constraints.
MKT-3.B.1 — "Changes in the determinants of consumer demand can cause the demand curve to shift." (CED p. 47)

Four things in that wording are worth a teacher's attention, because each one changes what you say in class.

MKT-3.A.4 says "own-price", and the book never does. Krugman's definition of a movement says "a change in that good's price" (p. 51); the phrase own price occurs 0 times in pp. 48–58 and 36 times elsewhere in the book. The framework's word is shorter and sharper. Teach it here, because the related-good trap in section 5 is easiest to state in it.

MKT-3.A.4 also calls the demand curve a "marginal benefit" curve. Marginal benefit occurs 0 times in Module 5. Your class met marginal benefit in Unit 1 (CBA-2.B.1, CED p. 40) and met diminishing marginal utility in Module 51. The parenthesis is the framework joining the two units: the height of the demand curve at any quantity is what the buyer of that unit would pay for it, which is its marginal benefit. It is also the idea consumer surplus needs in Topic 2.6.

MKT-3.B.1 names no determinant. The Macro statement for the same idea names one — income (MKT-2.B.1, Macro CED p. 37). The Micro statement names none, and income appears in the Micro topic only as a constraint, in MKT-3.A.3. Every one of Krugman's five factors is in scope as "the determinants of consumer demand", and none is privileged. A Macro study guide that tells a student "income is the safest determinant to name" is giving advice that has no footing here.

MKT-3.A.5 offers two explanations joined by "and/or". A complete answer to "why does the demand curve slope downward?" can use the income and substitution effects, or diminishing marginal utility, or both. Students should be able to give either; the strongest answers give the substitution effect first, because it is the one that holds for every good.

None of these is absent from the book. Every one is absent from this module, which is the distinction the project's standards ask to be kept. Two things follow.

Three of the five are reviews, not new teaching. Property rights and incentives were taught in Module 1 (flagged forward to Topic 2.1 in the Module 1 notes, glossary tier †). Diminishing marginal utility was taught in Module 51, whose notes end with the sentence that makes the link: marginal analysis "explains more precisely how an increase in price leads to less marginal utility per dollar and therefore a decrease in the quantity demanded" (p. 521). In a class that took Unit 1 in the recommended order, these are ten minutes of retrieval, not a lesson.

The substitution and income effects are the one genuinely new idea, and the book puts them 400 pages later. Module 46 opens with them (pp. 458–459) because Krugman's Section 9 is Behind the Demand Curve. For AP Microeconomics that is too late — Topic 2.1 asks for them on day one of Unit 2. Take pp. 458–459 out of sequence; they need nothing from Modules 6–45. Krugman's margin definitions, quoted:

Substitution effect (p. 458) — "the change in the quantity of that good demanded as the consumer substitutes the good that has become relatively cheaper for the good that has become relatively more expensive."
Income effect (p. 459) — "the change in the quantity of that good demanded that results from a change in the consumer's purchasing power when the price of the good changes."

Two sentences from the same pages are worth reading aloud, because they settle most of what the exam can ask:

  • For most goods "the income effect is not important", and most market demand curves slope downward

"solely because of the substitution effect" (p. 459).

  • When it matters, "the income effect usually reinforces the substitution effect" — for a normal

good. For an inferior good the two work in opposite directions, and the substitution effect is normally the larger (p. 459).

The FYI on Giffen goods (p. 459) is the one case where the income effect wins. It is not in the framework — Giffen occurs 0 times in the Micro CED — and Krugman calls its existence controversial. Mention it only if a student asks what would happen if an inferior good's income effect were huge.

Demand as the marginal benefit curve has to be supplied outright. Nothing in Module 5 says it. One sentence and one example are enough: at 8 units the demand curve's height is $3, so the buyer of the 8th unit values it at $3 — that is its marginal benefit. Then read MKT-3.A.4 aloud, with its parenthesis.

The constraints of MKT-3.A.3 include time and law, not just income. Budget constraint in Module 51 covers income. Time and "legal and regulatory frameworks" are named in the statement and taught nowhere in Krugman as demand constraints. One example each: a commuter who would pay for a gym but has no hour free (time); a 16-year-old who cannot buy a car's insurance in their own name (law).

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

"Demand" and "quantity demanded" are not synonyms, and the exam knows it

This is still the whole of the module's difficulty in either course. Krugman states the rule on p. 52:

"When economists talk about a 'change in demand,' saying 'the demand for X increased' … they mean that the demand curve for X or Y shifted — not that the quantity demanded rose or fell because of a change in the price."

Drill it as a two-question test, in the framework's word:

  1. Did the good's own-price change? If yes, it is a movement along — a change in *quantity

demanded*. Nothing shifts. (MKT-3.A.4)

  1. Did anything else change? If yes, the curve shifts — a change in demand. (MKT-3.B.1)

The price of a related good is not an own-price

"A rise in the price of tea increases the demand for coffee." Students hear price and reach for a movement. Write both goods' names on the board every time: whose price moved, and whose curve are we drawing?

The substitution effect is not the same thing as substitutes

This is the new trap Micro adds, and it is created by the vocabulary. Substitutes (entry on p. 52) are a determinant: another good's price moves and this good's curve shifts. The substitution effect (p. 458) is an explanation of the slope: this good's own price moves and buyers slide along its curve, partly by switching to other goods. Same switching behaviour, opposite side of the movement/shift line. Put the two on the board side by side:

Price that changedWhat happens to coffee's curve
Tea gets dearer; coffee demand risestea'sshifts right — tea is a substitute
Coffee gets dearer; some drinkers switch to teacoffee'smovement up along it — the substitution effect

The income effect is not an income change

The income effect is caused by a change in the good's own price, which changes what the buyer's income will buy. Nobody's pay changed. A change in actual income is a determinant and shifts the curve. Students who confuse the two will draw a shift for a price change — the own-price trap in a new costume.

For an inferior good, the income effect runs backwards — and the curve still slopes down

Price up → real income down → more of an inferior good demanded. That opposes the substitution effect. The law of demand survives because the substitution effect is normally the larger (Krugman p. 459). Students who learn "income effect reinforces the substitution effect" as a rule will get Module 46 FRQ 2 wrong.

Expectations run backwards from intuition

Expecting a fall means waiting, which decreases demand today. Expecting a rise means stocking up. Krugman's Starbucks example (p. 54). MCQ 4 tests exactly this.

An inferior good is not a bad good

Bus rides against taxi rides, fast food against casual dining (p. 53). Inferior describes the income relationship, not quality.

Say "all other things being equal" every single time

Coffee was dearer in 2006 than 2002 and consumption was higher (p. 50). Pose it as a puzzle before giving the answer.

Do not import the Macro vocabulary

The Macro student guide for this module mentions recessions in three places and tells students that income is "the only determinant your framework names". Both are wrong for this course: recession occurs 0 times in the Micro CED, and MKT-3.B.1 names no determinant at all. The Micro student guide says when incomes fall and treats the five determinants equally.

Note the row Normal and inferior goods → Income effect. It forces the sequence in period 2: the income effect cannot be finished until the class has met the income determinant in period 1.

Teach as a pair, never singly. Each of these is defined against the other:

  • Demand / quantity demanded — the curve against a point on it. This is the module
  • Movement along / shift of — own-price against everything else
  • Substitutes / substitution effect — a determinant against an explanation of the slope; the pair Micro adds
  • Income change / income effect — actual income against purchasing power changed by a price
  • Substitution effect / income effect — always present against sometimes significant
  • Substitutes / complements — a price rise in the other good pushes demand the opposite way
  • Normal / inferior goods — the same income rise, opposite shifts
  • Individual / market demand curve — one consumer against the horizontal sum of all of them
