The headline. Module 46 does two jobs, and only one of them belongs to the topic it is filed under. It closes Topic 2.1: MKT-3.A.5 says downward-sloping demand is "explained by the income effect and substitution effect", and this is the only place in the book where those two terms are defined — the Module 5 guide had to supply them from here. It then opens Topic 2.3 with the definition of elasticity and one way to calculate it. It opens Topic 2.3 with a method the framework does not use. The midpoint method is the organising technique of the second half of this module, and midpoint occurs 0 times in the Micro CED and 0 times across all nine College Board papers in this project — against 37 hits for elasticity and 62 for total revenue on those same papers. MKT-3.E.2 defines price elasticity of demand as "the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price", full stop. Krugman's own AP Review then demands the midpoint method in four of its nine items. Teach both, and be explicit about which is which: that is section 5. Everything else Topic 2.3 requires — the elastic/inelastic/unit-elastic benchmark, the determinants, total revenue, and the fact that elasticity changes along a linear demand curve — is in Module 47. Modules 46 and 47 are one topic cut in half, and should be taught as a pair.

The two essential knowledge statements this module exists to deliver, verbatim:

MKT-3.A.5 (CED p. 46) — "The conceptual relationship between price and quantity stated by the law of demand leads to downward-sloping demand curves explained by the income effect and substitution effect and/or by diminishing marginal utility."
MKT-3.E.1 (CED p. 49) — "Economists use the concept of elasticity to measure the magnitude of percentage changes in quantity owing to any given changes in the own-price, income, and prices of related goods."
MKT-3.E.2 (CED p. 49) — "Price elasticity of demand is measured by the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price or the responsiveness of the quantity demanded to changes in price. Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve, meaning slope is not elasticity."

Only the first sentence of MKT-3.E.2 is in this module. The second sentence is Module 47's Figure 47.4 — see section 3.

Krugman's four objectives (p. 458):

  1. How the income and substitution effects explain the law of demand
  2. The definition of elasticity, a measure of responsiveness to changes in prices or incomes
  3. The importance of the price elasticity of demand
  4. How to calculate the price elasticity of demand

Objective 1 is MKT-3.A.5; objectives 2 and 3 are MKT-3.E.1; objective 4 is MKT-3.E.2(c).

The margin definitions, in Krugman's own words

Substitution effect (p. 458) — "The substitution effect of a change in the price of a good is the change in the quantity of that good demanded as the consumer substitutes the good that has become relatively cheaper for the good that has become relatively more expensive."
Income effect (p. 459) — "The income effect of a change in the price of a good is the change in the quantity of that good demanded that results from a change in the consumer's purchasing power when the price of the good changes."
Price elasticity of demand (p. 460) — "The price elasticity of demand is the ratio of the percent change in the quantity demanded to the percent change in the price as we move along the demand curve (dropping the minus sign)."
Midpoint method (p. 462) — "The midpoint method is a technique for calculating the percent change. In this approach, we calculate changes in a variable compared with the average, or midpoint, of the initial and final values."

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

The module uses two different methods and does not say so

Equations 46-1 and 46-2 compute percent change against the initial value. Equation 46-4 computes it against the average. Both appear in the AP Review: CYU 2 and 3 and FRQ 1 want the midpoint method, CYU 4 explicitly wants the initial-value method, and MCQ 5 hands over finished percentages. A student who does not notice will get CYU 4 wrong by using midpoint.

Say it plainly: the elasticity is the ratio; the method is how you get the two percentages. On Figure 46.1's own numbers the initial-value method gives 0.200 and the midpoint method gives 0.206 — near enough that the difference looks like nothing, which is exactly why the gasoline example on p. 462 (200% higher one way, 66.7% lower the other) is the right one to teach from.

Which method the exam expects

The framework never names the midpoint method. MKT-3.E.2 and MKT-3.E.6 both say "percentage change in quantity … divided by the percentage change in price", and the CED's own sample items hand the student either finished percentages (sample MCQ 6, Exam Information p. 128: "a 2 percent increase … causes a 5 percent decrease") or a finished elasticity (sample FRQ 1(c), p. 133: "If the absolute value of the price elasticity of demand is 0.92 …"). Neither requires a technique at all.

So: know the midpoint method, because the textbook's questions require it and because it is the honest way to compute elasticity over a large price change; expect the exam to give you the percentages. If a released-style item ever does give two points and ask for a number, either method earns the point — but say which one you used.

"Dropping the minus sign" is the absolute value

Krugman's phrase is informal. The College Board writes absolute value (Exam Information pp. 133 and 137), and MKT-3.E.3 speaks of "the magnitude of the value of elasticity". Students should report the magnitude — writing the elasticity between absolute-value bars, or simply saying "in absolute value" — and should still be able to say why the raw number is negative: the law of demand.

A small income effect is not the same as no income effect

Krugman says that for most goods the income effect "is not important and has no significant effect" and that most demand curves slope downward "solely because of the substitution effect — end of story." That is a statement about size, not existence. MKT-3.A.5 names both effects, and an exam answer that says the income effect does not exist is wrong.

The income effect does not mean income changed

The commonest error in this module. Nobody's pay changed; the price changed, and that changed what the same pay will buy. Keep the phrase purchasing power in front of students, and keep Krugman's own contrast — real income against money income — on the board.

A Giffen good is a curiosity, not a counter-example

Krugman's own fyi box says the story "has never been validated in any real situation, nineteenth- century Ireland included". Two sentences, then move on — but note that Krugman's own solution to CYU 1(c) calls cafeteria meals a Giffen good, so a student who reads the solutions will meet the term again. The law of demand has no exception in this course.

An inferior good is not a bad good

Inferior is about what happens to demand when income rises, nothing else. Bus travel, supermarket own-brands and instant noodles are the standard examples; none of them is low quality by definition.

7. Suggested allocation — one and a half periods

Period 1 — why the demand curve slopes downward

  1. Recall from Module 5: the law of demand was asserted, not explained (10 min).
  2. The substitution effect, with the opportunity-cost framing from p. 458 — one good gets relatively

cheaper, so you give up less of the other to buy it (10 min).

  1. The income effect, through the rental-housing example: real income against money income (10 min).
  2. Normal and inferior goods, and the two directions: reinforcing, then opposing (10 min).
  3. CYU 1 (a–c) in pairs — the whole lesson is in those three cases (10 min).

Half of period 2 — elasticity as a number

  1. Why "the quantity falls" is not an answer a firm can use: the flu-vaccine question (5 min).
  2. Elasticity as the ratio of two percentage changes; Figure 46.1 worked on the board to 0.2 (10 min).
  3. Absolute value, and why the raw number is negative (5 min).
  4. The midpoint method, motivated by the gasoline example, then MCQ 3 (10 min).
  5. The method conversation from section 5, then set FRQ 1 (10 min).

Then go straight into Module 47. The two modules are one topic.
