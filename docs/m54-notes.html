The headline. Module 54 is well aligned on what it teaches, and short on one measure. Topic 3.1 has three essential knowledge statements. The module delivers the production function, fixed and variable inputs, the short run, total product, marginal product and diminishing marginal returns (PRD-1.A.1, PRD-1.A.3) with a table, two curves and a calculation, on one worked farm. Its two weak points are both in PRD-1.A.2: "Marginal product and average product change as input usage changes, and hence, total product changes." Average product is missing. Average product occurs 0 times in the text of pp. 542–547 and once in the whole book — as a wrong answer to the module's own MCQ 3 (p. 547). The framework names it three times in Topic 3.1 and its Sample Instructional Activity (CED pp. 61–62), uses it in sample multiple-choice question 7 (CED p. 129, key D), and it occurs 13 times on the eight released papers, 11 of them as average product of labor. The rule that connects it to marginal product — average product rises while marginal product is above it, falls while marginal product is below it — is stated nowhere in the book for product; Krugman teaches it only for cost, in Module 55 (pp. 555–556). The guide's entries 9 and 11 supply both. The rising phase is missing too. Krugman's marginal product falls from the first worker, so a student never sees diminishing returns set in. Increasing marginal returns occurs 0 times in all 927 pages, 0 times in the CED and 0 times on the released papers; the book meets the idea only as increasing returns from specialization on p. 556 (Module 55). Sample question 7 asks what happens "when diminishing returns set in", which presupposes a phase before. Entry 10 supplies it.

The three essential knowledge statements this topic carries, verbatim (CED p. 62):

PRD-1.A.1 — "The production function explains the relationship between inputs and outputs both in the short run and the long run." PRD-1.A.2 — "Marginal product and average product change as input usage changes, and hence, total product changes." PRD-1.A.3 — "Diminishing marginal returns occur as the firm employs more of one input, holding other inputs constant, to produce a product (output) in the short run."

The margin definitions, in Krugman's own words

Production function (p. 542) — "A production function is the relationship between the quantity of inputs a firm uses and the quantity of output it produces."
Fixed input (p. 542) — "A fixed input is an input whose quantity is fixed for a period of time and cannot be varied."
Variable input (p. 542) — "A variable input is an input whose quantity the firm can vary at any time."
Long run (p. 542) — "The long run is the time period in which all inputs can be varied."
Short run (p. 542) — "The short run is the time period in which at least one input is fixed."
Total product curve (p. 543) — "The total product curve shows how the quantity of output depends on the quantity of the variable input, for a given quantity of the fixed input."
Marginal product (p. 543) — "The marginal product of an input is the additional quantity of output produced by using one more unit of that input."
Diminishing returns (p. 545) — "There are diminishing returns to an input when an increase in the quantity of that input, holding the levels of all other inputs fixed, leads to a decline in the marginal product of that input."

Two points about the wording. The framework's phrase is diminishing marginal returns — diminishing marginal returns occurs 1 time in the module (p. 545) and 2 times in the book, against 7 times in the CED and 6 on the released papers; Krugman's diminishing returns (21 times in the module, 60 in the book) is the same idea. And Krugman never writes law of diminishing returns (0 times in the book and in the CED; once on the papers, in 2012): treat it as a synonym if students bring it from another text.

FRQ 1's rubric states the shape correctly: the slope of the total product curve is "positive and decreasing", and the explanation is that "each additional unit of labor increases total product by less than the previous unit of labor". Read it with students; it is the model sentence for every diminishing-returns question.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Diminishing returns do not make output fall

Students hear diminishing and draw a total product curve that turns down. Total product keeps rising while marginal product is positive; diminishing returns means it rises by less each time. Krugman's MCQ 4 keys this correctly, and sample question 7's option (C), "Total product begins to decline", is the same trap. Total product falls only if marginal product turns negative, which Krugman's farm never reaches (the 8th worker still adds 5 bushels). The guide's entry 8 says so.

Diminishing returns set in — they are not there from the first worker

Krugman's farm starts at its highest marginal product and falls from there, so students conclude that marginal product always falls. In many firms the first few workers each add more than the last, because they can divide the tasks (Krugman's p. 556); diminishing returns begin at the peak of marginal product. The guide's second firm, a bakery with one oven, rises from 12 to 32 loaves and then falls — diminishing returns set in with the fifth baker. Test question 11 asks where.

Average product does not start to fall when diminishing returns set in

This is the option sample question 7 tests (B, "Average product of labor begins to decline"), and the reason the question is a good one. In the bakery, marginal product peaks at 32 with the fourth baker and begins to fall with the fifth, but average product keeps rising until the fifth baker's 23 equals the average of 23, and falls only from the sixth. Marginal product must fall to the average before it can drag it down. The key is (D), marginal cost begins to increase — the cost mirror of the falling marginal product.

The short run is a condition, not a length of time

Krugman's own definition is right (p. 542, "at least one input is fixed"), and his MCQ 2 offers "less than a year" as a distractor. For a pizza shop, building a second oven may take months; for a farm, buying more land may take years; for an online seller, signing up another supplier takes a day. What defines the short run is that some input cannot be changed, not how many calendar days have passed.

Which input is "fixed" depends on the question

Krugman's fixed input is land; the exam's usual fixed input is capital ("assume labor is the variable input and capital is the fixed input", FRQ 1's rubric, p. 547), and sample question 7 says "labor is the only variable input". Teach the pattern, not the noun: one input is held constant, one is varied, and everything is measured against the varied one.

Diminishing returns and the limits that were lifted

MCQ 5 (p. 547) keys "investment in capital" for what alleviated the limits of diminishing returns, while the FYI (p. 546) credits technological progress and says diminishing returns "implies that the marginal product declines when all other things — including technology — remain the same". Both are consistent: more capital or better technology moves the whole production function up, and the curve at the new level still falls. Malthus was wrong about the whole function's position, not about the shape. The guide's entry 15 says this.

Do not confuse it with diminishing marginal utility

Module 51 teaches diminishing marginal utility, a statement about a consumer's satisfaction. Diminishing returns is a statement about a firm's output when one input is held fixed. The words overlap and the topics do not; Topic 3.1 never needs utility.

7. Suggested allocation — two periods

Krugman places the profit pair (Modules 52–53) before the production and cost modules; the framework places Topics 3.4–3.5 after Topics 3.1–3.3. Either order works for this module, because it uses no profit and no cost. In the framework's order this is the unit's opening lesson; in Krugman's it is a restart after two profit lessons.
