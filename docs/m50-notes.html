The headline. Module 50 is the pay-off of the whole unit. It adds consumer and producer surplus together, proves that the competitive equilibrium maximises the total, and then shows what a tax does to it: a wedge, a revenue rectangle, and a deadweight-loss triangle. MKT-4.A.5, POL-1.A.5 and POL-1.A.6 are all delivered properly, with figures. And then it leaves out half of its own topic. POL-1.A.2 says "Governments use taxes and subsidies to change incentives", and POL-1.A.3 says "Taxes and subsidies affect government revenues or costs." The word subsid occurs 0 times in pp. 495–510 — and 59 times across the nine released College Board papers, against 14 in the CED. This is the largest single gap in Unit 2: the exam tests subsidies heavily, and the textbook's one module on market intervention never mentions them. Two more framework words are missing. Allocative occurs 0 times in all 927 pages, while POL-1.A.4 turns on it. And Krugman's organising term is excise tax — which occurs 0 times in the Micro CED and twice across the released papers, where the phrase is per-unit tax, 21 times. Teach the idea under both names.

The essential knowledge statements this module delivers, verbatim:

MKT-4.A.5 (CED p. 53) — "Market equilibrium maximizes total economic surplus in the absence of market failures, meaning that perfectly competitive markets are efficient."
POL-1.A.5 (CED p. 55) — "Deadweight loss represents the losses to buyers and sellers as a result of government intervention in an efficient market."
POL-1.A.6 (CED p. 55) — "The incidence of taxes and subsidies imposed on goods traded in perfectly competitive markets depends on the elasticity of supply and demand."

And the two it does not:

POL-1.A.2 (CED p. 55) — "Governments use taxes and subsidies to change incentives in ways that influence consumer and producer behavior, shifting the supply and demand curves accordingly."
POL-1.A.3 (CED p. 55) — "Taxes and subsidies affect government revenues or costs."

POL-1.A.6 says "taxes and subsidies" too. Every incidence argument in this module is about a tax; the identical argument about a subsidy has to be supplied.

Krugman's two objectives (p. 495):

  1. The meaning and importance of total surplus and how it can be used to illustrate efficiency in

markets

  1. How taxes affect total surplus and can create deadweight loss

Objective 1 is MKT-4.A.5; objective 2 is POL-1.A.5. Neither mentions subsidies, which is the honest explanation of the gap: the module does exactly what it says it will.

The margin definitions, in Krugman's own words

Total surplus (p. 495) — "Total surplus is the total net gain to consumers and producers from trading in a market. It is the sum of producer and consumer surplus."
Excise tax (p. 499) — "An excise tax is a tax on sales of a good or service."
Deadweight loss (p. 506) — "The deadweight loss (from a tax) is the decrease in total surplus resulting from the tax, minus the tax revenues generated."
Administrative costs (p. 508) — "The administrative costs of a tax are the resources used by government to collect the tax, and by taxpayers to pay (or to evade) it, over and above the amount collected."

The efficiency argument, in three steps

Krugman proves MKT-4.A.5 with three thought experiments (Figures 50.2–50.4), and they are worth teaching as a numbered list because students remember the conclusion without the argument:

  1. Reallocate consumption — move a unit from a buyer with a high willingness to pay to one with a

lower one, and total surplus falls.

  1. Reallocate sales — move a sale from a low-cost seller to a high-cost one, and total surplus

falls.

  1. Change the quantity — trade one unit more or one unit fewer than the equilibrium quantity, and

total surplus falls.

So the market equilibrium has the right buyers, the right sellers and the right quantity. Krugman's own review question (MCQ 2) asks precisely this, and the answer is "none of the above".

The Potterville numbers are better than the book makes them look. Krugman computes the revenue ($200,000) and never computes the surpluses. On his own figures the pre-tax total surplus is $400,000, the post-tax total is $100,000, the revenue is $200,000, and the deadweight loss is therefore $100,000 — consumers and producers each losing $150,000, of which $100,000 each goes to the government and $50,000 each simply disappears. Work it on the board; it turns a diagram into an account that balances.

The subsidy gap is the one to take seriously. Across the nine College Board papers in this project, subsid occurs 59 times — more often than deadweight (30) and as often as consumer surplus (58). A student who has worked only from Krugman will have seen a per-unit subsidy diagram never. The economics is entirely symmetric with the tax case and takes one lesson:

  • A per-unit subsidy to sellers shifts supply down by the subsidy.
  • The price buyers pay falls; the price sellers receive (including the subsidy) rises; the gap

between them is the subsidy.

  • Quantity rises above the efficient level, so there is again a deadweight loss.
  • The government has a cost, not a revenue: subsidy × quantity.
  • The incidence rule is the same: the more inelastic side captures more of the benefit.

That last line is POL-1.A.6 word for word — it says "taxes and subsidies".

The allocative efficiency gap is a vocabulary problem with a long reach. POL-1.A.4 says intervention in an efficient market "can only decrease allocative efficiency", and the word appears nowhere in Krugman. The idea is exactly what Figures 50.2–50.4 prove. Supply the term here, and note that Modules 8 and 9 needed it too.

One reversal, in the textbook's favour. Krugman's excise tax is not the framework's phrase — excise occurs 0 times in the Micro CED and twice across the released papers, where the standard wording is per-unit tax (21 occurrences). Use both names from the first lesson.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

Who hands over the money does not decide who bears the tax

The single most important idea in the module, and the most counter-intuitive. In Figures 50.6 and 50.7 the tax is levied on hotel owners in one and on guests in the other, and both give the same answer: guests pay $100, owners keep $60, 5,000 rooms. Krugman's phrase is worth quoting: the incidence "doesn't depend on who officially pays the tax."

The supply curve shifts up by the amount of the tax, not to a new random position

A $40 tax shifts supply up $40 vertically, because sellers need $40 more per unit to supply the same quantity. Measure the shift vertically, never horizontally — and note that the equilibrium price rises by less than $40, which is the whole point.

Deadweight loss is not the tax revenue

Revenue is a transfer: the money leaves buyers and sellers and arrives at the government. The deadweight loss is the part that goes nowhere — the value of trades that no longer happen. Krugman's definition makes this explicit: the decrease in total surplus minus the revenue generated.

A tax that changes no quantity causes no deadweight loss

Krugman states this clearly on p. 507, and it is the cleanest way to see what the triangle measures. If the quantity traded does not fall, the triangle has no height and the tax is a pure transfer. That is why perfectly inelastic supply or demand produces a deadweight loss of zero — and it connects this module straight back to Module 48's extreme cases.

A bigger tax does not always mean more revenue

At Potterville's numbers a $40 tax raises $200,000 and a $60 tax raises only $150,000, because the quantity falls faster than the rate rises. Meanwhile the deadweight loss grows from $100,000 to $225,000. Students find this genuinely surprising, and it is the right preparation for Unit 6.

"Efficient" does not mean "fair"

Krugman's equity-and-efficiency section is short and the distinction is examinable in substance. A competitive equilibrium maximises total surplus; it says nothing about how that surplus is shared, and a policy that improves fairness often costs efficiency. Keep MCQ 3 for this.

Subsidies are not negative taxes in every respect

The geometry is mirrored — supply shifts down, a wedge opens, a triangle of deadweight loss appears — but two things are not mirrored. The government has a cost rather than a revenue, and the quantity ends up above the efficient level rather than below it. Both matter on an exam.

  1. Excise tax, also called a per-unit tax; Potterville at $80 and 10,000 rooms (5 min).
  2. A $40 tax on sellers: supply up $40, price to $100, quantity to 5,000, sellers keep $60 (15 min).
  3. The same tax on buyers: demand down $40, and the same answer (10 min).

  2. Subsidies, as the mirror image: supply down, buyers pay less, sellers receive more, quantity

above the efficient level, a government cost, and a deadweight loss (15 min).

  1. Set FRQ 1 and CYU 3 (5 min).
