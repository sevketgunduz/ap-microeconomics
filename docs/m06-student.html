Supply and Equilibrium

Nineteen terms: the eighteen in Module 6 of Krugman’s Economics for AP, plus one your course requires that the module leaves out. It covers two ideas — supply, and what happens when supply and demand are put on the same diagram — and one calculation you will use all through this unit.

Textbook pages 59–70·19 terms· Topics 2.2, 2.6 and 2.7·2 periods

How to use this guide

Read Module 6 in your textbook first, then work through the entries here in order. Entries 1 to 13 are supply; entries 14 to 19 put supply and demand together.

  • ★ Exam means the AP Microeconomics framework can test it. Every entry in this guide carries it.
  • ★ ⚑ means the framework can test it and Module 6 of your textbook does not teach it. One entry carries the flag. Learn it from here.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.
  • The calculator in the middle is the one to spend time on. You will be asked to calculate surpluses and shortages, and the only way to get that wrong is to subtract in the wrong order.

Good news, if you did Module 5 properly. Supply is demand with the slope reversed. The law runs the other way, the curve slopes up instead of down, and the shift factors are different — but own price means a movement, anything else means a shift is exactly the same rule, and it is still the rule that decides most of the marks.

⚑ One entry is not in Module 6. Your course says the equilibrium price carries information that guides how resources are used. Your textbook shows how the price gets to equilibrium but never says what the price tells people once it is there. Entry 19 fills the gap — and you have met the idea before, in Module 1.

The 19 terms

01

Quantity supplied

★ Exam
Definition

The actual amount of a good or service producers are willing to sell at some specific price.

Textbook page 59.

Word for word the mirror of quantity demanded, and it carries the same warning. Quantity supplied is a number attached to one price. It is one point on the curve, one row of the table.

Supply is the whole relationship — the entire curve. If a question says the quantity supplied rose, you have moved to a different point on the same curve.

Watch out. Everything you learned about not confusing demand with quantity demanded applies again, unchanged. The exam will test it twice: once on the demand side and once here.

Textbook

One row. At $1.00 a pound, quantity supplied is 10.0 billion pounds a year. At $1.25 it is 10.7 billion.

Numeric

Point E. At the equilibrium price of $1.00, quantity supplied is 10.0 billion pounds — and so is quantity demanded. That is what makes it the equilibrium.

Illustrative

Willing to sell. Brazilian farmers will plant coffee on their best land at a low price and on their worse land only if the price is high. Each price gives a different quantity supplied.

How it connects. The law of supply (entry 4) says how this number responds to price. A movement along the supply curve (entry 5) is a change in it.

Check yourself

1.1 Is “11.2 billion pounds” a supply or a quantity supplied?

Show answer

A quantity supplied, and it only means something once you say which price goes with it — here, $1.50. The supply is the whole schedule.

1.2 Which two words in the definition do the work?

Show answer

“Specific price.” Without a stated price the number is meaningless, which is exactly why it is a point rather than a curve.

02

Supply schedule

★ Exam
Definition

A table showing how much of a good producers will supply at different prices.

Textbook page 59, the right-hand side of Figure 6.1.

Same tool as the demand schedule, same order of work: the table comes first and the curve is drawn from it. Your framework names it: the market supply curve (schedule) is built by adding up individual supply curves (schedules), so expect tables as well as graphs.

Watch out. Read the direction before you label a table. A demand schedule and a supply schedule look identical on the page; the only difference is whether quantity rises or falls as price rises.

Textbook

Coffee beans. $0.50 → 8.0 billion pounds; $1.00 → 10.0; $2.00 → 11.6. Price up, quantity up.

Numeric

Spot the difference. The demand schedule at $2.00 gives 7.1 billion; the supply schedule gives 11.6. Same price, two different questions.

Illustrative

Two columns, one diagram. Put the demand and supply schedules side by side and the equilibrium is the row where the two numbers match.

How it connects. The supply curve (entry 3) is this table drawn, and comparing it with the demand schedule is how you find the equilibrium price (entry 15) without a graph.

Check yourself

2.1 A table shows $2 → 40 units and $4 → 55 units. Demand or supply?

Show answer

Supply. Quantity rises as price rises, which is the law of supply. A demand schedule would run the other way.

2.2 How would you find the equilibrium price from two schedules with no graph?

Show answer

Find the price where quantity demanded and quantity supplied are the same number. In the coffee table that is $1.00, where both are 10.0 billion pounds.

03

Supply curve

★ Exam
Definition

Shows the relationship between quantity supplied and price. It slopes upward.

Textbook page 59, Figure 6.1.

The drawing rules are the ones you already know, with one change: the curve goes up instead of down, and it is labelled S.

  • Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal.
  • Label both axes.
  • Label the curve S, or S₁ if there will be a second one.

When both curves are on one diagram, label both. An unlabelled pair costs marks even when the intersection is in exactly the right place.

Watch out. Drawing the supply curve downward-sloping by accident is the single most common slip in a hurried answer, because you have just spent a week drawing demand curves. Check the direction before you write anything else.

Textbook

Figure 6.1. Seven points from the schedule, joined, sloping up from $0.50/8.0 to $2.00/11.6.

Numeric

Plotting one point. The row $1.50 / 11.2 becomes the point 11.2 across, $1.50 up — which is the right-hand end of the surplus in the diagram above.

Illustrative

Why upward. Brazilian farmers use their best coffee land first. Getting more beans means using worse land, which is only worth doing at a higher price.

How it connects. It is the supply schedule (entry 2) drawn, its slope is the law of supply (entry 4), and crossing it with the demand curve gives equilibrium (entry 14).

Check yourself

3.1 Which way does a supply curve slope, and why?

Show answer

Upward. A higher price makes producers willing to sell more, so price and quantity supplied move together.

3.2 You draw both curves on one diagram and label neither. What have you lost?

Show answer

Marks for labelling, which are awarded separately from the economics — and possibly the reader’s ability to tell which curve you meant to move later in the question.

04

Law of supply

★ Exam
Definition

Other things being equal, the price and quantity supplied of a good are positively related.

Textbook page 60.

Your framework states it this way: a change in own-price causes a change in quantity supplied in the same direction and a movement along the supply curve. Use those words: own-price, same direction, movement along.

Note the same escape clause as the law of demand: other things being equal. If you see the price of something rise while the quantity sold falls, the law has not broken — something else moved.

Watch out. “Positive relationship” does not mean “good”. It means the two variables move in the same direction. For demand your framework says in the opposite direction; for supply, in the same direction. Learn them as a pair.

Textbook

Brazilian land. Whether farmers restrict coffee to ideal locations or expand onto less suitable land depends on the price they expect. Higher price, more land planted.

Numeric

The schedule proves it. Price from $1.00 to $1.25 and quantity supplied from 10.0 to 10.7 billion pounds. Both up.

Illustrative

New entrants. Vietnam and Madagascar can grow coffee. Whether they do depends on the price — which is the law of supply working through who enters at all.

How it connects. It is why the supply curve (entry 3) slopes upward, and it is what a movement along the curve (entry 5) illustrates. It is also how a rising price signals producers to supply more (entry 19). It is a statement about quantity supplied (entry 1), never about supply.

Check yourself

4.1 State the law of supply in your framework’s own words.

Show answer

A change in own-price causes a change in quantity supplied in the same direction — a movement along an upward-sloping supply curve. Say “quantity supplied”, not “supply”.

4.2 Which phrase does your framework use for demand where it says “in the same direction” for supply?

Show answer

“In the opposite direction.” The two statements are otherwise the same sentence with the direction swapped.

05

Movement along the supply curve

★ Exam
Definition

A change in the quantity supplied of a good that is the result of a change in that good’s own price.

Textbook page 60, and Figure 6.3 on page 62 — the arrow from A to B.

The curve does not move. The price changed, and the curve already told you what quantity would be supplied at that price.

One cause, and only one: the good’s own price. Everything else in this module shifts the curve instead.

Watch out. On the supply side the own-price trap has a second form. A rise in the price of an input is not a rise in the price of the good, so it shifts the curve. A rise in the price of the good itself moves you along it. Ask which one the question named.

Textbook

A to B. The price of coffee rises from $1.00 to $1.50 and quantity supplied rises from 10.0 to 11.2 billion pounds. Same curve, higher point.

Numeric

Read it off one column. Both numbers are in the supply column of the table above. A movement never needs a new column.

Illustrative

A housing boom. House prices rise, so more homeowners put their houses up for sale. The price of the thing being sold went up: movement along.

How it connects. It is the law of supply (entry 4) in action, it changes quantity supplied (entry 1), and it is defined against a change in supply (entry 6). A rise in an input price (entry 7) is not an own price.

Check yourself

5.1 During a property boom, more homeowners put their houses on the market. Movement or shift?

Show answer

Movement along the supply curve for houses. The price of houses — the good itself — is what rose.

5.2 Construction workers move to a hurricane-hit area because wages there are higher. Movement or shift, and in which market?

Show answer

A movement along the supply curve of construction labour in that area. The good on the horizontal axis is labour, and its price — the wage — is what rose.

06

Change in supply — a shift

★ Exam
Definition

A shift of the supply curve, which changes the quantity supplied at any given price.

Textbook pages 60–62, Figures 6.2 and 6.3 — the arrow from A to C.

Same four-word test as demand: at any given price. If more would be supplied at $2.00 and at $1.50 and at $1.00, the curve has moved.

Directions: an increase in supply is a shift to the right; a decrease in supply is a shift to the left. Note what that means on an upward-sloping curve — a rightward shift looks like the curve moving down. Say right and left and you can never be misread.

Watch out. “Right” means the same thing for both curves, but it looks different. An increase in demand shifts the curve up-and-right; an increase in supply shifts it down-and-right. Both are increases. Both are rightward.

Textbook

Vietnam enters. At $1.00 the quantity supplied goes from 10.0 to 12.0 billion pounds; at $1.50, from 11.2 to 13.4. Every row rose, so the curve moved right.

Numeric

A to C. Hold the price at $1.00 and quantity supplied goes from 10.0 billion on S₁ to 12.0 billion on S₂. The price never changed, so this cannot be a movement.

Illustrative

Farm vets. Vets moved into pet practice, so fewer offered farm services at any given price. The supply curve for farm veterinary services shifted left.

How it connects. It is the opposite case to a movement along (entry 5), it is caused by entries 7 to 11 — input prices, related goods in production, technology, expectations and the number of producers — and it moves the market supply curve (entry 13).

Check yourself

6.1 Strawberry farmers open roadside stands during harvest season, even though prices are low then. Movement or shift?

Show answer

A shift — an increase in supply. More is offered despite the low price, which means more at any given price. The season changed, not the price.

6.2 Why is “the supply curve shifted down” risky?

Show answer

Because for an upward-sloping curve, down and right are the same movement and it is an increase in supply — which sounds like the opposite. Use right and left.

07

Input prices

★ Exam
Definition

An input is anything used to produce a good or service. A rise in an input’s price makes production more costly, so supply decreases — the curve shifts left.

Textbook page 62. This is the first of the five supply shift factors.

Input prices are the determinant you will meet most often, because every good has inputs. But your framework does not single any determinant out — it says only that changes in the determinants of supply can cause the supply curve to shift — so know all five equally.

The direction is worth saying slowly, because it is the reverse of what students expect: input price up means supply down, curve left.

Watch out. An input price is not the good’s own price. Wages are an input price for a restaurant, so higher wages shift the restaurant’s supply curve left. But in the market for labour, the wage is the price on the axis, so a higher wage is a movement along. Always ask what is on the horizontal axis.

Textbook

Newsprint, 1994–95. Newsprint prices rose sharply, the supply of newspapers fell, several papers closed and new ventures were cancelled.

Numeric

Multiple-choice 1. “The wages of workers producing good X increase” is the answer to which of the following will decrease the supply of X. An input price rise, so a leftward shift.

Illustrative

Vanilla ice cream. Vanilla beans, cream and sugar are the inputs. If vanilla doubles in price, less ice cream is supplied at every price.

How it connects. It causes a change in supply (entry 6), never a movement (entry 5), and it is the supply-side counterpart of consumer income on the demand side. Technology (entry 9) works through the same channel, the cost of production.

Check yourself

7.1 Fast-food chains must raise wages to attract staff. What happens to the supply curve for fast food?

Show answer

It shifts left. Wages are an input price for fast food, and a higher input price decreases supply at every price.

7.2 The price of cream rises. Is that a determinant of the supply of ice cream, and which way does the curve move?

Show answer

Yes — cream is an input, and a dearer input raises the cost of producing ice cream at every price. Supply decreases; the curve shifts left.

08

Substitutes and complements in production

★ Exam
Definition

Substitutes in production are goods a producer can make instead of each other. Complements in production come out together.

Textbook page 63. This is shift factor 2: prices of related goods or services.

This is the hardest idea in the module, and it is hard for one reason: you have just spent a week on substitutes and complements in consumption, and these are different.

Substitutestea and coffeepetrol and heating oil, from one barrel of crude

Watch out. Read the question for who is switching. If a buyer chooses between the two goods, it is consumption and the demand curve moves. If a producer chooses which to make, it is production and the supply curve moves. Your textbook’s multiple-choice 2 is built entirely on this and most students answer it with the consumption case.

Textbook

The oil refinery. It makes petrol and heating oil from the same crude. If heating oil gets dearer, it makes more heating oil and less petrol — so the supply curve for petrol shifts left. Substitutes in production.

Numeric

Oil and natural gas. Gas comes up as a by-product of oil wells. A higher gas price means more wells drilled and therefore more oil supplied. Complements in production.

Illustrative

Vets. Farm practice and pet practice are substitutes in production for a veterinarian. Pet work paid better, so the supply of farm vets shifted left.

How it connects. It is a change in supply (entry 6) caused by another good’s price, and it is the exact counterpart of substitutes and complements in consumption from Module 5.

Check yourself

8.1 An increase in the demand for steak raises the price of steak. What happens to the supply of leather?

Show answer

It increases — the supply curve for leather shifts right. Beef and hides come off the same animal, so leather is a complement in production for steak. This is your textbook’s multiple-choice 2.

8.2 Chicken is a substitute for steak for a shopper. Does a higher steak price shift the supply curve for chicken?

Show answer

No. That is a substitute in consumption, so it shifts the demand curve for chicken to the right. Supply is untouched.

09

Technology

★ Exam
Definition

All the methods people can use to turn inputs into useful goods and services. Better technology lowers the cost of production, so supply increases — the curve shifts right.

Textbook page 63. Shift factor 3.

Note how wide the definition is. Technology here does not mean computers; it means any method of turning inputs into output, including a better strain of seed or a faster way of sorting beans.

Your framework names this determinant: it asks you to explain how producers respond to changes in incentives and technology.

Technology essentially only moves one way in these questions. Methods improve; they rarely get worse. So a technology question is almost always a rightward shift.

Watch out. A technological advance increases supply, not demand, and it shifts the curve rather than moving along it. Your textbook’s multiple-choice 3 offers “a movement along the supply curve” as a distractor precisely because that is the mistake.

Textbook

Disease-resistant corn. An improved strain makes farmers willing to supply more corn at any given price.

Numeric

Sorting beans. A Brazilian farmer sorts coffee by tossing beans in the air. Better sorting equipment means more beans handled in less time, and the curve shifts right.

Illustrative

Bigger cruise ships. New technology made larger ships possible, which are cheaper to run per passenger. More cabins offered at lower prices — a rightward shift.

How it connects. Like input prices (entry 7), it works by changing the cost of production, and it produces a change in supply (entry 6).

Check yourself

9.1 A technological advance in textbook printing. What happens?

Show answer

An increase in textbook supply — the supply curve shifts right. Not a movement along it, and nothing happens to demand.

9.2 Is a faster harvesting method “technology” in this sense?

Show answer

Yes. Technology means any method of turning inputs into output, not just electronics.

10

Expectations

★ Exam
Definition

If producers expect the price to rise later, they hold stock back and supply less today. If they expect it to fall, they sell now and supply more today.

Textbook pages 63–64. Shift factor 4.

This is the one factor that works in the opposite direction from its demand counterpart, and the reason is obvious once you say it: buyers and sellers are on opposite sides of the same bargain. If a price rise is coming, buyers want to buy now and sellers want to sell later.

Watch out. Do not carry the demand rule across. Expecting a price rise increases demand today and decreases supply today. Learning the two together is easier than learning either alone, because they are mirror images.

Textbook

Oil refineries. Petrol is stored for long periods before sale. Knowing demand peaks in summer, refiners hold stock back in spring — supply today falls.

Numeric

The mirror. Expect a rise: demand today shifts right, supply today shifts left. Both changes push today’s price up, which is why expectations can be self-fulfilling.

Illustrative

A farmer with a silo. If wheat is expected to be dearer after the winter, the grain stays in the silo. Supply this month falls without a single field changing.

How it connects. A change in supply (entry 6), and the exact mirror of expectations on the demand side in Module 5.

Check yourself

10.1 Producers expect the price of their good to fall next month. What happens to supply today?

Show answer

It increases — the curve shifts right. Sell now while the price is still high.

10.2 Buyers and sellers both expect a price rise. Which way does each curve move?

Show answer

Demand shifts right (buy now) and supply shifts left (sell later). Both push today’s price up.

11

Number of producers

★ Exam
Definition

More producers means more supplied at every price, so the market supply curve shifts right.

Textbook page 64. Shift factor 5.

Nothing about any existing producer changes. The curve moves because there are more curves in the sum — exactly as a baby boom moved the demand curve in Module 5.

Watch out. This factor moves the market supply curve only. Each individual producer’s curve stays exactly where it was. Questions that ask “whose curve moved?” are testing this.

Textbook

Vietnam. New coffee-producing countries entered in the early 2000s, competing with Latin America. The market supply curve shifted right and beans were unusually cheap.

Numeric

Two producers become three. With Mr Figueroa and Mr Bien Pho supplying 3,000 and 2,000 pounds at $2, the market quantity is 5,000. Add a third and it rises without either of the first two doing anything different.

Illustrative

Farm vets, in reverse. Fewer vets in farm practice means less supplied at every price — a leftward shift from a fall in the number of producers.

How it connects. It is the factor that acts directly on the market supply curve (entry 13) rather than on any individual supply curve (entry 12). Like the other four determinants, it causes a change in supply (entry 6).

Check yourself

11.1 Twenty new coffee farms open. Whose supply curve moves?

Show answer

The market supply curve, and it shifts right. No existing farmer’s individual curve moves at all.

11.2 Which demand-side factor is this the mirror of?

Show answer

The number of consumers. Both work by changing how many curves are in the horizontal sum.

12

Individual supply curve

★ Exam
Definition

The relationship between quantity supplied and price for one producer.

Textbook page 64, panels (a) and (b) of Figure 6.5.

Your textbook introduces one seller at a time so the market curve can be built in front of you. Your framework requires it: the market supply curve is derived from the summation of individual supply curves.

Watch out. A change for one producer — a new machine, a cheaper supplier — shifts that producer’s curve and the market curve, because the market curve is the sum.

Textbook

Mr Figueroa. SFigueroa shows the quantity of coffee beans he will sell at any given price.

Numeric

At $2 a pound. Mr Figueroa supplies 3,000 pounds and Mr Bien Pho 2,000. Two individual curves, two numbers.

Illustrative

Each slopes up. Every producer supplies more at a higher price, which is why the sum of them does too.

How it connects. Add them horizontally and you get the market supply curve (entry 13). More producers means more curves in that sum — entry 11.

Check yourself

12.1 One of three coffee farmers buys a machine and supplies 500 more pounds at every price. Which curves shift?

Show answer

That farmer’s individual supply curve and the market supply curve both shift right, by 500 pounds at every price. The other two farmers’ curves do not move.

12.2 Does an individual supply curve slope upward?

Show answer

Yes — for one producer just as for the market, which is why the horizontal sum slopes upward too.

13

Market supply curve

★ Exam
Definition

The horizontal sum of the individual supply curves of all producers: it shows the quantity supplied by everyone at any given price.

Textbook page 64, panel (c) of Figure 6.5.

Pick a price, add the quantities, plot the total. You add quantities and hold the price fixed — the same operation as on the demand side, because every producer faces the same market price.

Your framework describes exactly this: the market supply curve (schedule) is derived from the summation of individual supply curves (schedules), and it is upward-sloping.

Watch out. Never add prices. If two producers each supply 2,000 pounds at $2, the market supplies 4,000 pounds at $2 — not 4,000 pounds at $4.

Textbook

Two producers. At $2 a pound, 3,000 + 2,000 = 5,000 pounds supplied to the market.

Numeric

A third producer. Add someone supplying 1,000 pounds at $2 and the market quantity becomes 6,000, with neither of the others changing anything.

Illustrative

Which curve a question means. If a question says “the supply curve” with no qualifier, it means this one.

How it connects. It is the sum of individual supply curves (entry 12), it is what a change in supply (entry 6) shifts, and crossing it with market demand gives equilibrium (entry 14). New producers (entry 11) add to the sum.

Check yourself

13.1 At $5, three producers would supply 10, 14 and 6 units. What is the market quantity supplied at $5?

Show answer

30 units. Hold the price fixed and add the quantities: 10 + 14 + 6.

13.2 Why must the sum of upward-sloping individual supply curves itself slope upward?

Show answer

At a higher price every producer supplies more than before, so the total at the higher price is larger. Add upward-sloping curves and you get an upward-sloping curve.

14

Equilibrium

★ Exam
Definition

An economic situation in which no individual would be better off doing something different.

Textbook page 66.

Your textbook’s image is the supermarket checkout. A new till opens, there is a rush, and then things settle so that every queue is about the same length. Nobody can save time by switching. That is equilibrium.

In a market, that condition becomes: the price has moved to a level at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. That is exactly how your framework puts it — the price brings the quantity supplied and the quantity demanded into balance, and the market clears with no shortages or surpluses.

Watch out. Equilibrium does not mean fair or good. It means nobody has a reason to change their behaviour. Whether it is efficient is a separate question — and your course does ask it, later in this unit, using consumer and producer surplus.

Textbook

The checkout queue. All lines the same length, nobody better off switching.

Numeric

Point E. At $1.00 a pound, producers want to sell 10.0 billion pounds and consumers want to buy 10.0 billion pounds. Equal, so the market clears.

Illustrative

Only one price works. Look at the gap column in the table above. Every other price leaves someone frustrated.

How it connects. It needs both the market supply curve (entry 13) and the demand curve. Surplus (entry 16) and shortage (entry 17) are defined as departures from it. On the diagram it is where the supply curve (entry 3) crosses the demand curve, at the equilibrium price and quantity (entry 15).

Check yourself

14.1 Which of these is true at equilibrium: the schedules are identical at every price, or the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied?

Show answer

The quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied — at one price. The two schedules are completely different at every other price, which is the point of the diagram.

14.2 Does equilibrium mean everyone who wants the good gets it?

Show answer

No. It means everyone willing to pay the equilibrium price finds a seller. People who will not pay that much simply do not buy, and that is not a shortage.

15

Equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity

★ Exam
Definition

The equilibrium price is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. It is also called the market-clearing price. The equilibrium quantity is the quantity bought and sold at that price.

Textbook page 66, Figure 6.6.

On a diagram it is where the two curves cross. In a free-response answer, label the price PE on the vertical axis and the quantity QE on the horizontal axis, with dashed lines to the intersection.

“Market-clearing” is the useful name: at that price every buyer willing to pay it finds a seller, and every seller willing to accept it finds a buyer. Nothing is left over and nobody is turned away.

Watch out. The equilibrium quantity is one number, not two. At equilibrium the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied are the same number, which is what makes it possible to call it the quantity traded.

Textbook

Coffee beans. PE = $1.00 per pound and QE = 10 billion pounds a year.

Numeric

Check it from the tables. At $1.00 the demand schedule gives 10.0 and the supply schedule gives 10.0. The only row where they match.

Illustrative

In a free-response answer. Curves labelled S and D, intersection marked, PE and QE labelled on the axes. That is the whole of the first free-response question in your textbook.

How it connects. It is where equilibrium (entry 14) sits on the diagram, and it is the price that market forces (entry 18) drive the market back to. What it tells people once it is there is entry 19. Without a graph, find it from the two schedules (entry 2).

Check yourself

15.1 Why is the equilibrium price also called the market-clearing price?

Show answer

Because it clears the market: every willing buyer finds a seller and every willing seller finds a buyer, so nothing is left unsold and nobody is left unserved.

15.2 On the coffee diagram, what are PE and QE?

Show answer

$1.00 a pound and 10 billion pounds a year.

16

Surplus

★ Exam
Definition

Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. Also called excess supply. It happens when the price is above equilibrium.

Textbook page 68, Figure 6.7.

You will be asked to calculate it, and then to say what happens next. The calculation is one subtraction, in one order:

Surplus = quantity supplied − quantity demanded.

At $1.50 a pound: producers want to sell 11.2 billion pounds, consumers want to buy 8.1 billion, so the surplus is 11.2 − 8.1 = 3.1 billion pounds.

Watch out. Subtract in that order or you will report a negative surplus. And do not confuse this with consumer surplus or producer surplus, which you meet later in this unit. They are a different idea that happens to share the word — a market with a surplus in this sense has less of that kind of surplus, not more.

Textbook

At $1.50. 11.2 supplied, 8.1 demanded, surplus 3.1 billion pounds. Some producers simply cannot find a buyer.

Numeric

At $2.00. 11.6 supplied, 7.1 demanded, surplus 4.5 billion pounds. The further above equilibrium, the bigger the gap.

Illustrative

On the graph. It is the horizontal distance between the two curves, measured at the price line. Show it as a labelled arrow.

How it connects. It is a departure from equilibrium (entry 14) in one direction; shortage (entry 17) is the other. It is what makes market forces (entry 18) push the price down.

Check yourself

16.1 At $1.25, quantity supplied is 10.7 and quantity demanded is 8.9. Surplus or shortage, and how big?

Show answer

A surplus, because the price is above $1.00. 10.7 − 8.9 = 1.8 billion pounds.

16.2 Does a surplus mean the good is unpopular?

Show answer

No. It means the price is too high. At $1.00 the same market clears completely.

17

Shortage

★ Exam
Definition

Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. Also called excess demand. It happens when the price is below equilibrium.

Textbook page 68, Figure 6.8.

Shortage = quantity demanded − quantity supplied.

At $0.75 a pound: consumers want 11.5 billion pounds, producers offer 9.1 billion, so the shortage is 11.5 − 9.1 = 2.4 billion pounds. There are would-be buyers who cannot find a seller.

Notice that the two subtractions are in opposite orders. That is not a trick — it is so that both answers come out positive. The name tells you which way round to subtract.

Watch out. A shortage is not the same as scarcity. Everything is scarce; a shortage is a specific, temporary condition caused by a price that is below the market-clearing level, and it disappears when the price rises.

Textbook

At $0.75. 11.5 demanded, 9.1 supplied, shortage 2.4 billion pounds.

Numeric

At $0.50. 14.2 demanded, 8.0 supplied, shortage 6.2 billion pounds. Further below equilibrium, bigger gap.

Illustrative

After a snowfall. Everybody wants a second-hand snowblower at once. At the old price there are not enough, so the price is bid up.

How it connects. The mirror of surplus (entry 16), and what makes market forces (entry 18) push the price up. It is a departure from equilibrium (entry 14) on the low-price side.

Check yourself

17.1 At $0.50, quantity demanded is 14.2 and quantity supplied is 8.0. Which is it, and how big?

Show answer

A shortage, because the price is below $1.00. 14.2 − 8.0 = 6.2 billion pounds.

17.2 Which subtraction gives a shortage, and why that order?

Show answer

Quantity demanded minus quantity supplied. Demand is the larger of the two below equilibrium, so that order gives a positive number.

18

Market forces restoring equilibrium

★ Exam
Definition

Whenever a market is out of balance, market forces drive the price toward equilibrium: a surplus pushes the price down, a shortage pushes it up.

Textbook pages 68–69.

The mechanism is people, not magic, and you should be able to say who does what:

  • Surplus. Some sellers cannot find buyers. A frustrated seller offers a lower price to poach business and tempt more buyers in. Prices fall until the surplus is gone.
  • Shortage. Some buyers cannot find sellers. A frustrated buyer offers more than the going price, or sellers realise they can charge more. Prices rise until the shortage is gone.

So the market price always moves toward the equilibrium price — the one price at which there is neither a surplus nor a shortage.

Watch out. In this module it is the price that is wrong and moves; the curves stay still. In the next module a curve moves and a new equilibrium price follows. Both are true, they are different situations, and mixing them up is the most expensive mistake in this part of the course.

Textbook

At $1.50. A 3.1 billion pound surplus gives frustrated sellers a reason to cut prices, which pushes the price down to $1.00.

Numeric

At $0.75. A 2.4 billion pound shortage means buyers bid the price up, back to $1.00.

Illustrative

The checkout again. The rush to the new till and the settling afterwards is the same process: an imbalance creates an incentive, and acting on it removes the imbalance.

How it connects. It is what makes the equilibrium price (entry 15) more than a definition, and it is driven by surplus (entry 16) and shortage (entry 17). What the moving price tells buyers and sellers is entry 19.

Check yourself

18.1 The market price is below the equilibrium price. What happens next, and why?

Show answer

The price rises. There is a shortage, so frustrated buyers offer more and sellers realise they can charge more.

18.2 Who actually changes the price in a surplus?

Show answer

Sellers who cannot find buyers. They cut prices to take business from other sellers and to bring more buyers in.

19

Equilibrium price as a signal

★ ⚑
Definition

The equilibrium price carries information. A rising price tells producers to put more resources into a good and tells buyers to use less of it; a falling price says the opposite. That is how prices guide the allocation of scarce resources.

Not in this module. Your textbook makes the point in Module 1, pages 2–3, and calls prices “economic signals” much later, on page 627.

⚑ Not in Module 6 — learn it from here. Your course says the equilibrium price provides information that guides how resources are used. Module 6 shows how the price reaches equilibrium but never says what the price tells anyone.

Nobody decides how much coffee the world should grow. When more people want coffee, its price rises — and that price reaches farmers in Brazil and Vietnam who will never meet the drinkers. It tells them that planting more is now worth it. The same price tells some drinkers to switch to tea. Two groups who never talk to each other both change what they do, because of one number.

Entry 18 is about how market forces move the price. This entry is about what the moved price says. A shortage’s rising price tells sellers to bring more; a surplus’s falling price tells them to move resources somewhere else.

Watch out. A signal only works if the price is free to move. If a price is held below equilibrium, the shortage stays and the message never arrives — producers have no reason to bring more. You will see exactly this with price ceilings, two modules from now.

Textbook

Command economies. Module 1: without prices free to rise when something is short, producers had no reason to make more of it, and shops were famous for long lines. In a market, high prices during a shortage give producers the incentive to make more.

Numeric

Closing a shortage. At $0.75 the coffee market is short by 2.4 billion pounds. As the price rises to $1.00, quantity supplied rises from 9.1 to 10.0 billion and quantity demanded falls from 11.5 to 10.0. Sellers offer 0.9 billion more and buyers want 1.5 billion less: 0.9 + 1.5 = 2.4, and the gap is gone.

Illustrative

Farm vets and pet vets. Your textbook’s feature on page 65: pet owners paid more for care, and vets moved out of farm practice into pet practice. The higher fees told vets where their skills were now wanted.

How it connects. It is what the equilibrium price (entry 15) does once market forces (entry 18) have got it there, and on the sellers’ side it works through the law of supply (entry 4).

Check yourself

19.1 A poor harvest sends the price of avocados sharply higher. Name one message the higher price sends to buyers and one it sends to growers elsewhere.

Show answer

To buyers: use fewer avocados — some switch to other foods, a movement up along their demand curve. To growers: planting avocados is now more rewarding, so more land and effort move into them, a movement up along the supply curve.

19.2 Why can a shortage not send its signal if the price is not allowed to rise?

Show answer

Because the price is the signal. If it stays below equilibrium, sellers see no reward for supplying more and buyers see no reason to use less, so the shortage persists and resources do not move.

The six things to get right from Module 6

  1. Own price → movement. Anything else → shift. The same rule as demand, for the second time.
  2. In production is not in consumption. Ask who is switching — a producer or a buyer — before you decide which curve moves.
  3. Input prices move supply the opposite way. Input price up, supply down, curve left.
  4. Surplus = Qs − Qd, above equilibrium. Shortage = Qd − Qs, below. Two subtractions, opposite orders, both giving a positive number.
  5. Here the price is wrong and moves. In the next module a curve moves instead. Keep the two situations apart.
  6. The price is a message. A rising price tells producers to bring more and buyers to use less; a falling one says the opposite. That is how a market decides where resources go.
