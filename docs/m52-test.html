Learning objective CBA-2.C asks students to explain how firms respond to profit opportunities and to calculate profit or loss; the module stops before entry and exit (Module 60) and before average total cost (Modules 55 and 59). Teach entries 12, 17 and 18 of the guide before setting the paper.
One convention. Krugman's margin definition subtracts "the explicit cost and depreciation"; the exam subtracts explicit costs. This paper follows the exam and counts depreciation as one of the explicit costs the accountant records — every stem says so where it matters.

The business used throughout. Unless a question says otherwise, it refers to Rosa's Taco Truck over one year. Rosa sells 20,000 tacos at $6 each. She pays $30,000 for ingredients, $8,000 for fuel and permits and $22,000 in wages to a helper, and her accountant records $4,000 of depreciation on the truck, so her explicit costs total $64,000. Rosa owns the truck: she could sell it for $40,000 and put the money in a bank paying 5% a year. She left a job paying $48,000 a year to run the truck, and she needs $4,000 a year as compensation for the risk of running her own business. Those three are her only implicit costs.

Fifteen items need arithmetic, because the learning objective's third verb is calculate and the released papers ask for this topic as a calculation more often than as a definition. The synthesis items each ask students to judge a claim: that owned land is free, that risk is not a cost, that a firm reported more economic than accounting profit, what accounting profit is in long-run equilibrium, and when a small business should keep going.

1. What is Rosa's total revenue for the year?

  • (A) $2,000
  • (B) $56,000
  • (C) $120,000
  • (D) $64,000
  • (E) $184,000

2. Which of the following is an explicit cost of Rosa's business?

  • (A) The value of the hours she spends cooking
  • (B) The $48,000 salary she gave up
  • (C) The $2,000 a year the truck's sale value would earn in a bank
  • (D) The $4,000 she needs as compensation for risk
  • (E) The $22,000 she pays her helper

3. A baker has been renting her oven for $3,000 a year. Which change turns that explicit cost into an implicit one?

  • (A) She pays the rent once a year instead of monthly.
  • (B) She uses her savings to buy the oven outright.
  • (C) She takes out a bank loan to pay the rent.
  • (D) She hires her brother at the market wage to run the oven.
  • (E) The landlord raises the rent to $3,600.

4. Jamal is deciding whether to take a ten-week summer program. The fees are $2,500 and the books $300. The program means giving up a summer job paying $420 a week.

What is the full opportunity cost of the program?

  • (A) $11,200
  • (B) $2,800
  • (C) $4,200
  • (D) $7,000
  • (E) $1,400

5. A farmer who owns her land outright says: "My land costs me nothing, so my farm is profitable as long as crop sales cover my bills for seed, fuel and labor."

Which evaluation is correct?

  • (A) She is wrong: the land could be leased or sold, and that forgone income is an implicit cost, so her economic profit can be negative even when sales cover her bills.
  • (B) She is right: owning the land means she pays nothing to use it.
  • (C) She is right, because the land was bought in the past and its price cannot be recovered.
  • (D) She is wrong, because she must also subtract the depreciation of the land.
  • (E) She is wrong, because the property tax on her land is an implicit cost.

  • (A) $120,000
  • (B) $56,000
  • (C) $60,000
  • (D) $2,000
  • (E) $66,000

  • (A) $0, because she owns the truck
  • (B) $4,000
  • (C) $6,000
  • (D) $2,000
  • (E) $40,000

  • (A) $2,000

What is the firm's economic profit?

  • (B) $0
  • (C) $800
  • (D) $2,000
  • (E) $4,800

10. Dev puts $60,000 of his savings into a print shop. A savings account would have paid 4% a year.

What is the yearly implicit cost of this financial capital?

  • (A) $240
  • (B) $60,000
  • (C) $0, because Dev pays no one for the use of his own money
  • (D) $62,400
  • (E) $2,400

11. Rosa is offered a job paying $52,000 a year instead of the $48,000 she gave up. Nothing about the taco truck changes.

Which is correct?

  • (A) Her accounting profit and her economic profit both fall by $4,000.
  • (B) Her economic profit from the truck falls to −$2,000, so she would be better off financially taking the job.
  • (C) Her accounting profit falls by $4,000.
  • (D) Her economic profit is unchanged, because the job offer has nothing to do with the truck.
  • (E) Her economic profit rises to $6,000.

12. A friend tells Rosa: "Your $4,000 for risk is not a real cost — nobody sends you a bill for it. Leave it out and your true economic profit is $6,000."

Which evaluation is correct?

  • (A) The friend is wrong: compensation for risk is an implicit cost — the extra return Rosa needs for putting her savings at risk — so her economic profit is $2,000.
  • (B) The friend is right: economic profit subtracts only costs that are paid.
  • (C) The friend is right, because the risk is already covered by the depreciation on the truck.
  • (D) The friend is wrong, because compensation for risk is an explicit cost, like an insurance premium.
  • (E) The friend is wrong, because Rosa's economic profit is $56,000.

13. A rival truck parks on Rosa's street, and the compensation Rosa needs for the risk of staying in business rises from $4,000 to $6,000 a year. Nothing else changes.

What is Rosa's economic profit now?

  • (A) $8,000
  • (B) $2,000, because compensation for risk is not counted in profit
  • (C) $0, a normal profit
  • (D) −$4,000
  • (E) $50,000

14. Consider a firm whose implicit costs are positive. Which statement is true?

  • (A) Accounting profit equals economic profit.
  • (B) Accounting profit is less than economic profit, because accounting profit does not consider implicit costs.
  • (C) Accounting profit is less than economic profit, because accounting profit does not consider explicit costs.
  • (D) Accounting profit is greater than economic profit, because accounting profit does not consider implicit costs.
  • (E) Accounting profit is greater than economic profit, because accounting profit does not consider explicit costs.

15. A business magazine reports that a firm earned an accounting profit of $70,000 and an economic profit of $80,000 last year.

Which evaluation is correct?

  • (A) Impossible, because accounting profit and economic profit are always equal.
  • (B) Plausible, if the firm's implicit costs were large.
  • (C) Plausible, if the firm's depreciation was $10,000.
  • (D) Plausible, because economic profit adds the value of the owner's time.
  • (E) Impossible: economic profit is accounting profit minus implicit costs, and implicit costs cannot be negative.

16. Kwame's repair shop earns a normal profit. To run it he gave up a salary of $38,000, and the savings he put into the shop would have earned $2,500 a year. He has no other implicit costs.

What is Kwame's accounting profit?

  • (B) $0
  • (C) $38,000
  • (D) $2,500
  • (E) −$40,500

17. A perfectly competitive firm is producing its profit-maximizing quantity. Its average total cost is $15, its average variable cost is $11, and the market price is $15.

Which of the following is true?

  • (A) Its economic profit will attract new firms to the industry.
  • (B) It earns zero economic profit and will stay in business.
  • (C) It is making a loss and should shut down.
  • (D) Its accounting profit is zero.
  • (E) Its accounting profit exceeds its implicit costs.

18. A pineapple farm operates in a perfectly competitive industry that is in long-run equilibrium. The farm has both explicit and implicit costs.

Is the farm's accounting profit positive, negative or zero?

  • (A) It cannot be determined without knowing the farm's explicit costs.
  • (B) Zero, because firms in long-run equilibrium earn zero profit.
  • (C) Negative, because its implicit costs are not covered.
  • (D) Positive, and equal to its implicit costs, because its economic profit is zero.
  • (E) Positive and greater than its implicit costs, which is what attracts new farms.

19. A firm's total revenue is $200,000, its explicit costs are $150,000 and its implicit costs are $60,000.

Which describes its profit?

  • (A) An economic loss of $210,000
  • (B) An accounting profit of $50,000 and an economic profit of $50,000
  • (C) An accounting profit of $50,000 and an economic loss of $10,000
  • (D) An accounting loss of $10,000 and an economic profit of $50,000
  • (E) An accounting profit of $140,000 and an economic loss of $10,000

20. Two cafés each report an accounting profit of $35,000. Owner X could earn $30,000 working elsewhere; owner Y could earn $40,000. Neither has any other implicit cost.

Basing their decisions on profit alone, what should the owners do?

  • (A) Neither decision changes, because firms respond to accounting profit.
  • (B) Both should stay open, because both cafés are profitable.
  • (C) Both should close, because neither earns enough.
  • (D) X should close and Y should stay open.
  • (E) X should stay open and Y should close.

21. A snack stand takes in $5,000 in July. Its explicit costs, including depreciation, are $1,200, and the implicit cost of the owner's capital is $300. The owner will keep the stand only if it earns at least a normal profit.

Which conclusion is correct?

  • (A) She should keep it if her time is worth $3,500 or less.
  • (B) She should keep it, because her accounting profit is $3,800.
  • (C) She should close it, because counting her time always makes economic profit negative.
  • (D) She should keep it if her time is worth less than $5,000.
  • (E) She cannot decide until she knows the explicit cost of her time.

22. Firms in a market with no barriers to entry are earning positive economic profit.

Which describes the long-run adjustment?

  • (C) Firms exit, market supply decreases, and the price rises.
  • (D) New firms enter until accounting profit falls to zero.

  • (D) Firms exit until accounting profit falls to zero.
  • (E) Some firms exit, market supply decreases, and the price rises until the remaining firms earn a normal profit.

Solutions — every option explained

Question 1 — Profit and total revenue · Application

Answer: (C)

Total revenue is price times quantity: 20,000 × $6 = $120,000. No cost is subtracted.

  • (A) ✗ Economic profit.
  • (B) ✗ Accounting profit, not revenue.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Rosa's explicit costs.
  • (E) ✗ Adds the explicit costs to revenue.

Question 2 — Explicit cost · Analysis

Answer: (E)

An explicit cost is paid out in money. Of the five, only the helper's wages leave the business as a payment; the other four are benefits Rosa gives up.

  • (A) ✗ Implicit, and already counted in the salary she gave up.
  • (B) ✗ Implicit: the entrepreneur's time.
  • (C) ✗ Implicit: the cost of financial capital.
  • (D) ✗ Implicit: nobody pays it.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 3 — Implicit cost · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Once she owns the oven she pays no rent, but the savings she spent on it would have earned interest. The cost has moved from a payment to a benefit given up.

  • (A) ✗ The timing of a payment does not change what it is.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The rent is still paid, and the loan's interest is a second explicit cost.
  • (D) ✗ A wage paid at the market rate is explicit.
  • (E) ✗ The explicit cost rises; it does not change kind.

Question 4 — The full cost of a choice · Application

Answer: (D)

Explicit cost $2,500 + $300 = $2,800; implicit cost 10 × $420 = $4,200. The full cost is the sum: $2,800 + $4,200 = $7,000.

  • (A) ✗ Counts the forgone wages twice.
  • (B) ✗ Explicit cost only.
  • (C) ✗ Implicit cost only.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The difference between the two parts, not the sum.

Question 5 — Using your own resources · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Owning a resource does not make using it free: it could earn income in its next-best use. The land's forgone rent or sale income is an implicit cost, so sales that cover her bills give a positive accounting profit and possibly a negative economic profit — the position of the New England farms in Krugman's fyi.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Confuses paying nothing with costing nothing.
  • (C) ✗ The land can still be leased or sold today; its alternative use is not in the past.
  • (D) ✗ Land does not wear out, and depreciation would be an accounting entry in any case.
  • (E) ✗ A tax is paid in money, so it is explicit.

Question 6 — Accounting profit · Application

Answer: (B)

Accounting profit is total revenue minus explicit costs: $120,000 − $64,000 = $56,000.

  • (A) ✗ Revenue.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Leaves out the $4,000 of depreciation the accountant records.
  • (D) ✗ Economic profit.
  • (E) ✗ Subtracts the implicit costs instead of the explicit ones.

Question 7 — Depreciation · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The implicit cost of capital is what the truck's value would earn elsewhere: 5% of $40,000 = $2,000. Depreciation is a different thing — the truck's wear, which the accountant has already counted among the explicit costs.

  • (A) ✗ Owning the truck moves its cost from explicit to implicit; it does not remove it.
  • (B) ✗ That is the depreciation.
  • (C) ✗ Adds the depreciation to the interest given up.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The truck's value, not the yearly income it could earn.

Question 8 — Economic profit · Application

Answer: (A)

Implicit costs are $2,000 + $48,000 + $4,000 = $54,000. Economic profit = $56,000 − $54,000 = $2,000, or equally $120,000 − $64,000 − $54,000.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Accounting profit.
  • (C) ✗ Subtracts only the implicit costs from revenue.
  • (D) ✗ Subtracts the depreciation a second time.
  • (E) ✗ Leaves out compensation for risk.

Question 9 — Economic profit · Application

Answer: (C)

Total revenue 500 × $12 = $6,000. Economic profit = $6,000 − $4,000 − $1,200 = $800.

  • (A) ✗ Revenue.
  • (B) ✗ Would require the costs to equal revenue exactly.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Accounting profit: the implicit cost is left out.
  • (E) ✗ Subtracts only the implicit cost.

Question 10 — Cost of financial capital · Application

Answer: (E)

The implicit cost of the money is the interest it would have earned: 4% of $60,000 = $2,400 a year.

  • (A) ✗ A decimal slip: 0.4% rather than 4%.
  • (B) ✗ The sum invested, not the yearly income it could earn.
  • (C) ✗ An implicit cost involves no payment by definition.
  • (D) ✗ Adds the sum to the interest.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 11 — The entrepreneur's time · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Rosa's time is now worth $52,000 elsewhere, so her implicit costs rise by $4,000 to $58,000. Accounting profit is still $56,000; economic profit is $56,000 − $58,000 = −$2,000. An economic loss says her resources would earn more elsewhere — here, in the job.

  • (A) ✗ Only economic profit falls.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Accounting profit counts no implicit cost, so it does not change.
  • (D) ✗ Her next-best alternative is exactly what the implicit cost measures.
  • (E) ✗ The sign is reversed: a better alternative makes the truck look worse.

Question 12 — Compensation for risk · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

An implicit cost is a benefit given up, not a bill. Rosa could have earned a safe return; she needs more than that to put her savings at risk, and that extra is as much part of her opportunity cost as the bank interest. Leaving it out gives $6,000; counting it gives $2,000.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Describes accounting profit, not economic profit.
  • (C) ✗ Depreciation is the truck's wear and is on the accountant's books; it has nothing to do with risk.
  • (D) ✗ The friend's conclusion is wrong but so is this reason: an insurance premium is paid, while compensation for risk is not.
  • (E) ✗ That is her accounting profit.

Question 13 — Compensation for risk · Application

Answer: (C)

Implicit costs become $2,000 + $48,000 + $6,000 = $56,000, equal to her accounting profit. Economic profit is $56,000 − $56,000 = $0: a normal profit, and no reason to leave.

  • (A) ✗ Adds the increase instead of subtracting it.
  • (B) ✗ Compensation for risk is an implicit cost and is counted.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Subtracts the whole $6,000 from an economic profit that already had $4,000 of risk taken out.
  • (E) ✗ Subtracts only the risk compensation from accounting profit.

Question 14 — Accounting profit is never less than economic profit · Analysis

Answer: (D)

Economic profit is accounting profit minus implicit costs. When those are positive, accounting profit is the larger, and the reason is the one given. 2024 multiple-choice question 32 asks the same question with the same five options.

  • (A) ✗ Only when implicit costs are zero, which the stem rules out.
  • (B) ✗ Right reason, wrong direction.
  • (C) ✗ Accounting profit does subtract explicit costs.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Right direction, wrong reason.

Question 15 — Accounting profit is never less than economic profit · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

For economic profit to exceed accounting profit, implicit costs would have to be −$10,000. An implicit cost is a benefit given up, and giving something up cannot be worth less than nothing. The report is impossible.

  • (A) ✗ Right conclusion, wrong reason: the two differ whenever there are implicit costs.
  • (B) ✗ Large implicit costs make economic profit smaller.
  • (C) ✗ Depreciation is already in accounting profit and cannot raise economic profit above it.
  • (D) ✗ The owner's time is subtracted, not added.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 16 — Normal profit · Application

Answer: (A)

At normal profit economic profit is zero, so accounting profit exactly covers the implicit costs: $38,000 + $2,500 = $40,500.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Normal profit is zero economic profit.
  • (C) ✗ Leaves out the cost of his financial capital.
  • (D) ✗ Leaves out his time.
  • (E) ✗ Reverses the sign.

Question 17 — Normal profit · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Price equals average total cost, so total revenue equals total cost, including the implicit costs: economic profit is zero. That is a normal profit, so the firm stays. 2016 multiple-choice question 24 is built the same way.

  • (A) ✗ Zero economic profit attracts no one.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ There is no loss, and price is well above average variable cost.
  • (D) ✗ Accounting profit equals the implicit costs, which are positive.
  • (E) ✗ They are equal, not one greater.

Question 18 — Normal profit · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

In long-run equilibrium a competitive firm earns zero economic profit. Its accounting profit is then total revenue minus explicit costs, which equals the implicit costs — positive, because the farm has some. The 2024 free-response question on a pineapple farm asks exactly this.

  • (A) ✗ The sign follows from the definitions; no numbers are needed.
  • (B) ✗ Confuses zero economic profit with zero accounting profit.
  • (C) ✗ At normal profit every implicit cost is covered.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ That would be positive economic profit, which is not long-run equilibrium.

Question 19 — Economic loss · Application

Answer: (C)

Accounting profit $200,000 − $150,000 = $50,000. Economic profit $50,000 − $60,000 = −$10,000: an economic loss of $10,000.

  • (A) ✗ Total cost reported as a loss.
  • (B) ✗ Ignores the implicit costs in economic profit.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The two are swapped.
  • (E) ✗ Subtracts the implicit costs to find accounting profit.

Question 20 — Firms respond to economic profit · Analysis

Answer: (E)

X's economic profit is $35,000 − $30,000 = $5,000; Y's is $35,000 − $40,000 = −$5,000. Firms respond to economic profit, so X stays and Y does better by leaving — the two cases of Babette's café.

  • (A) ✗ Firms respond to economic profit.
  • (B) ✗ Judges by accounting profit.
  • (C) ✗ X earns more than in her next-best use.
  • (D) ✗ The two are reversed.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 21 — Firms respond to economic profit · Synthesis

Answer: (A)

Accounting profit is $5,000 − $1,200 = $3,800; after the $300 implicit cost of capital, $3,500 is left to cover her time. She earns at least a normal profit if her time is worth $3,500 or less — the reasoning of Krugman's Sunny's Sno Cone Stand question.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Ignores every implicit cost.
  • (C) ✗ Counting her time lowers economic profit but need not make it negative.
  • (D) ✗ Compares her time with revenue, ignoring every other cost.
  • (E) ✗ Her time has no explicit cost; its cost is implicit, and the question is what it is worth.

Question 22 — Entry and exit · Analysis

Answer: (B)

Economic profit means outsiders can do better here than in their current use, so they enter. Entry raises market supply, the price falls, and entry stops when economic profit is zero.

  • (A) ✗ Entry adds to supply, not demand.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ Firms exit when there are losses.
  • (D) ✗ Entry stops at zero economic profit; accounting profit is still positive.
  • (E) ✗ With free entry, positive economic profit cannot last.

Question 23 — Entry and exit · Analysis

Answer: (E)

An economic loss means the firms' resources would earn more elsewhere, whatever their books say. Some leave; supply falls; the price rises until those remaining earn a normal profit.

  • (A) ✗ Exit raises the price, so firms stop leaving long before all are gone.
  • (B) ✗ Firms respond to economic profit.
  • (C) ✗ Nobody enters a market making economic losses.
  • (D) ✗ Exit stops at zero economic profit, with accounting profit still positive.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 24 — Profit per unit · Application

Answer: (C)

Profit = (P − ATC) × Q = ($8 − $6.50) × 200 = $300. Check with totals: $1,600 − $1,300 = $300.

  • (A) ✗ Adds revenue and cost.
  • (B) ✗ The profit on one unit; it must be multiplied by quantity.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Total revenue.
  • (E) ✗ Total cost.

Question 25 — Profit per unit · Application

Answer: (D)

Profit = ($10 − $12) × 100 = −$200, a loss of $200. Average variable cost does not enter the calculation of profit; it matters for the separate question of whether to shut down in the short run.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 18 concept entries appear. CBA-2.C.2 — the implicit costs and normal profit — carries the first eighteen items, and CBA-2.C.1 — firms respond to economic profit — carries items 19 to 23. The last two are the per-unit calculation the learning objective's calculate verb asks for.

Bloom levels. Application 11 · Analysis 9 · Synthesis 5, a ratio of 1 : 0.8 : 0.5 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. The paper is more numerical than the standard because the learning objective's third verb is calculate, and the released papers examine this topic with numbers as often as with words.

Six items test what Module 52 does not teach. Items 12 and 13 are compensation for risk (risk is 0 times in pp. 530–535); items 22 and 23 are entry and exit (Module 60); items 24 and 25 use average total cost (Modules 55 and 59). If the class has not met average total cost, hold items 17, 24 and 25 back until it has.

Four items are modelled on released questions, with new numbers or contexts: Question 9 on 2016 multiple-choice question 23 (economic profit from revenue, explicit and implicit cost), Question 14 on 2024 question 32 (which profit is larger, and why), Question 17 on 2016 question 24 (price equal to average total cost), and Question 18 on the 2024 free-response question on a pineapple farm in long-run equilibrium.

Question 18 is the one to discuss aloud. It is the most common confusion in the topic — that zero economic profit means zero accounting profit — and the released free-response question that tests it gives no numbers at all.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: an implicit cost treated as zero because nothing is paid (7A, 10C, 12B); accounting profit used where economic profit is asked (8B, 9D, 19B, 20B, 21B); implicit costs subtracted instead of explicit ones (6E, 8C, 19E); normal profit read as zero accounting profit (16B, 17D, 18B); depreciation confused with the cost of capital (7B, 7C, 12C); a per-unit figure left unmultiplied (24B, 25C); a cost subtracted twice (8D, 13D); and accounting profit treated as what firms respond to (20A, 22D, 23B, 23D).

Two distractors are half right, flagged in the solutions: 14B has the right reason and the wrong direction, and 14E the right direction and the wrong reason. 15A reaches the right conclusion for the wrong reason.

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 5 · D 5 · E 5 — exactly even, with no letter repeating in consecutive questions.
