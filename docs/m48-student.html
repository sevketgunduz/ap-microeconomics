Other Elasticities

Nineteen terms from Module 48 of Krugman’s Economics for AP. Three more elasticities — cross-price, income, and supply — and one rule that changes: from here on, the sign is the answer.

Textbook pages 475–481·19 terms· Topics 2.4 and 2.5·2 periods

How to use this guide

Read Module 48 in your textbook first. Entries 1 to 7 are cross-price elasticity; entries 8 to 11 are income elasticity; entries 12 to 18 are the price elasticity of supply and what determines it; entry 19 is the story that uses two of them at once.

  • ★ Exam means the framework can test it.
  • ★ ⚑ means it is examinable and Module 48 of your textbook does not teach it. Entries 5 and 17 carry the flag.
  • ○ Background means it helps you understand the topic but will not be asked about on its own.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it.

The one idea. Every elasticity in this module is the same ratio you already know. What changes is what goes in it — and that the sign now carries information. Positive cross-price means substitutes, negative means complements; positive income means normal, negative means inferior. Never drop a minus sign again.

⚑ Two third cases your textbook never lists. A cross-price elasticity can be positive, negative or zero — and zero means the goods are not related, a phrase that appears nowhere in your textbook. And your course names the price of alternative inputs as a determinant of supply elasticity where your textbook says availability. Entries 5 and 17.

The nineteen terms

01

Elasticity beyond price

★ Exam
Definition

Elasticity measures how responsive any variable is to a change in any other. Price and quantity demanded were only the first pair.

Textbook page 475.

Quantity demanded does not depend on the good's own price alone. It depends on the prices of related goods and on income — the demand shifters you met in Module 5. Each of those relationships can be measured, and the measure is always the same ratio:

elasticity = % change in the thing that responds ÷ % change in the thing that moved

This module adds three of them: cross-price elasticity of demand, income elasticity of demand, and the price elasticity of supply.

Watch out. Say which elasticity you mean, every time. “The elasticity is 1.8” is ambiguous; “the income elasticity of demand is 1.8” is not.

1

Price of the good → quantity demanded. Price elasticity of demand. Modules 46 and 47.

2

Price of another good → quantity demanded. Cross-price elasticity of demand.

3

Income → quantity demanded. Income elasticity of demand.

How it connects. The first new one is cross-price elasticity (entry 2), the second is income elasticity (entry 8), and the one on the other side of the market is the price elasticity of supply (entry 12).

Check yourself

1.1 A firm wants to know how its sales respond to a rival's advertising. Can elasticity help?

Show answer

Yes. The same ratio works: percentage change in your quantity sold divided by percentage change in their advertising spend. The method is completely general.

1.2 Why is it never enough to say “demand is elastic” in this module?

Show answer

Because there are now three elasticities in play. Price-elastic, income-elastic and cross-price are different measurements and can point different ways for the same good.

02

Cross-price elasticity of demand

★ Exam
Definition

The percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good, divided by the percentage change in the price of another.

Textbook page 475, Equation 48-1.

cross-price elasticity between A and B = % change in quantity of A demanded ÷ % change in the price of B

Your textbook's pair is hot dogs and hot dog buns. Raise the price of hot dogs and people buy fewer buns — so the demand curve for buns shifts left, and the cross-price elasticity is negative.

Note which good is on top. Divide the change in A's quantity by the change in B's price and you have measured the effect of B on A. Swap them and you are measuring something else.

Watch out. Write the formula with the goods named. “Quantity of A over price of B” earns the formula mark on a free-response question; an unlabelled ratio does not.

1

Hot dogs dearer → fewer buns bought. Negative cross-price elasticity.

2

Hot dogs dearer → more hamburgers bought. Positive cross-price elasticity.

3

Hot dogs dearer → same number of umbrellas bought. A cross-price elasticity of about zero — the case in entry 5.

How it connects. It is one of the elasticities the general idea (entry 1) opens up, its sign separates substitutes (entry 3) from complements (entry 4), and because the sign carries the answer it is the first place keeping the minus sign (entry 7) matters.

Check yourself

2.1 The price of tea rises 10% and the quantity of coffee demanded rises 4%. What is the cross-price elasticity of coffee with respect to tea?

Show answer

+0.4. Four divided by ten, and positive because both moved the same way.

2.2 Why does the order of the two goods matter?

Show answer

Because the formula divides a quantity change for one good by a price change for the other. Reversing them measures the reverse relationship and generally gives a different number.

03

Substitutes — a positive sign

★ Exam
Definition

If the cross-price elasticity is positive, the goods are substitutes: a rise in one price raises demand for the other.

Textbook pages 475–476.

Hot dogs and hamburgers. Put up the price of hot dogs and shoppers switch to hamburgers, so the quantity of hamburgers demanded rises while the price of hot dogs rises. Both changes are positive, so the ratio is positive.

This is the substitution effect of Module 46 reappearing as a measurement — and it is the demand shifter of Module 5 acquiring a number.

Watch out. A positive cross-price elasticity does not mean demand went up because the good got cheaper. Nothing happened to this good's own price; the change came from the other good.

1

Butter and margarine, +0.25. Your textbook's review question: substitutes, but weak ones.

2

Two brands of the same cereal, +3. Very close substitutes.

3

Train tickets and coach tickets. Positive: dearer trains fill coaches.

How it connects. It is the positive case of cross-price elasticity (entry 2), its opposite is the negative case (entry 4), and how far above zero it sits is a measure of how close the two goods are (entry 6).

Check yourself

3.1 Coffee rises in price and the quantity of tea demanded rises 6%. Substitutes or complements?

Show answer

Substitutes — the cross-price elasticity is positive.

3.2 A cross-price elasticity is reported as +0.05. What does that say?

Show answer

The goods are substitutes in principle, but very weak ones: a price change in one barely moves demand for the other.

04

Complements — a negative sign

★ Exam
Definition

If the cross-price elasticity is negative, the goods are complements: a rise in one price reduces demand for the other.

Textbook page 476.

Hot dogs and hot dog buns. Dearer hot dogs mean fewer hot dogs eaten, and fewer hot dogs eaten mean fewer buns bought. The price went up and the quantity of the other good went down, so the ratio is negative.

Your textbook's free-response question gives a strong case: when the price of good A falls, the quantity of good B demanded doubles, giving a cross-price elasticity of −3. The two are complements, and strong ones.

Watch out. “Negative” here is not a judgement about the goods and not a number to be tidied away. If you drop the minus sign you turn complements into substitutes and lose the mark.

1

Printers and ink cartridges. Negative: dearer printers, fewer cartridges sold.

2

Petrol and large cars. Negative, and historically a strong one.

3

Good A and good B in your textbook's review, −3. Strong complements — the quantity of B moves three times as fast as the price of A.

How it connects. It is the negative case of cross-price elasticity (entry 2), its opposite is the positive case (entry 3), how far below zero it sits is a measure of strength (entry 6), and the case between the two is goods that are not related (entry 5).

Check yourself

4.1 Tennis rackets rise in price and the quantity of tennis balls demanded falls 8%. What kind of goods are these?

Show answer

Complements. The cross-price elasticity is negative.

4.2 Why can a cross-price elasticity of −0.1 and one of −4 both mean complements?

Show answer

Because the sign alone settles the relationship. The size says how strong it is: −0.1 is a weak complement, −4 a very strong one.

05

Goods that are not related

★ ⚑
Definition

If the cross-price elasticity is about zero, the two goods are not related: a change in one price leaves demand for the other where it was.

Not in Module 48 — your textbook's table lists only two cases.

⚑ Not in Module 48 — learn it from here. Your course says the cross-price elasticity tells you whether goods are “substitutes, complements, or not related”. The phrase not related appears nowhere in your textbook — not in this module, not in its summary table, not in its 927 pages. The idea is simple; the case is just missing.

Your course names three outcomes for a cross-price elasticity: substitutes, complements, or not related. Your textbook's summary table has a row for negative and a row for positive, and no row for zero.

The missing case is the easiest of the three to see. What happens to the quantity of toothpaste demanded when the price of bicycle tyres goes up? Nothing. The percentage change in quantity is zero, so the cross-price elasticity is zero, and the goods are unrelated.

Most pairs of goods in an economy are like this. Only a handful of goods are close enough to anything else to be substitutes or complements at all.

Watch out. Zero means “no relationship”, not “a weak one”. A small positive number like +0.05 still means substitutes; it is only at zero that the goods are unrelated.

1

Toothpaste and bicycle tyres. Cross-price elasticity zero: unrelated.

2

Shoelaces and mobile phone contracts. Zero again. No plausible connection.

3

Pencils and paper. Not zero — these are complements, and the elasticity is negative.

How it connects. It is the third case alongside complements (entry 4), and it is the zero point of the scale that the strength of the relationship (entry 6) measures.

Check yourself

5.1 An exam option says “cross-price elasticity equals 0, so the goods are substitutes”. Is it right?

Show answer

No. Zero means unrelated. Substitutes require a positive value, however small.

5.2 Name a pair of goods you would expect to have a cross-price elasticity of about zero, and say why.

Show answer

Any pair with no connection in use or in production — socks and garden gnomes, say. A change in one price gives buyers no reason at all to change their purchases of the other.

06

How big the number is

★ Exam
Definition

The sign says which relationship. The size says how strong it is.

Textbook pages 475–476.

Your textbook is explicit: if two goods are close substitutes the cross-price elasticity will be “positive and large”; if they are not close substitutes it will be “positive and small”. The same scale runs downwards for complements: slightly below zero means weak complements, strongly negative means strong complements.

So a cross-price elasticity carries two pieces of information at once, and a complete answer uses both.

Watch out. Compare sizes by magnitude. −4 is a stronger relationship than +0.3, even though −4 is the smaller number on a number line.

1

+3 for two brands of cereal. Substitutes, and very close ones.

2

+0.25 for butter and margarine. Substitutes, but weak — most butter buyers are not switching.

3

−3 for your textbook's goods A and B. Complements, and strong ones.

How it connects. It refines the positive case (entry 3) and the negative case (entry 4), and at exactly zero it becomes no relationship at all (entry 5).

Check yourself

6.1 Which pair is more closely related: one with a cross-price elasticity of +0.2 or one with −2.5?

Show answer

The pair at −2.5. Compare magnitudes: 2.5 is a much stronger link than 0.2, and the sign tells you it is a complementary one.

6.2 Two goods have a cross-price elasticity of +0.02. How would you describe them?

Show answer

Technically substitutes, but so weakly related that in practice they behave almost like unrelated goods.

07

Keeping the minus sign

★ Exam
Definition

For the price elasticity of demand the sign is always negative and is dropped. For cross-price and income elasticity the sign is the answer.

Textbook page 476.

Your textbook says it outright: “in the case of the cross-price elasticity of demand, the sign (plus or minus) is very important … So we cannot drop the minus sign as we did for the price elasticity of demand.”

ElasticitySignWhat the sign tells you
Price elasticity of demandalways negativeNothing — so it is dropped
Cross-price elasticityeitherSubstitutes or complements
Income elasticityeitherNormal or inferior
Price elasticity of supplyalways positiveNothing — there is nothing to drop

Watch out. This is the most expensive habit to carry over from Module 46. A student who reports a cross-price elasticity of −3 as “3” has just called complements substitutes.

1

Price elasticity of demand −1.4. Report 1.4. The sign carries no information.

2

Cross-price elasticity −3. Report −3. Dropping the sign destroys the answer.

3

Income elasticity −0.6. Report −0.6: an inferior good.

How it connects. It applies to cross-price elasticity (entry 2), to normal goods (entry 9) and to inferior goods (entry 10), and it is the one rule that does not carry over from the price elasticity of demand.

Check yourself

7.1 You calculate a cross-price elasticity of −0.8 and write “0.8, so the goods are substitutes”. What has gone wrong?

Show answer

You dropped a sign that was carrying the answer. A negative cross-price elasticity means complements.

7.2 Why is there no sign problem for the price elasticity of supply?

Show answer

Because the supply curve slopes upward: price and quantity supplied move the same way, so the ratio is positive to begin with.

08

Income elasticity of demand

★ Exam
Definition

The percentage change in the quantity of a good demanded, divided by the percentage change in the consumer's income.

Textbook page 476, Equation 48-2.

income elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income

Your textbook's review question: Chelsea's income rises from $12,000 to $18,000 and her CD purchases go from 10 to 40 a year. By the midpoint method income is up 40% and quantity is up 120%, so the income elasticity is 3.

Economists use it to predict which industries will grow as a country gets richer — which is exactly what the farming story at the end of this guide is about.

Watch out. The denominator is income, not price. Nothing in this calculation has anything to do with the good's own price, and a student who divides by a price change has computed something else entirely.

1

Income +40%, CDs +120%. Income elasticity 3. Strongly normal.

2

Income +22.2%, units +40%. Income elasticity 1.8 — your textbook's MCQ 2.

3

Income +10%, bus journeys −4%. Income elasticity −0.4: an inferior good.

How it connects. It is the second elasticity the general idea (entry 1) opens up, its sign separates normal goods (entry 9) from inferior goods (entry 10), and among normal goods it subdivides into income-elastic and income-inelastic (entry 11).

Check yourself

8.1 A household's income rises 25% and its spending on restaurant meals rises 50% in quantity. What is the income elasticity?

Show answer

+2. Fifty divided by twenty-five, positive because both rose.

8.2 Why can the income elasticity of demand be negative when the price elasticity of demand never is?

Show answer

Because quantity demanded can move either way when income moves — up for a normal good, down for an inferior one. Against the good's own price it can only move one way.

09

Normal goods — a positive sign

★ Exam
Definition

A positive income elasticity means the good is normal: more income, more of it.

Textbook page 476.

Your textbook's own wording: “When the income elasticity of demand is positive, the good is a normal good — that is, the quantity demanded at any given price increases as income increases.”

Almost everything is normal. What the number does not tell you is how fast: a normal good can grow faster or slower than income, which is the next distinction.

Watch out. “Positive” is the whole answer to “what is the income elasticity of a normal good?” It is not necessarily 1, or greater than 1 — your textbook's MCQ 3 is built on exactly this.

1

Restaurant meals. Positive, and large. A pay rise shows up here first.

2

Bread. Positive, but small. People do not eat much more bread as they get richer.

3

Chelsea's CDs, +3. Positive, and very large.

How it connects. It is the positive case of income elasticity (entry 8), its opposite is inferior goods (entry 10), the sign matters for the reason in keeping the sign (entry 7), and above or below 1 it splits into income-elastic and income-inelastic (entry 11).

Check yourself

9.1 An exam asks for the income elasticity of demand for a normal good. What is the best answer?

Show answer

Positive. Any specific number would be a guess — normal goods range from just above zero to well above 1.

9.2 Income falls 10% and sales of a good fall 6%. Normal or inferior?

Show answer

Normal: the income elasticity is +0.6, because quantity moved the same way as income.

10

Inferior goods — a negative sign

★ Exam
Definition

A negative income elasticity means the good is inferior: more income, less of it.

Textbook page 476.

Again your textbook's wording: “When the income elasticity of demand is negative, the good is an inferior good — that is, the quantity demanded at any given price decreases as income increases.”

This is the Module 46 definition of an inferior good, now measured. There the direction was the definition; here the sign reproduces it.

Watch out. Inferior is still not an insult. It describes which way demand moves when income moves, and nothing else.

1

Long-distance coach travel. Negative: people switch to trains and flights as incomes rise.

2

Supermarket own-brand goods. Negative, though not strongly.

3

Instant noodles. Negative, and a textbook case.

How it connects. It is the negative case of income elasticity (entry 8), its opposite is normal goods (entry 9), it is why keeping the sign (entry 7) matters, and it is half the explanation in the story of farming (entry 19).

Check yourself

10.1 A good's income elasticity is −1.5. Describe it.

Show answer

Strongly inferior: a 10% rise in income cuts the quantity demanded by 15%.

10.2 Can a good be inferior for one household and normal for another?

Show answer

Yes. It depends on where a household sits: own-brand goods may be inferior for a rich household and normal for a very poor one.

11

Income-elastic and income-inelastic

★ Exam
Definition

Among normal goods: above 1 is income-elastic (demand grows faster than income); between 0 and 1 is income-inelastic (demand grows, but more slowly).

Textbook page 476.

Both kinds are normal — the sign is positive in each case. The benchmark of 1 now separates two speeds rather than two directions:

  • Income-elastic (above 1): luxuries. Second homes, international travel. Income up 10%, quantity up more than 10%.
  • Income-inelastic (between 0 and 1): necessities. Food, clothing. Income up 10%, quantity up less than 10%.

This is what lets economists forecast which industries grow fastest as a country gets richer.

Watch out. Income-elastic is not the same as price-elastic. A good can be income-elastic and price-inelastic at once, and the two numbers answer different questions.

1

Chelsea's CDs, +3. Income-elastic: demand grows three times as fast as income.

2

Food. Income-inelastic: positive but well under 1 — the first half of the farming story.

3

Kylie's good X, +1.8. Income-elastic, and comfortably so.

How it connects. It subdivides the income elasticity (entry 8), it applies only to normal goods (entry 9), and it is the first half of the explanation in why farming shrank (entry 19).

Check yourself

11.1 A good has an income elasticity of 0.3. Is it normal or inferior, and is it a luxury or a necessity?

Show answer

Normal, because the sign is positive; and a necessity, because 0.3 is below 1 — income-inelastic.

11.2 Why is an income elasticity of −0.4 neither income-elastic nor income-inelastic?

Show answer

Because both of those terms describe normal goods. A negative income elasticity means the good is inferior, which is a different category altogether.

12

Price elasticity of supply

★ Exam
Definition

The percentage change in the quantity supplied, divided by the percentage change in price — measured as you move along the supply curve.

Textbook page 477, Equation 48-3.

price elasticity of supply = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price

Your textbook's example: if the price of tomatoes rises 10% and the quantity supplied also rises 10%, the price elasticity of supply is 1. If quantity rises 5%, it is 0.5; if it rises 20%, it is 2.

It is the same formula as the price elasticity of demand with one word changed — and with one convenience: because the supply curve slopes upward, the answer is already positive. There is no minus sign to drop.

Watch out. Read the question carefully. “Quantity supplied” and “quantity demanded” look alike on a busy exam page, and a supply elasticity computed from a demand schedule is simply wrong.

1

Price +10%, quantity supplied +10%. Elasticity of supply 1 — unit elastic.

2

Price +40%, hours supplied +50%. 1.25, from your textbook's web-design question: elastic.

3

Price +20%, quantity supplied +40%. 2 — elastic, from the review's second free-response question.

How it connects. It is the supply-side member of the family the general idea (entry 1) opens up, it is classified by the same benchmark of 1 (entry 13), and at its extremes it becomes a question about inputs (entry 16).

Check yourself

12.1 A 25% price rise brings a 15% rise in quantity supplied. What is the price elasticity of supply?

Show answer

0.6, and it is positive without any adjustment.

12.2 Why does the price elasticity of supply never need a minus sign dropped?

Show answer

Because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction along an upward-sloping supply curve, so the ratio is positive from the start.

13

The benchmark of 1 for supply

★ Exam
Definition

Exactly the same dividing line: above 1 supply is elastic, below 1 inelastic, at 1 unit elastic.

Textbook page 477.

Your course states this for supply in the same words it uses for demand, with one word changed: the benchmark is a magnitude of 1, “where the change in the price and the change in the quantity supplied are proportional”.

So nothing new has to be learned here. If you can classify a demand elasticity you can classify a supply one.

Watch out. There is no total-revenue rule on the supply side. The revenue results of Module 47 belong to the demand elasticity; do not try to apply them here.

1

Supply elasticity 0.4. Inelastic. Producers cannot respond much.

2

Supply elasticity 1. Unit elastic — the tomato case.

3

Supply elasticity 2. Elastic. Output responds twice as fast as price.

How it connects. It classifies the price elasticity of supply (entry 12), and its two limiting cases are a vertical supply curve (entry 14) and a horizontal one (entry 15).

Check yourself

13.1 Price rises 30% and quantity supplied rises 12%. Elastic or inelastic supply?

Show answer

Inelastic: 12 ÷ 30 = 0.4, which is below 1.

13.2 What does a supply elasticity of exactly 1 mean in words?

Show answer

Quantity supplied changes by exactly the same percentage as the price — proportionally, in the framework's own word.

14

Perfectly inelastic supply

★ Exam
Definition

The quantity supplied is fixed: the elasticity is 0 and the supply curve is a vertical line.

Textbook page 478, Figure 48.1 panel (a).

Your textbook's example is the part of the radio spectrum suitable for mobile phones. Governments sell the right to use it, and for technical reasons the number of frequencies cannot be increased at all — the supply curve is vertical at 100 frequencies. Raise the price from $2,000 to $3,000 and the quantity supplied does not move.

Your textbook notes something useful: perfectly inelastic and perfectly elastic cases are easier to find in supply than in demand. Land, spectrum, seats in a stadium, an original painting — the quantity really is fixed.

Watch out. Fixed is not zero. A vertical supply curve means the quantity cannot change, not that nothing is supplied. Misreading this wrecks every tax diagram you will meet in Topic 2.8.

1

Mobile phone frequencies. Vertical at 100, whatever the price.

2

Land in a city centre. Essentially fixed, which is why its price can rise so far.

3

Tickets for a stadium that seats 60,000. Perfectly inelastic on the night.

How it connects. It is the zero end of the supply scale (entry 13), its mirror image is perfectly elastic supply (entry 15), and it is what you get when the inputs (entry 16) are impossible to obtain.

Check yourself

14.1 A supply curve is vertical at 500 units. The price triples. What is the elasticity of supply?

Show answer

Zero. The percentage change in quantity supplied is zero whatever happens to price.

14.2 Why is a vertical supply curve more believable than a vertical demand curve?

Show answer

Because physical limits really do fix some quantities — spectrum, land, seats. On the demand side there is almost always some response at some price.

15

Perfectly elastic supply

★ Exam
Definition

Producers will supply any quantity at one price and none below it: the elasticity is infinite and the supply curve is a horizontal line.

Textbook page 479, Figure 48.1 panel (b).

Your textbook's example is pizza. Suppose a pizza costs $12 to make, counting every opportunity cost. Below $12 nobody would bother; at $12 or just above, the ingredients are plentiful and any number of pizza parlours could open. So the supply curve is horizontal at $12.

The reason is in the inputs: flour, tomatoes and cheese can all be produced in greater quantity without pushing up their own prices much.

Watch out. Horizontal supply is about cost, not generosity. The line sits at the price that just covers the cost of production, including opportunity cost.

1

Pizza at $12. Horizontal: any quantity at $12, none below.

2

A standard screw or nail. Close to horizontal — make as many as you like at the going price.

3

Bottled water from an ordinary plant. Inputs are cheap and plentiful, so supply is very elastic.

How it connects. It is the infinite end of the supply scale (entry 13), its mirror image is perfectly inelastic supply (entry 14), and it is what cheap alternative inputs (entry 17) makes possible.

Check yourself

15.1 A supply curve is horizontal at $40. What happens if buyers want twice as much?

Show answer

They get it, at $40. A perfectly elastic supply can expand without the price rising at all.

15.2 Why would no producer supply below the horizontal line's price?

Show answer

Because that price just covers the cost of production including opportunity cost. Below it, producers do better doing something else.

16

Determinant 1 — the availability of inputs

★ Exam
Definition

Supply is elastic when the inputs are easy to get and can be moved into and out of production cheaply. It is inelastic when they are fixed or costly to shift.

Textbook page 479.

This is the main determinant, and your textbook's two examples are chosen to make it obvious. Pizza inputs are everywhere, so pizza supply is almost perfectly elastic. Radio spectrum cannot be manufactured at all, so its supply is perfectly inelastic.

In between sit the goods that depend on a limited natural resource — gold, copper, coffee that grows only on certain land, fish that can only be caught up to a point. Their supply is inelastic because the input cannot simply be scaled up.

Watch out. Ask about the input, not the product. Pizza and spectrum are both ordinary goods; what makes their supply elasticities opposite is what they are made from.

1

Pizza. Flour, cheese, tomatoes — all abundant. Supply very elastic.

2

Radio spectrum. Cannot be made at all. Supply perfectly inelastic.

3

Gold. A limited natural resource, so supply is inelastic even at high prices.

How it connects. It is the determinant behind the price elasticity of supply (entry 12), it explains a vertical supply curve (entry 14), and your course states it in terms of the price of alternative inputs (entry 17).

Check yourself

16.1 Why is the supply of handmade violins by a famous maker inelastic?

Show answer

Because the essential input — that maker's time and skill — cannot be increased, however high the price goes.

16.2 A factory can switch between making chairs and making tables at almost no cost. What does that imply about the supply elasticity of each?

Show answer

Both are elastic. Inputs that shift cheaply between uses let output respond quickly to a price change.

17

Determinant 2 — the price of alternative inputs

★ ⚑
Definition

Supply is more elastic when the inputs you would need are cheap to attract away from their other uses.

Not in Module 48 — your textbook talks about availability, your course about price.

⚑ Not in Module 48 — learn it from here. Your course names the determinant as the price of alternative inputs. That phrase occurs nowhere in Module 48 — your textbook says the availability of inputs. The economics is identical, and a question using the framework's wording is asking about your textbook's idea.

Your course names this determinant directly: the price elasticity of supply “depends on certain factors such as the price of alternative inputs”. Your textbook names the availability of inputs instead, and the phrase alternative inputs never appears.

They are the same economics stated from two directions. An input is “available” to your industry when it is cheap enough to bid away from whoever is using it now:

  • Tomatoes are cheap to attract into pizza-making, so pizza supply is elastic.
  • A cardiac surgeon's hours are extremely expensive to attract into any new use, so the supply of heart operations is inelastic.

If a question asks about the price of alternative inputs, it is asking this question.

Watch out. Do not read “alternative inputs” as “substitute goods”. It is about what producers buy, not about what consumers buy — an input price, not a rival product's price.

1

Cheap, plentiful flour. Easy to attract into baking: supply of bread is elastic.

2

Specialist aerospace titanium. Expensive and fought over, so supply of aircraft parts is inelastic.

3

Seasonal farm labour at harvest. If wages elsewhere rise, attracting pickers costs more and supply becomes less elastic.

How it connects. It is your course's wording for the availability of inputs (entry 16), and it is the condition that makes perfectly elastic supply (entry 15) possible, alongside the passage of time (entry 18).

Check yourself

17.1 Two industries need the same input. In one it is cheap to attract; in the other producers must outbid a booming rival sector. Which has the more elastic supply?

Show answer

The first. Cheap alternative inputs mean output can be expanded without costs rising much, so quantity supplied responds strongly to price.

17.2 Your textbook says supply is elastic when inputs are “readily available”. How is that the same as your course's wording?

Show answer

An input is readily available precisely when it is cheap to obtain from its other uses. Availability and price are two ways of describing the same condition.

18

Determinant 3 — time, and the two runs

★ Exam
Definition

Supply becomes more elastic the longer producers have to respond. The long-run elasticity of supply usually exceeds the short-run one.

Textbook pages 479–480.

Flu vaccine is your textbook's case. Vaccine has to be grown in cultures over many months, so in the 2004 season no price could have raised output — the short-run elasticity was close to zero. If the price stayed at $90 for several years, producers would build bigger plants and hire more technicians, and output would rise a great deal.

So economists distinguish the short-run elasticity of supply — weeks or months — from the long-run elasticity — several years. The same distinction appears on the demand side in Module 47, and for the same reason.

Watch out. Short run and long run are about how much producers can change, not about dates on a calendar. In some industries a year is the long run; in others ten years is not.

1

Flu vaccine in one season. Perfectly inelastic in practice: the cultures take a year.

2

Flu vaccine over five years. Much more elastic — new plants, more technicians.

3

Oil from a new field. Years from decision to first barrel, so very inelastic in the short run.

How it connects. It is the third determinant after the price of alternative inputs (entry 17), and it is the reason a measured supply elasticity belongs to a period as well as to a market — as the farming story (entry 19) does on the other side.

Check yourself

18.1 A sharp rise in the price of copper produces almost no extra copper for three years, then a lot. What does that show?

Show answer

That supply is far more elastic in the long run: opening a mine takes years, so the short-run response is near zero.

18.2 Why might a government that wants more housing be disappointed in the first year of a subsidy?

Show answer

Because housing supply is very inelastic in the short run — land, planning and construction all take time. The long-run response is much larger.

19

Why farming shrank

○ Background
Definition

Two elasticities together explain why a sector can succeed technically and still shrink: demand for food is income-inelastic and price-inelastic.

Textbook pages 476–477, the fyi box.

In the 1940s about one American in six lived on a farm; by 1991 the official figure was 1.9%, and the government stopped publishing it. Your textbook explains the decline with two elasticities and nothing else.

  • Income elasticity below 1. As people get richer they spend more on food, but far less than in proportion. So food's share of national income falls, and with it farming's share.
  • Price elasticity of demand below 1. Farming has become steadily more productive, which pushes food prices down — and with inelastic demand, a lower price reduces total revenue. Progress in farming is good for consumers and bad for farmers.

Your textbook's verdict: the farm sector has been “a victim of success”.

Watch out. This story needs both elasticities. Either one alone leaves the puzzle unexplained, and an answer that uses only one will not earn full credit on a question like it.

1

Incomes double. Food spending rises, but much less than double. Agriculture's share falls.

2

Yields rise and food prices fall. With demand price-inelastic, farmers' total revenue falls — the Module 47 rule in action.

3

A farmer adopts a better seed and prospers. Individually yes. When every farmer does it, the price falls and the sector as a whole loses.

How it connects. It is income-inelastic demand (entry 11) and the contrast with inferior goods (entry 10) working together, and the second half of it is the same idea as a response that takes time (entry 18).

Check yourself

19.1 Which two elasticities does this story need, and what is the sign or size of each?

Show answer

The income elasticity of demand for food, positive but below 1; and the price elasticity of demand for food, below 1. One shrinks farming's share of spending, the other turns productivity gains into lower revenue.

19.2 Is food an inferior good, according to this story?

Show answer

No. Its income elasticity is positive — spending on food rises with income — just by less than income does. That is income-inelastic, not inferior.

Things to get right

  • Keep the sign. It is dropped only for the price elasticity of demand. For cross-price and income elasticity the sign is the answer.
  • Say which elasticity. Price-elastic, income-elastic and cross-price are three different measurements.
  • Zero means unrelated. Positive is substitutes however small; negative is complements however small.
  • Positive is the whole answer for a normal good's income elasticity. Not 1, not above 1 — positive.
  • Income-elastic and inferior are different things. Both income-elastic and income-inelastic goods are normal.
  • Order matters in cross-price. Quantity of one good over price of the other — say which is which.
  • Vertical supply is fixed supply, not absent supply.
  • Supply is more elastic in the long run, because producers can change what they use.
