Changes in Equilibrium

Sixteen terms: the fourteen in Module 7 of Krugman’s Economics for AP, plus two your course adds — what a shift does to consumer and producer surplus, and why the size of a change depends on the slopes of the curves. Everything here is examinable; there is no background material at all.

The 16 terms

01

Change in equilibrium

★ Exam
Definition

When a curve shifts, the crossing point moves, so there is a new equilibrium price and a new equilibrium quantity.

Textbook page 71.

Your framework puts it in one sentence: factors that shift the market demand and market supply curves cause price, quantity, consumer surplus, producer surplus and total economic surplus to change — and how much they change depends on how responsive buyers and sellers are. Price and quantity are this module. One curve moving is entries 2 to 7; both moving is entries 8 to 14; surplus and the size of the change are entries 15 and 16.

This is not the same situation as the surpluses and shortages in Module 6. There the price was wrong and the curves stood still. Here the curves move and the price follows. Entry 14 sets the two side by side, and it is worth reading now as well as later.

Watch out. A question in this module never starts out of equilibrium. It starts in equilibrium, something happens, and it ends in a different equilibrium.

Textbook

Vietnam. New producers entered coffee growing, supply rose, and the price fell. Nobody set a wrong price — the market moved to a new crossing.

Numeric

On the diagram. Start at price 8, quantity 12. Every button moves the crossing somewhere else. None of them leaves it where it was.

Illustrative

A medical report. News that chocolate is good for you raises the demand for chocolate. Supply is untouched, and a new equilibrium follows.

How it connects. Entries 2 to 5 are the four cases when one curve moves; entry 6 is why they come out that way; entries 8 to 11 are what happens when both move.

Check yourself

1.1 Your framework names five things that change when a curve shifts. Which two does Module 7 of your textbook cover?

Show answer

Price and quantity. The other three — consumer surplus, producer surplus and total surplus — are entry 15 here, and your textbook teaches them in Module 49.

1.2 Does a change in equilibrium mean the market was previously out of equilibrium?

Show answer

No. It was in equilibrium, a curve moved, and it settles at a new one. Being out of equilibrium at a wrong price is Module 6’s situation.

02

An increase in demand

★ Exam
Definition

Demand shifts right. The equilibrium price rises and the equilibrium quantity rises.

Textbook page 72, Figure 7.1.

Both up. This is the easiest of the four to remember and the one to derive the others from.

Follow the chain rather than the result. Demand shifts right, so at the old price there is now a shortage. A shortage pushes the price up, and as the price rises sellers move up along the supply curve. The new crossing is higher and further right.

Watch out. The supply curve did not move. Students often draw a second supply curve because “more is being sold”. More is being sold because sellers slid along the curve they already had.

Textbook

Tea gets dearer. Tea and coffee are substitutes, so the demand for coffee shifts right. Price and quantity of coffee both rise.

Numeric

On the diagram. Press Demand ↑. The crossing moves from price 8, quantity 12 to price 9, quantity 14.

Illustrative

Cheaper petrol in the 1990s. Petrol and large cars are complements, so cheaper petrol raised the demand for large cars — price and quantity of large cars both up.

How it connects. It is the adjustment chain (entry 6) run once. A decrease in demand (entry 3) is the same thing backwards. Sellers gain producer surplus (entry 15), and how much of the change is price and how much is quantity depends on the slope of supply (entry 16). An increase in supply (entry 4) is its supply-side counterpart.

Check yourself

2.1 Which curve do you slide along when demand shifts right?

Show answer

The supply curve — the one that did not move. That is always the rule: the curve that stays still is the one you travel along.

2.2 What imbalance exists for a moment at the old price?

Show answer

A shortage: at the old price, quantity demanded now exceeds quantity supplied. That shortage is what pushes the price up.

03

A decrease in demand

★ Exam
Definition

Demand shifts left. The equilibrium price falls and the equilibrium quantity falls.

Textbook page 72.

Both down. Entry 2 run backwards, and it takes half a minute if entry 2 is solid: at the old price there is now a surplus, the surplus pushes the price down, and sellers move down along the supply curve.

Together with entry 2 this gives the first half of the pattern worth carrying: when demand moves, price and quantity go the same way.

Watch out. “Bad news about a product” is usually a demand event, not a supply event. If a salmonella outbreak is traced to tomatoes, the tomatoes still exist and can still be grown — what changed is that nobody wants them.

Textbook

Cheaper pay-per-view. When a cable company cuts film prices, cinemas have more unfilled seats: the demand for cinema tickets shifts left, so price and quantity both fall.

Numeric

On the diagram. Press Demand ↓. The crossing moves from price 8, quantity 12 to price 7, quantity 10.

Illustrative

A faster chip arrives. Buyers postpone, so demand for computers with the old chip shifts left.

How it connects. The mirror of an increase in demand (entry 2). Both are cases of the adjustment chain (entry 6).

Check yourself

3.1 A salmonella outbreak is attributed to tomatoes. Which curve moves, and which way?

Show answer

Demand, to the left. Price and quantity both fall. Supply is unchanged — growers can still grow exactly as many tomatoes as before.

3.2 Complete the pattern: when demand moves, price and quantity go…

Show answer

…the same way. Both up for an increase, both down for a decrease.

04

An increase in supply

★ Exam
Definition

Supply shifts right. The equilibrium price falls and the equilibrium quantity rises.

Textbook page 73.

Opposite directions, and that is the second half of the pattern: when supply moves, price and quantity go opposite ways.

The chain again: supply shifts right, so at the old price there is a surplus, the surplus pushes the price down, and buyers move down along the demand curve. Cheaper, and more of it.

Watch out. Price falling and quantity rising feels contradictory if you are thinking about demand. It is not: buyers did not change their minds, they simply bought more because it got cheaper. That is a movement along the demand curve, not a shift of it.

Textbook

Vietnam enters. New producers shifted the supply curve for coffee beans right, and the price fell — exactly as predicted.

Numeric

On the diagram. Press Supply ↑. The crossing moves from price 8, quantity 12 to price 7, quantity 14.

Illustrative

Cheaper recycling. Technology lowered the cost of recycling paper, so the supply of recycled paper rose — and it is used more, at a lower price.

How it connects. The supply-side counterpart of an increase in demand (entry 2), and the mirror of a decrease in supply (entry 5). Buyers gain consumer surplus (entry 15).

Check yourself

4.1 Coffee beans get cheaper. What happens in the market for cups of coffee?

Show answer

Beans are an input, so the supply of cups shifts right: the price of a cup falls and the quantity rises.

4.2 Which curve do you slide along here?

Show answer

The demand curve — again, the one that did not move.

05

A decrease in supply

★ Exam
Definition

Supply shifts left. The equilibrium price rises and the equilibrium quantity falls.

Textbook pages 72–73, Figure 7.2.

The chain: supply shifts left, so at the old price there is a shortage, the shortage pushes the price up, and buyers move up along the demand curve, buying less.

This is the case that most often appears in news stories — a drought, a strike, a rise in an input price — and the one where students most often answer “prices went up so demand fell”. Demand did not fall. Quantity demanded fell, along an unchanged curve.

Watch out. Get the vocabulary right in written answers. Supply decreased (a shift); quantity demanded decreased (a movement). Writing “demand decreased” here describes a completely different diagram and loses the mark.

Textbook

The Vietnamese drought. Supply of coffee beans shifted left, the price rose and the quantity traded fell.

Numeric

On the diagram. Press Supply ↓. The crossing moves from price 8, quantity 12 to price 9, quantity 10.

Illustrative

Dearer machinery. If the machines used to make a good become more expensive, supply falls and the price of the good rises.

How it connects. The mirror of an increase in supply (entry 4), and one of the two ingredients in the opposite-directions case (entry 9). Like the other three, it is the adjustment chain (entry 6) run once.

Check yourself

5.1 A drought destroys part of the crop. Price rises and less is bought. Did demand fall?

Show answer

No. Supply fell, which pushed the price up; buyers then moved up along an unchanged demand curve, so quantity demanded fell. Demand itself is exactly where it was.

5.2 Which two of the four single-shift cases raise the price?

Show answer

An increase in demand and a decrease in supply. They differ in what happens to quantity: it rises in the first and falls in the second.

06

The adjustment chain

★ Exam
Definition

A shift creates a surplus or shortage at the old price. That imbalance moves the price, and the price change produces a movement along the curve that did not shift, until a new equilibrium is reached.

Textbook pages 71–72.

Learn this and you never have to memorise the four results. Four steps, in order:

  1. Which curve moved, and which way?
  2. At the old price, is there now a surplus or a shortage?
  3. A surplus pushes the price down; a shortage pushes it up.
  4. That price change moves you along the other curve to the new crossing.

Step 4 is the one students skip, and it is the one that gives you the quantity.

Watch out. The surplus or shortage here is temporary — it exists only at the old price and disappears at the new equilibrium. In Module 6 the imbalance was the whole story; here it is the middle of the story.

Textbook

Figure 7.1 narrated. Demand right → shortage at P₁ → price rises → movement up along supply → E₂ with higher price and higher quantity.

Numeric

Figure 7.2 narrated. Supply left → shortage at P₁ → price rises → movement up along demand → E₂ with higher price and lower quantity.

Illustrative

Two shortages, two answers. Both examples above start with a shortage and both end with a higher price — but the quantities go opposite ways, because a different curve moved.

How it connects. It generates entries 2 to 5 — including a decrease in demand (entry 3) — and it uses the surplus and shortage you met in Module 6. It only runs once you have diagnosed the event (entry 7), and entry 14 shows how to draw it.

Check yourself

6.1 Supply shifts left. Run the four steps.

Show answer

Supply left; at the old price quantity demanded now exceeds quantity supplied, so a shortage; the shortage pushes the price up; the higher price moves buyers up along the demand curve, so quantity falls. Price up, quantity down.

6.2 Why do two cases that both begin with a shortage end with different quantity answers?

Show answer

Because the curve you slide along is different. If demand shifted, you slide along supply and quantity rises; if supply shifted, you slide along demand and quantity falls.

07

Diagnosing the event

★ Exam
Definition

Before you draw anything, decide which curve the event moves and in which direction.

Textbook page 71.

One question settles it: did the event change what buyers want at a given price, or what sellers will offer at a given price?

  • Buyers → demand moves. Tastes, income, prices of related goods, expectations, number of buyers.
  • Sellers → supply moves. Input prices, technology, prices of related goods in production, expectations, number of sellers.
  • The good’s own price → neither curve moves. That is never the event in this module.

Watch out. Drawing the wrong curve beautifully earns nothing. Spend the first ten seconds of any question naming the curve and the direction, out loud or on the page, before the pencil touches the axes.

Textbook

Petrol gets dearer, SUV market. Petrol is a complement for SUV buyers, so demand for SUVs shifts left.

Numeric

Coffee beans get cheaper, market for cups. Beans are an input for sellers, so supply of cups shifts right.

Illustrative

More buyers plus better technology. Two events, two curves: demand right and supply right. That is a simultaneous shift, which is entry 8 onwards.

How it connects. It comes before the adjustment chain (entry 6) — step 1 of the chain is this — and before drawing the answer (entry 14).

Check yourself

7.1 The wages of workers who make a good rise. Which curve, which way?

Show answer

Supply, to the left. Wages are an input price, so this changes what sellers will offer at any given price.

7.2 Consumers expect the price of a good to fall next month. Which curve, which way?

Show answer

Demand, to the left, today. It changes what buyers want now. Remember that expectations move demand and supply in opposite directions.

08

Simultaneous shifts

★ Exam
Definition

Both curves move at the same time. One event shifts demand and another shifts supply.

Textbook page 73, Figure 7.3.

Real markets do this constantly, and released AP Microeconomics exams ask about it regularly — usually by asking which change leaves one of the two answers impossible to settle.

The method does not change. Handle each shift separately, ask what each one does to price and to quantity, and then combine. One variable will get two pushes in the same direction — that one is settled. The other gets two pushes in opposite directions — that one depends on which shift is bigger.

Watch out. Every simultaneous-shift question has exactly one determinate answer and exactly one indeterminate answer. If you have concluded that both are determinate, or that neither is, you have made a mistake.

Textbook

Figure 7.3. Two panels, both with demand up and supply down. Panel (a) has the bigger demand shift, panel (b) the bigger supply shift — and the quantities go opposite ways.

Numeric

On the diagram. Press D↑ and S↓. Two crossings appear, both at price 10, at quantities 14 and 10.

Illustrative

A new chip. Buyers postpone (demand falls) while makers clear stock (supply rises). Opposite directions, so the price is settled and the quantity is not.

How it connects. It splits into opposite directions (entry 9) and the same direction (entry 10), and it is resolved by relative magnitude (entry 11).

Check yourself

8.1 How many of the two answers are indeterminate in a simultaneous shift?

Show answer

Exactly one. The other is completely determined, and a full answer always states it.

8.2 More buyers enter a market and technology improves. Which two shifts are these?

Show answer

Demand increases and supply increases — both to the right, so the same direction.

09

Shifts in opposite directions

★ Exam
Definition

Demand up with supply down, or demand down with supply up. The price is determinate and the quantity is indeterminate.

Textbook page 74.

Take demand up and supply down. An increase in demand raises the price; a decrease in supply also raises the price. Two pushes the same way, so the price definitely rises.

But an increase in demand raises the quantity while a decrease in supply lowers it. Two pushes opposite ways, so the quantity could go either way.

Watch out. Indeterminate does not mean unchanged. On the diagram the quantity moves in both panels — up in one and down in the other. Staying exactly the same is a knife-edge coincidence, not the expected outcome.

Textbook

Figure 7.3 again. Both panels show the price rising. Only the quantity differs between them.

Numeric

On the diagram. D↑ and S↓ gives price 10 in both panels — up from 8 — with quantity 14 in one and 10 in the other.

Illustrative

The reverse case. Demand down and supply up: the price definitely falls, and the quantity is again indeterminate.

How it connects. One of the two halves of simultaneous shifts (entry 8), and the exact opposite of the same-direction case (entry 10). A decrease in supply (entry 5) is one of its ingredients, relative magnitude (entry 11) resolves it, and it produces an indeterminate quantity (entry 12).

Check yourself

9.1 Demand decreases and supply increases. What can you say for certain?

Show answer

The equilibrium price falls. Both shifts push it down. The quantity is indeterminate without knowing which shift is larger.

9.2 Why is the price certain here?

Show answer

Because both shifts push it the same way. A rise in demand and a fall in supply each raise the price on their own, so together they certainly do.

10

Shifts in the same direction

★ Exam
Definition

Both curves right, or both left. The quantity is determinate and the price is indeterminate.

Textbook page 74.

Both shifting right: an increase in demand raises the quantity, and an increase in supply raises it too. Two pushes the same way, so the quantity definitely rises. But the demand increase raises the price while the supply increase lowers it — so the price could go either way.

Compare with entry 9 and the symmetry is exact. Whichever variable gets two pushes the same way is the one you can answer.

Watch out. This is the case exam questions ask about most often: which change makes the price indeterminate? The answer is “an increase in demand and an increase in supply” — both in the same direction — or both decreasing.

Textbook

Coffee over a decade. Both supply and demand for coffee beans increased. The quantity traded certainly rose; the price depended on which rose more.

Numeric

On the diagram. D↑ and S↑ gives quantity 16 in both panels — up from 12 — with price 9 in one and 7 in the other.

Illustrative

Both left. Demand and supply both falling: the quantity definitely falls, and the price is indeterminate.

How it connects. The mirror of opposite directions (entry 9), and the case the word indeterminate (entry 12) is most often attached to. It is the other half of simultaneous shifts (entry 8), resolved by relative magnitude (entry 11).

Check yourself

10.1 Demand and supply both increase. What is certainly true?

Show answer

The equilibrium quantity increases. The price may rise, fall or stay the same.

10.2 State the rule that covers entries 9 and 10 in one sentence.

Show answer

Shifts in opposite directions settle the price; shifts in the same direction settle the quantity. The other variable is indeterminate.

11

Relative magnitude

★ Exam
Definition

Which way the indeterminate variable actually goes depends on which curve shifts further.

Textbook page 74.

“Indeterminate” is not a refusal to answer. It means the answer needs one more piece of information, and that piece is the relative size of the two shifts. Your textbook puts it as: a curve that shifts a disproportionately greater distance has a disproportionately greater effect.

With straight-line curves the arithmetic is neat. In the diagram above, the determinate variable moves with the sum of the two shifts and the indeterminate one with their difference — which is exactly why both panels of each pair land on the same determinate value.

Watch out. If a question gives you the relative sizes, the answer is no longer indeterminate and you should say what happens. “A large increase in demand and a small decrease in supply” has a definite answer: price up, quantity up.

Textbook

Panel (a). A big increase in demand against a small decrease in supply — the quantity rises.

Numeric

Panel (b). A small increase in demand against a big decrease in supply — the quantity falls, from 12 to 10, while the price still reaches 10.

Illustrative

Equal shifts. If the two shifts are exactly equal the indeterminate variable does not change at all. That is one outcome among three, not the normal case.

How it connects. It resolves the ambiguity in entry 9 and entry 10, and it is why two panels (entry 13) are needed to show the idea honestly. It is how a simultaneous shift (entry 8) is finally settled, and it is what would make an indeterminate answer (entry 12) determinate.

Check yourself

11.1 Demand rises a lot and supply falls a little. What happens to price and quantity?

Show answer

Price rises — that was certain anyway. Quantity rises, because the demand shift dominates. Nothing is indeterminate once you are told the sizes.

11.2 Can the indeterminate variable stay exactly the same?

Show answer

Yes, if the two shifts are exactly equal — but that is one of three possibilities, not what “indeterminate” means.

12

“Indeterminate”

★ Exam
Definition

The word released AP exams use for the variable whose direction cannot be settled. Your textbook says ambiguous; they mean the same thing.

Textbook page 74 uses “ambiguous”; released AP Microeconomics exams use “indeterminate”.

Use indeterminate in written answers. It is the word on released AP Microeconomics exams; a few questions say “cannot be determined” instead, which means the same thing.

The model sentence. “The equilibrium price rises; the change in equilibrium quantity is indeterminate without knowing the relative sizes of the two shifts.”

Learn that shape. Name the determinate variable and its direction, then name the indeterminate one. An answer that says only “it depends” has thrown away the half of the question you could have answered.

Watch out. Indeterminate is not the same as “no change”, and it is not the same as “cannot be answered”. Half the question always has a definite answer.

Exam style

The usual question. “Which of the following changes would result in an indeterminate change in the equilibrium price?” The answer is an increase in demand and an increase in supply — the same direction.

Later in the course

It comes back. In the labor market of Unit 5, if firms want more workers while more people look for work, employment certainly rises and the change in the wage is indeterminate. Same rule, with a wage on the vertical axis.

Illustrative

Two words, one idea. If a textbook says ambiguous and a question says indeterminate, nothing has changed. Answer the same way.

How it connects. It is what entry 9 and entry 10 produce, and relative magnitude (entry 11) is what would resolve it.

Check yourself

12.1 Write the model sentence for a decrease in demand together with an increase in supply.

Show answer

“The equilibrium price falls; the change in equilibrium quantity is indeterminate without knowing the relative sizes of the two shifts.”

12.2 Which change makes the equilibrium price indeterminate?

Show answer

Any change where the two curves shift in the same direction — both increasing, or both decreasing.

Showing two panels

★ Exam
Definition

To show an indeterminate outcome honestly you need two diagrams: the same two shift directions, drawn with different relative sizes.

Textbook page 74, the two panels of Figure 7.3.

One diagram can only show one outcome, so a single picture of an ambiguous case is not a proof of anything — it just shows the case you happened to draw. Two panels show both possibilities and make the word indeterminate mean something.

Your textbook’s Check Your Understanding 2 asks for exactly this: draw the market for computers with the old chip twice, once where the quantity falls and once where it rises.

Watch out. If a question asks you to show an indeterminate result and you draw one diagram, you have answered a different question. Draw both, and label which is which.

Drawing the answer, and how this differs from Module 6

★ Exam
Definition

Show a change on a labelled graph: original curves and equilibrium, new labelled curve, new equilibrium labelled on both axes.

Textbook page 76, the printed five-mark rubric.

The five marks, one each:

15

Who gains and who loses: surplus after a shift

★ ⚑
Definition

Consumer surplus is what buyers would have been willing to pay minus what they actually pay — the area below the demand curve and above the price. Producer surplus is the price minus what sellers would have accepted — the area above the supply curve and below the price. A shift changes both.

Not in this module. Your textbook defines both in Module 49 and shows how a change in price affects each on pages 486–492.

⚑ Not in Module 7 — learn it from here. Your course says a shift changes consumer surplus, producer surplus and total surplus as well as price and quantity. Module 7 mentions none of them.

The reliable rule uses the curve that did not move. When supply shifts, buyers slide along their unchanged demand curve, so consumer surplus moves opposite to the price: supply up, price down, consumer surplus up. When demand shifts, sellers slide along their unchanged supply curve, so producer surplus moves with the price: demand up, price up, producer surplus up.

The other side’s surplus depends on exactly how the shifted curve moved. On the usual exam diagram — straight lines, shifted in parallel — it moves the same way as the equilibrium quantity, and so does total surplus.

Watch out. This is not the “surplus” of Module 6. That was unsold goods at a price above equilibrium. Consumer and producer surplus are gains from trade. A market can have a shortage and still give its buyers plenty of consumer surplus.

Textbook

A Florida frost. Module 49 asks about the harm to consumers from a frost in Florida that drives up orange prices. The frost cuts the supply of oranges, the price rises along the unchanged demand curve, and consumer surplus falls.

Numeric

On the diagram. At the start, price 8 and quantity 12: consumer surplus = ½ × 12 × (14 − 8) = 36 and producer surplus = ½ × 12 × (8 − 2) = 36. After Demand ↑, price 9 and quantity 14: producer surplus = ½ × 14 × (9 − 2) = 49, and consumer surplus = ½ × 14 × (16 − 9) = 49. Both rose with the quantity.

Illustrative

Cheaper phones. A new technology cuts the cost of making phones. Supply shifts right, the price falls, and buyers gain consumer surplus: they pay less for every phone they would have bought anyway, and they buy more.

How it connects. It applies an increase in demand (entry 2) and an increase in supply (entry 4) to buyers and sellers, and how large the gain is depends on the slopes in entry 16.

Check yourself

15.1 A frost cuts the supply of strawberries. What certainly happens to consumer surplus, and why?

Show answer

It falls. Supply shifted, so buyers slide up their unchanged demand curve: they pay a higher price and buy fewer strawberries.

15.2 Demand for concert tickets rises. Which surplus certainly rises?

Show answer

Producer surplus. Demand shifted, so sellers slide up their unchanged supply curve: they get a higher price and sell more tickets.

16

How big the change is: steep and flat curves

★ ⚑
Definition

How far price and quantity move after a shift depends on the slope of the curve you slide along. If it is steep — quantity not very responsive to price — most of the shift shows up as a change in price. If it is flat — very responsive — most shows up as a change in quantity.

Not in this module. Your textbook measures this responsiveness as elasticity, starting in Module 46.

⚑ Not in Module 7 — learn it from here. Your course says the impact of a shift depends on the price elasticities of demand and supply. Module 7 never mentions elasticity.

Take the same increase in demand twice. With a steep supply curve, sellers cannot expand output easily, so the extra demand mostly bids the price up. With a flat supply curve, sellers expand output readily, so the quantity rises a lot and the price barely moves.

The directions are exactly what entry 2 says: price up, quantity up. Only the sizes differ. You will learn to measure this as elasticity later in this unit — steep means inelastic, flat means elastic.

Watch out. Slope changes the size of a change, never its direction. A demand increase raises price and quantity whatever the slopes. An answer that reverses a direction because a curve is steep is wrong.

Steep supply

Hotel rooms on a festival weekend. The number of rooms is nearly fixed. When demand rises, almost all of it shows up as higher prices.

Numeric

One shift, two supply curves. Demand shifts from P = 14 − ½Q to P = 19 − ½Q. With the steep supply curve P = 2Q − 16, the price goes from 8 to 12 and the quantity from 12 to 14. With the flat supply curve P = 6.5 + Q/8, the price goes from 8 to only 9 and the quantity from 12 to 20.

Flat supply

Pencils. More are easy to make. When demand rises, the quantity sold rises a lot and the price hardly moves.

How it connects. It sets the size of every result in entries 2 to 5 — see an increase in demand (entry 2) — and of the surplus changes in entry 15. Your textbook calls the idea elasticity.

Check yourself

16.1 Demand for beachfront hotel rooms rises in summer, and the number of rooms is nearly fixed. What changes most, price or quantity?

Show answer

The price. Supply is steep — nearly vertical — so the extra demand cannot be met with more rooms and is bid into the price.

16.2 Does a steep supply curve change the direction in which the price moves after demand rises?

Show answer

No. The price still rises and the quantity still rises. A steep curve only makes the price rise larger and the quantity rise smaller.

Tackle the test — free response

Question 1 (5 points). Draw a correctly labelled graph showing the SUV market in equilibrium. On your graph, show the effect on equilibrium price and quantity if the price of petrol increases.

Show the answer and the rubric

Petrol and SUVs are complements in consumption, so a higher petrol price makes owning an SUV less attractive: demand for SUVs shifts left. Price and quantity both fall.

The five marks, one each:

  1. Vertical axis labelled Price (or P), horizontal axis labelled Quantity (or Q)
  2. A downward-sloping demand curve and an upward-sloping supply curve, both labelled
  3. Equilibrium price and quantity found at the intersection and labelled on the axes
  4. A new, labelled demand curve shown to the left of the original
  5. The new equilibrium price and quantity found at the intersection of the original supply curve and the new demand curve, and labelled on the axes

Note mark 5: the supply curve is not redrawn. Only one curve moves.

Question 2. Draw a correctly labelled graph showing the market for cups of coffee in equilibrium. Show the effect of a decrease in the price of coffee beans on equilibrium price and quantity.

Show the answer

Beans are an input for a cup of coffee, so a cheaper input shifts supply right. The price of a cup falls and the quantity rises.

This pairs with question 1 deliberately: one is a demand-side event and one is a supply-side event, and the only way to tell them apart is to ask whether the change affects buyers or sellers.

Same five marks. Label the new supply curve S₂, and label P₂ and Q₂ on the axes.

Question 3 — the one your exam board published. Which of the following occurs if the price of oranges is below the equilibrium price? (A) There is a surplus of oranges. (B) The quantity supplied of oranges is greater than the quantity demanded. (C) The quantity supplied of oranges is greater than the quantity sold. (D) The quantity purchased of oranges is greater than the quantity sold. (E) The quantity demanded of oranges is greater than the quantity supplied.

Show the answer

(E). Below the equilibrium price buyers want more than sellers offer: a shortage. That shortage is exactly the step in the chain of entry 6 — it is what pushes the price up after demand rises or supply falls.

Why the others fail: (A) and (B) describe a surplus, which happens above equilibrium. (C) and (D) confuse the quantity sold with the quantity offered or wanted — in any market the quantity bought and the quantity sold are the same number.

The surplus that certainly moves belongs to the side whose curve did not shift. A steep curve turns a shift mostly into a price change; a flat one mostly into a quantity change.

The six things to get right from Module 7

  1. Diagnose before you draw. Did the event change what buyers want, or what sellers offer?
  2. Run the chain, do not recall the result. Shift → imbalance at the old price → price moves → movement along the other curve.
  3. Opposite directions settle the price. The same direction settles the quantity.
  4. Say “indeterminate”, and say which variable it applies to — then give the other variable’s direction, because that half is always answerable.
  5. Label the new price and quantity on the axes. Two of five marks live there.
  6. Say who gains. The side whose curve did not move: buyers when supply shifts, sellers when demand shifts.
