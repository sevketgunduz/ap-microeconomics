Krugman's three stated objectives for the module (p. 23):

  1. How trade leads to gains for an individual or an economy
  2. The difference between absolute advantage and comparative advantage
  3. How comparative advantage leads to gains from trade in the global marketplace

This is the best-aligned module in Section 1. All three objectives map onto MKT-2, every knowledge element is covered, and both required data formats — PPCs and tables — appear. The gaps are two missing vocabulary items and one claim the text shows but never states. See sections 3 and 5.

Note the skill shift. Topic 1.3 was graph-drawing (Skill 4.A); Topic 1.4 is quantitative — MKT-2.A.b says determine using data from PPCs or tables, and MKT-2.B.b says calculate mutually beneficial terms of trade. Plan for arithmetic, not just diagrams.

CED-listed resources for this topic (p. 37): Davidson Next Comparative Advantage & Trade; Classroom Resources International Economics and the AP Microeconomics Course and International Economics—The Basics of Absolute and Comparative Advantage.

The knowledge elements, in the CED's own words

Learning objective MKT-2.A (CED p. 37):

a. Define absolute advantage and comparative advantage. b. Determine (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) absolute and comparative advantage.
  • MKT-2.A.1 — Absolute advantage describes a situation in which an individual, business, or

country can produce more of a good or service than any other producer with the same quantity of resources.

  • MKT-2.A.2 — Comparative advantage describes a situation in which an individual, business, or

country can produce a good or service at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.

Learning objective MKT-2.B (CED p. 37):

a. Explain (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) how specialization according to comparative advantage with appropriate terms of trade can lead to gains from trade. b. Calculate (using data from PPCs or tables as appropriate) mutually beneficial terms of trade.
  • MKT-2.B.1 — Production specialization according to comparative advantage,

not absolute advantage, results in exchange opportunities that lead to consumption possibilities beyond the PPC.

  • MKT-2.B.2 — Comparative advantage and opportunity costs determine the terms of trade for

exchange under which mutually beneficial trade can occur.

POL-1.B.1 (CED p. 56) — Equilibria in competitive markets may be altered by the decision to open an economy to trade with other countries; equilibrium price can be higher or lower than under autarky, and the gap between domestic supply and demand is filled by trade. Opening an economy to trade affects consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total economic surplus.

Module 4 gives you the comparative-advantage half of the story — why countries trade and who exports what. It does not give you the world-price model Topic 2.9 actually tests: there is no world price line, no import/export quantity read off a domestic supply-and-demand diagram, no tariff, no quota, and no surplus analysis. Verified across the whole book — world price and autarky occur zero times, and tariff appears on six sheets, none of them a topic heading.

Say so plainly when you teach Figure 4.3: this explains the direction of trade; the welfare analysis comes in Unit 2 from a different source. See section 6.

3. Two required terms this module never names

Unlike Modules 1 and 3, nothing here is missing conceptually. Both gaps are vocabulary — Krugman teaches the idea thoroughly and simply does not attach the CED's word to it.

"Terms of trade"

The phrase does not appear anywhere in the book — zero occurrences across all 927 pages. Yet MKT-2.B.2 names it, MKT-2.B.a requires explaining specialization "with appropriate terms of trade," and MKT-2.B.b requires students to calculate mutually beneficial terms of trade.

Krugman teaches exactly this on pp. 26–27, at length and well: Hank will not give up more than 2 coconuts per fish, Tom will not give up more than 4/3 fish per coconut, so the deal must sit between the two opportunity costs. He just never labels it.

Supply the term and make the range explicit. Using Table 4.1:

Tom's opportunity costHank's opportunity costMutually beneficial terms of trade
1 fish costs3/4 coconut2 coconutsbetween 3/4 and 2 coconuts per fish
1 coconut costs4/3 fish1/2 fishbetween 1/2 and 4/3 fish per coconut

The deal in Table 4.2 — 10 fish for 10 coconuts — is 1 coconut per fish, inside the range. So is the 1 fish for 1½ coconuts in Check Your Understanding 2. The 15-coconuts-for-5-fish deal Krugman rules out is 3 coconuts per fish, outside it. Teach students to write the range first, then test any proposed deal against it. That is precisely what MKT-2.B.b asks for.

"Consumption possibilities beyond the PPC"

MKT-2.B.1 states that specialization leads to consumption possibilities beyond the PPC. Krugman's Figure 4.2 shows this, but the text and caption say only that each castaway "can consume more of both goods than either could without trade" — a weaker claim. A student can pass the module believing trade means more than before rather than outside your own production possibilities.

Prove it numerically from Figure 4.2:

PPCConsumption with tradeMax on own PPC at that fish quantityBeyond the PPC?
Tom40 fish or 30 coconuts, slope −3/430 fish, 10 coconuts30 − ¾(30) = 7.5 coconutsYes — 10 > 7.5
Hank10 fish or 20 coconuts, slope −210 fish, 10 coconuts20 − 2(10) = 0 coconutsYes — 10 > 0

Have students plot both consumption points on the PPCs and see them sit outside. It takes five minutes and it converts a vague claim into the exact statement the CED requires.

FRQ 1 is the most valuable single item in Section 1. Its 9-point rubric walks the full chain: compute opportunity cost from intercepts (Country A ¼ computer per bushel, Country B 1¼), identify absolute advantage and justify it by output, identify comparative advantage and justify it by opportunity cost, name the import, then state the minimum acceptable export price and explain why. That last part is MKT-2.B.b, and the rubric's reasoning — "Country A's opportunity cost of producing corn is ¼ computers, so that is the lowest price they will accept" — is exactly the boundary logic to drill.

Between FRQ 1 (graph) and FRQ 2 (table), this module covers both data formats the CED names. That fills the gap left by Module 3, which offers no table-based work at all.

5. Corrections and teaching points

The input/output trap — the biggest failure point in this topic

The two review items present data in opposite directions, and students who memorize a procedure rather than the definition will get one of them backwards:

ItemData formatReading
Check Your Understanding 1Italy: an automobile takes 8 workers/day; a washing machine takes 3 workers/dayInput per unit — lower is better
FRQ 2Country A: 15 bushels of wheat per worker per week; 60 units of textilesOutput per worker — higher is better

Absolute advantage flips direction between the two. Teach students to ask, before any arithmetic: "Is this table output-per-input, or input-per-output?" Then compute opportunity cost as a ratio within one producer's column and compare across producers — never compare raw numbers across producers to get comparative advantage.

Worth stating explicitly: the two are reciprocals. If Country A makes 15 bushels per worker, that is 1/15 of a worker per bushel.

Absolute advantage is not comparative advantage — the CED says so in the EK itself

MKT-2.B.1 contains the clause "according to comparative advantage, not absolute advantage" — the College Board wrote the correction into the required knowledge. This is the classic student error, and it is the one place where Krugman is stronger than the CED text:

  • p. 27 — "comparative, not absolute, advantage is the basis for mutual gain"
  • p. 27 — "as long as people have different opportunity costs, everyone has a comparative advantage

in something, and everyone has a comparative disadvantage in something"

  • Figure 4.2 caption, p. 26 — Hank "has an absolute disadvantage in both goods but a comparative

advantage in coconuts"

  • The margin definition itself ends: "Having an absolute advantage is not the same thing as having a

comparative advantage"

  • The FYI on p. 27 makes it concrete: Bangladesh is at an absolute disadvantage in almost everything

and still has a comparative advantage in clothing

Use all five. This is the module's strongest pedagogy and it is exactly what the exam targets.

Read comparative advantage off the slope

Figure 4.3 makes the point in one line: the flatter PPC belongs to the country with the comparative advantage in the good on the horizontal axis. Tie this back to Module 3, p. 19 — the slope of a straight-line PPC is the opportunity cost of the horizontal-axis good. Students who learned the slope there get comparative advantage from a graph almost for free.

One caution on the Adam Smith pin factory

The pin-factory passage (p. 23) illustrates division of labor within a firm — gains from specialization through practice and task-splitting. Comparative advantage is a different mechanism: gains from differing opportunity costs, which exist even with no learning-by-doing at all. Both are real, but only the second is what MKT-2 tests. Use the pin factory as motivation, then move to Tom and Hank for the actual model.
