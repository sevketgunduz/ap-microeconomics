    Choosing a plant: short run and long run

    The firm can build one of five plant sizes. Each has its own short-run ATC curve. Pick a plant, then change output, and compare the cost with the best plant for that output.

    Plant built:

    Illustrative industry: plant k has short-run ATC = 0.8 × m(k) × (40k/Q + 4 + 10Q/k), where m(k) = 0.7 + 0.15(k − 3)², so plant k is cheapest at Q = 2k. LRATC is the lowest short-run ATC over every possible plant.

    Returns to scale: double everything

    Scale every input up by the same factor and see what happens to output and to cost per unit. Hoang describes the outcome but never names the three cases: returns to scale is 0 times in the book.

    Illustrative firm: 1 machine ($40) and 1 worker ($20) make 10 units. Output = 10 × (scale)r, with r = 1.2, 1 or 0.8.

    Minimum efficient scale and the number of firms

    How many firms can a market hold at the lowest cost? Compare the size of the market with the minimum efficient scale.

Hoang's examples used: the Paris Métro, Silicon Valley, the 20-per-cent input example and the Common mistake on cans. The plant curves, the returns-to-scale firm and the numbers marked illustrative are invented for practice; the chip-making and craft-brewing cases go beyond Hoang and should be checked against current reporting.
    For the teacher: where Hoang needs correcting or adding to
    • Returns to scale occurs 0 times in Hoang, so increasing, constant and decreasing returns to scale (the second of Topic 3.3's four statements) are added in full.
    • Hoang's diseconomies example on p. 211, inputs up 20 per cent and output up only 10 per cent, is decreasing returns to scale; diseconomies of scale are its effect on average cost. Krugman makes the same slip on p. 562.
    • Hoang's margin note on p. 194 says long-run average cost "is equal to the average fixed cost (AFC) plus the average variable cost (AVC)". In the long run there are no fixed costs.
    • Hoang marks the minimum efficient scale at "the lowest point" of LRAC (p. 209). Where LRAC has a flat bottom, MES is the smallest output on it; the CED calls the flat part efficient scale.
    • Hoang never states the CED's fourth statement, that MES helps decide how concentrated a market is. The link is implicit in his BLADE barriers (p. 182) and natural monopoly (p. 199); the lab's MES section makes it explicit.
    • External economies of scale (Hoang pp. 210–211) are not in the AP framework; treat them as background.
