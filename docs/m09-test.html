Section A — examinable content

1. Which of the following policies is a quantity control?

  • (A) A law that apartment rents may not exceed $800 a month.
  • (B) A rule that only boats holding a license may fish for clams, with licenses issued for a fixed total catch.
  • (C) A minimum wage of $12 an hour.
  • (D) A law that petrol may not be sold for less than $3 a gallon.
  • (E) A law that taxi fares may not exceed $3 a mile.

2. In the textbook's taxi market the equilibrium is $5 a ride and 10 million rides a year. Which of the following quotas is binding?

  • (A) 14 million rides a year.
  • (B) 12 million rides a year.
  • (C) 10 million rides a year.
  • (D) 8 million rides a year.
  • (E) None — a quota binds only if fares are also regulated.

3. The equilibrium catch in a fishery is 9 million pounds a year. The government sets a quota of 11 million pounds. Which describes the result?

  • (A) No effect: the quota is above the equilibrium quantity, so 9 million pounds are still caught at the equilibrium price.
  • (B) The catch rises to 11 million pounds, because fishers are now allowed to catch more.
  • (C) The price buyers pay rises, because a quota always raises it.
  • (D) A deadweight loss equal to the value of the pounds between 9 million and 11 million.
  • (E) A quota rent equal to the equilibrium price times 2 million pounds.

4. In the textbook's taxi market, quantity demanded = 20 − 2P (millions of rides a year, P in dollars a ride). What is the demand price of 7 million rides — the fare at which riders would want exactly 7 million rides?

  • (A) $3.50
  • (B) $5.00
  • (C) $13.00
  • (D) $3.00
  • (E) $6.50

5. In the textbook's swordfish problem, quantity supplied = P − 5 (millions of pounds, P in dollars a pound), and a quota limits the catch to 7 million pounds. What is the supply price of 7 million pounds — the price at which fishers would supply exactly that amount?

  • (A) $18
  • (B) $14
  • (C) $12
  • (D) $15
  • (E) $2

6. Under the textbook's quota of 8 million taxi rides a year, what fare do riders pay, and what does a driver who rents a medallion keep per ride after paying for it?

  • (A) Riders pay $6; the driver keeps $4.
  • (B) Riders pay $5; the driver keeps $5.
  • (C) Riders pay $4; the driver keeps $6.
  • (D) Riders pay $6; the driver keeps $6.
  • (E) Riders pay $4; the driver keeps $4.

7. Under the 8-million-ride quota the fare is $6. At that fare, what is the state of the market for rides?

  • (A) A shortage of 4 million rides, as under a price ceiling.
  • (B) A surplus of 4 million rides, as under a price floor.
  • (C) A shortage of 2 million rides — the rides the quota cuts.
  • (D) Neither a shortage nor a surplus at that fare: riders want exactly the 8 million rides allowed.
  • (E) A shortage that grows over time, because the fare is not allowed to rise.

8. Under the 8-million-ride quota, riders pay $6 a ride and drivers would supply 8 million rides for $4 a ride. What is the quota rent per ride?

  • (A) $6
  • (B) $2
  • (C) $1
  • (D) $4
  • (E) $16

9. Under the swordfish quota of 7 million pounds, buyers pay $18 a pound and fishers would supply 7 million pounds for $12 a pound. The equilibrium without the quota is $14 and 9 million pounds. What is the total quota rent?

  • (A) $6 million
  • (B) $12 million
  • (C) $54 million
  • (D) $126 million
  • (E) $42 million

10. The city cuts the taxi quota from 8 million to 6 million rides a year. At 6 million rides the demand price is $7 and the supply price $3. What happens to the quota rent?

  • (A) It falls, per ride and in total, because fewer rides are sold.
  • (B) It rises per ride, to $4, but falls in total, because fewer rides are sold.
  • (C) It rises from $2 to $4 a ride, and in total from $16 million to $24 million a year.
  • (D) It stays at $2 a ride, because neither curve has moved.
  • (E) It rises to $3 a ride — the $7 fare less the old $4 — and in total to $18 million.

11. Under the 8-million-ride quota (equilibrium 10 million rides at $5; demand price $6 and supply price $4 at the quota), what is the deadweight loss?

  • (A) $2 million a year
  • (B) $4 million a year
  • (C) $16 million a year
  • (D) $1 million a year
  • (E) $18 million a year

  • (C) The horizontal distance between 8 million and 10 million rides at $5.
  • (D) The rectangle between $4 and $6 together with the triangle to its right.
  • (E) The triangle between the demand and supply curves from 8 million to 10 million rides, with its point at the equilibrium.

13. Under the swordfish quota of 7 million pounds (equilibrium $14 and 9 million pounds; demand price $18 and supply price $12 at the quota), what is the deadweight loss?

  • (D) $4 million

14. Under the taxi quota, a rider would pay $6 for one more ride and a driver would supply it for $4. What does this comparison show?

  • (A) The market is allocatively efficient, because both prices are above zero.
  • (B) Too many rides are being taken, so the quota should be tightened.
  • (C) The quota rent is a deadweight loss.
  • (D) Fewer rides are taken than the allocatively efficient quantity: another ride is worth more to a rider than it costs to supply.
  • (E) Drivers are underpaid, so the market will move to $6 for both.

15. A city's taxi market is competitive and produces the efficient quantity before any intervention. Which statement about a binding quota is correct?

  • (A) It raises allocative efficiency if it is set close to the equilibrium quantity.

  • (C) It can only decrease allocative efficiency.
  • (D) It raises allocative efficiency, because it prevents a shortage.
  • (E) Its effect on allocative efficiency depends on who owns the licenses.

16. Under the 8-million-ride quota the fare rises from $5 to $6 and rides fall from 10 million to 8 million. By how much does riders' consumer surplus fall?

  • (A) $9 million a year
  • (B) $8 million a year
  • (C) $2 million a year
  • (D) $16 million a year
  • (E) $1 million a year

17. Which is the correct accounting of the 8-million-ride quota, per year?

  • (A) Riders lose $16 million, drivers lose nothing, license holders gain $16 million; total surplus is unchanged.
  • (B) Riders lose $9 million and drivers gain $9 million; total surplus is unchanged.
  • (C) Riders and drivers each lose $1 million, and license holders lose $16 million.
  • (D) Riders lose $9 million and drivers lose $9 million, so $18 million is lost to the economy.
  • (E) Riders lose $9 million, drivers lose $9 million and license holders gain $16 million, so total surplus falls by $2 million — the deadweight loss.

18. New York could hand the 8 million rides' worth of medallions to existing owners free, or it could auction them each year to the highest bidders. Compared with giving them away, auctioning them would…

  • (A) lower the fare, because drivers would no longer pay rent to medallion owners.
  • (B) move the $16 million of quota rent to the city, leaving the fare, the number of rides and the deadweight loss unchanged.
  • (C) remove the deadweight loss, because the government collects the rent.
  • (D) raise the fare above $6, because drivers must now pay the city as well.
  • (E) make the quota non-binding.

19. In the textbook's taxi market, compare a $4 price ceiling with a quota of 8 million rides.

  • (A) The ceiling cuts rides to 8 million; the quota leaves 10 million rides.
  • (B) Both create a shortage of 4 million rides.
  • (C) The ceiling has no deadweight loss, because riders pay less.
  • (D) Both cut rides to 8 million with the same $2 million deadweight loss, but under the ceiling riders pay $4 and face a shortage of 4 million, while under the quota they pay $6 and there is no shortage.
  • (E) The quota's deadweight loss is $18 million, because the quota rent is lost as well.

20. With the 8-million-ride quota in place, tourism collapses and demand falls by 4 million rides at every fare, so quantity demanded = 16 − 2P. What happens?

  • (A) The quota rent rises, because riders are scarcer.
  • (B) A surplus of 4 million rides appears.
  • (C) The quota stops binding: the new equilibrium is $4 and 8 million rides, exactly the quota.
  • (D) The fare stays at $6, because the quota fixes it.
  • (E) The deadweight loss rises to $4 million.

21. Instead, demand rises by 2 million rides at every fare, so quantity demanded = 22 − 2P, with the 8-million quota unchanged. Which is correct?

  • (A) The quota stops binding.
  • (B) The quota rent stays at $2 a ride, because the quota has not changed.
  • (C) The fare stays at $6 and the deadweight loss at $2 million.
  • (D) The quota rent rises to $3 a ride, but the deadweight loss stays at $2 million because the quota has not changed.
  • (E) The quota rent rises to $3 a ride and the deadweight loss to $4.5 million, because the market would now choose 11 million rides.

Solutions

Question 1 — Quantity control, or quota · Application

Answer: (B)

A quantity control limits how much can be bought or sold, usually through licenses (Krugman, p. 88). The clam rule fixes the total catch and enforces it with licenses — the textbook's own FYI example. The others all fix a price.

  • (A) ✗ A legal maximum rent is a price ceiling.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ A minimum wage is a price floor on labor.
  • (D) ✗ A legal minimum price is a price floor.
  • (E) ✗ The trap: it is about taxis, but it limits the fare, so it is a price ceiling.

Question 2 — Binding and non-binding quotas · Application

Answer: (D)

A quota binds only below the equilibrium quantity. Of the five, only 8 million rides is below 10 million — Krugman's own quota.

  • (A) ✗ Above the equilibrium quantity; it constrains nobody.
  • (B) ✗ The textbook's own example of a non-binding quota (p. 91).
  • (C) ✗ At the equilibrium quantity the market already chooses that amount.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ A quota binds by itself; the fare adjusts to it.

Question 3 — Binding and non-binding quotas · Analysis

Answer: (A)

A quota is a limit, not a target. Set above the 9-million equilibrium, it forbids nothing anyone wanted to do.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Permission to catch more is not a reason to catch more; at prices above equilibrium buyers will not take it.
  • (C) ✗ Only a binding quota raises the price buyers pay.
  • (D) ✗ No trades are prevented, so there is no deadweight loss.
  • (E) ✗ A license earns rent only when the quota binds.

Question 4 — Demand price · Application

Answer: (E)

Set quantity demanded equal to 7: 20 − 2P = 7, so P = 13/2 = $6.50 — the value in Krugman's schedule (p. 89).

  • (A) ✗ $3.50 is the supply price of 7 million rides (7/2).
  • (B) ✗ The equilibrium fare; the demand price of a smaller quantity is higher.
  • (C) ✗ Stops at 20 − 7 = 13 and forgets to divide by 2.
  • (D) ✗ The gap between the demand price and the supply price of 7 million rides.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 5 — Supply price · Application

Answer: (C)

Set quantity supplied equal to 7: P − 5 = 7, so P = $12.

  • (A) ✗ $18 is the demand price of 7 million pounds, read off the other curve.
  • (B) ✗ The equilibrium price, at 9 million pounds.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ Splits the difference between $18 and $12; the supply price is a point on the supply curve.
  • (E) ✗ Computes 7 − 5 instead of solving P − 5 = 7.

Question 6 — Price and quantity under a binding quota · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Riders are on the demand curve at 8 million rides: $6. Drivers are on the supply curve: $4 is what they need. The $2 between goes to the medallion owner (pp. 90–91).

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ The equilibrium, which a binding quota moves away from.
  • (C) ✗ Reversed: buyers pay the higher price.
  • (D) ✗ A driver who rents a medallion pays $2 of the fare for it.
  • (E) ✗ Reads the supply curve for both sides.

Question 7 — Price and quantity under a binding quota · Analysis

Answer: (D)

At $6 quantity demanded is 20 − 12 = 8 million — exactly the quota. The fare has risen to the point where riders want no more rides than are allowed, so the market clears.

  • (A) ✗ 4 million is the shortage a $4 ceiling would create (12 − 8); a quota lets the fare rise.
  • (B) ✗ 4 million is the surplus a $6 floor would create; under the quota drivers receive $4, not $6.
  • (C) ✗ The 2 million rides cut by the quota are lost trades, not unmet demand at $6.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The fare is free to rise; it is the quantity that is fixed.

Question 8 — Quota rent · Application

Answer: (B)

Quota rent per ride = demand price − supply price = $6 − $4 = $2 (Krugman, p. 91).

  • (A) ✗ The whole fare; the driver's $4 has to come out of it.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ $6 − $5: compares the fare with the old equilibrium instead of with the supply price.
  • (D) ✗ What the driver keeps, not what the license earns.
  • (E) ✗ $16 million is the rent for the whole market; per ride it is $2.

Question 9 — Quota rent · Application

Answer: (E)

Rent per pound = $18 − $12 = $6; on the 7 million pounds caught, $6 × 7 million = $42 million.

  • (A) ✗ $6 million is the deadweight loss (question 13).
  • (B) ✗ Multiplies the rent by the 2 million pounds the quota cuts, not by the 7 million caught.
  • (C) ✗ Multiplies by the equilibrium quantity, 9 million pounds, which is no longer caught.
  • (D) ✗ $18 × 7 million is what buyers spend, not the rent.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 10 — Quota rent · Synthesis

Answer: (C)

At 6 million rides the rent is $7 − $3 = $4 a ride, and $4 × 6 million = $24 million against $2 × 8 million = $16 million. A tighter quota raises the rent per ride by more than it cuts the number of rides — Krugman's CYU 1.

  • (A) ✗ The rent per ride rises: buyers pay more and sellers need less.
  • (B) ✗ True about the rent per ride, false about the total: $24 million is more than $16 million.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The curves have not moved, but the quota has moved along them.
  • (E) ✗ Keeps the old $4 supply price; at 6 million rides drivers need only $3.

Question 11 — Deadweight loss · Application

Answer: (A)

½ × (10 − 8) million rides × ($6 − $4) = $2 million a year — the triangle in Figure 9.2.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Forgets the ½: base times height is a rectangle.
  • (C) ✗ The quota rent, which license holders receive.
  • (D) ✗ Uses $1, the rise in the fare, as the height; the height is the whole $2 gap.
  • (E) ✗ Adds the rent to the triangle; the rent is a transfer, not a loss.

Question 12 — Deadweight loss · Analysis

Answer: (E)

The lost trades are the rides between 8 and 10 million, each worth more to a rider than it costs a driver. Their lost value is the triangle between the curves (pp. 90, 92).

  • (A) ✗ That rectangle is the quota rent, received by license holders.
  • (B) ✗ That is the total value of the rides still taken.
  • (C) ✗ A distance — the lost quantity — not an area of lost value.
  • (D) ✗ Counts the rent as a loss as well.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 13 — Deadweight loss · Application

Answer: (B)

½ × (9 − 7) million pounds × ($18 − $12) = $6 million.

  • (A) ✗ Forgets the ½.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The total quota rent (question 9).
  • (D) ✗ Uses $18 − $14 as the height — the buyers' half of the gap only.
  • (E) ✗ The 2 million pounds lost is a quantity, not a value.

Question 14 — Allocative efficiency · Analysis

Answer: (D)

The efficient quantity is where the last unit is worth exactly what it costs. A $6 value against a $4 cost means the market has stopped short of it: too few rides.

  • (A) ✗ Efficiency is about value against cost at the margin, not whether prices are positive.
  • (B) ✗ Reversed: the gap says another ride is worth taking.
  • (C) ✗ The rent is a transfer; the comparison is about the rides not taken.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ The quota holds the two prices apart; the market cannot close the gap while it binds.

Question 15 — Allocative efficiency · Analysis

Answer: (C)

The framework's rule: intervention through quantity controls in a market producing the efficient quantity can only decrease allocative efficiency.

  • (A) ✗ Close to the equilibrium the loss is small, but it is still a loss.
  • (B) ✗ The rent stays in the market, but the trades the quota forbids are lost.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ There was no shortage to prevent; the market was in equilibrium.
  • (E) ✗ Ownership decides who gets the rent, not how many rides are lost.

Question 16 — Surplus under a quota · Analysis

Answer: (A)

Riders pay $1 more on each of the 8 million rides still taken, $8 million, and lose the 2 million rides no longer taken, worth ½ × 2 million × $1 = $1 million. In total $9 million.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ Counts the higher fare and forgets the lost rides.
  • (C) ✗ The whole deadweight loss, of which riders bear only half.
  • (D) ✗ The whole quota rent, half of which comes from drivers.
  • (E) ✗ Counts only the riders' share of the triangle.

Question 17 — Surplus under a quota · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

Riders lose $9 million (question 16) and drivers lose the same — $1 less on each of 8 million rides, plus $1 million on rides no longer given. License holders gain $16 million. Total surplus falls by $9 + $9 − $16 = $2 million, the deadweight loss of question 11.

  • (A) ✗ Drivers lose too: they keep $4 instead of $5.
  • (B) ✗ Drivers do not gain; the rent goes to whoever holds the medallions.
  • (C) ✗ License holders gain the rent; they do not lose it.
  • (D) ✗ Forgets that $16 million of the $18 million is received by license holders.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 18 — Surplus under a quota · Synthesis

Answer: (B)

The fare and the number of rides are set by the quota and the two curves; who holds the licenses cannot change them. Auctioning only changes who collects the $2 a ride. This is the logic of the one released AP item on quotas, 2024 Q23: a tariff raises government revenue and a quota, unless its licenses are sold, does not.

  • (A) ✗ Drivers still pay for the right to drive — to the city instead of to an owner.
  • (B) ✓ Correct.
  • (C) ✗ The rides the quota forbids are still forbidden.
  • (D) ✗ The auction price is the rent; it comes out of the $6, it does not add to it.
  • (E) ✗ The number of licenses, not their owner, decides whether the quota binds.

Question 19 — Quotas compared with price controls · Synthesis

Answer: (D)

At $4 drivers offer 2 × 4 = 8 million rides and riders want 20 − 8 = 12 million: a shortage of 4 million, and 8 million rides taken. The quota also allows 8 million, at a $6 fare that clears the market. Same lost rides, same triangle, $2 million.

  • (A) ✗ The quota cuts rides to 8 million too.
  • (B) ✗ The quota leaves no shortage at the fare riders pay.
  • (C) ✗ The ceiling loses the same 2 million rides; who pays less is a transfer.
  • (D) ✓ Correct.
  • (E) ✗ Counts the quota rent as a loss.

Question 20 — When demand or supply shifts · Analysis

Answer: (C)

16 − 2P = 2P gives P = $4 and Q = 8 million: the market now chooses the quota quantity by itself — Krugman's CYU 2 (Solutions p. S-7).

  • (A) ✗ The rent falls — to zero — as the demand price at 8 million falls to the supply price.
  • (B) ✗ A quota cannot create a surplus; at $4 riders want exactly 8 million rides.
  • (C) ✓ Correct.
  • (D) ✗ The quota fixes the quantity, not the fare.
  • (E) ✗ No trades are now prevented, so the deadweight loss is zero.

Question 21 — When demand or supply shifts · Synthesis

Answer: (E)

The demand price of 8 million rides is now (22 − 8)/2 = $7; the supply price is still $4, so the rent is $3. The market would choose 22 − 2P = 2P, or 11 million rides at $5.50, so the triangle is ½ × 3 million × $3 = $4.5 million.

  • (A) ✗ The equilibrium quantity moves further above the quota, so it binds harder.
  • (B) ✗ The rent depends on the demand price at the quota, which has risen.
  • (C) ✗ The fare rises to $7.
  • (D) ✗ The rent is right; the loss is not, because more trades are now forbidden.
  • (E) ✓ Correct.

Question 22 — License and the price of a license · Analysis · background

Answer: (A)

A medallion earns the quota rent every year it is held; its price reflects that stream (pp. 88, 91). As New York grew and the number of medallions stayed fixed, the rent grew with it.

  • (A) ✓ Correct.
  • (B) ✗ A medallion costs almost nothing to make; its value is the right it gives.
  • (C) ✗ Medallions traded between owners at market prices.
  • (D) ✗ Higher costs would lower the rent, not raise it.
  • (E) ✗ More medallions lower the rent; owners objected to the 2004 sale for that reason (p. 93).

Question 23 — The wedge · Analysis · background

Answer: (D)

Krugman (p. 91): a quota "drives a wedge between the demand price and the supply price of a good" — what buyers pay against what sellers receive.

  • (A) ✗ At the equilibrium price those two are equal.

Notes for the teacher

Content validity. All 16 concept entries appear across the 25 items. Module 9 is well aligned, so the weighting follows the module: the definition and the binding condition (1–3), reading the diagram (4–7), the rent and the loss (8–13), and the two supplied entries (14–18), which carry five items between them because the framework asks for them and the book does not. Every figure comes from Krugman's two linear schedules.

Bloom levels. Application 8 · Analysis 12 · Synthesis 5, a ratio of 1 : 1.5 : 0.62 against the standard's 1 : 3 : 1.75. Reading a demand price, a supply price, a rent and a triangle are genuine application items, and LO POL-1.A(c) asks for them; the five synthesis items each combine two results — a rent per unit with a total (10), the full surplus accounting (17), ownership against efficiency (18), two controls on one market (19) and a shift under a fixed quota (21).

True premise, false conclusion. 10B starts from something true — the rent per ride rises — and concludes that the total falls; 21D gets the new rent right and then holds the deadweight loss fixed because the quota has not moved.

Distractor design. Every wrong option encodes a documented error. The recurring ones: reading the wrong curve (4A, 5A, 6C, 6E); answering with the equilibrium price (4B, 5B, 6B); counting the quota rent as a loss (11C, 11E, 12A, 12D, 17D, 19E); dropping the ½ (11B, 13A); carrying a price-control result across to a quota (7A, 7B, 20B, 24A); and confusing a per-unit figure with a total (8E, 9B, 9C).

Answer distribution. A 5 · B 5 · C 5 · D 5 · E 5. No two consecutive items share a key: the longest run is one.

Timing. 50 minutes for 25 items; twelve need arithmetic, and none more than four operations.
