If you have taught the Macroeconomics version of this module, throw the tiering away. A Macro edition of these notes exists at AP Macroeconomics/module_01_teaching_notes.md and it tells you that five of the seventeen terms are examinable and the rest is background. That is correct for that course and badly wrong for this one. Twelve of the seventeen are examinable here, and the module carries four topics rather than one and a half. Section 5 lists the reversals one by one.

The two Enduring Understandings this module sits under, quoted verbatim:

MKT-1 (CED p. 34) — "Most resources are scarce, and in most cases the use of resources involves constraints and trade-offs."
CBA-1 (CED p. 38) — "Rational economic decisions require the evaluation of costs and benefits."

Krugman's five stated objectives for the module (p. 2):

  1. How scarcity and choice are central to the study of economics
  2. The importance of opportunity cost in individual choice and decision making
  3. The difference between positive economics and normative economics
  4. When economists agree and why they sometimes disagree
  5. What makes macroeconomics different from microeconomics

Objectives 1 and 2 are examinable and carry most of the module. Objectives 3 and 4 are not — normative occurs 0 times in the Micro CED. Objective 5 has no essential knowledge statement either, but it is worth five minutes because it tells students which half of the subject they are in.

Verdict on alignment. This is a strong module for this course and you should teach it properly. Scarcity, resources, individual choice and opportunity cost land squarely on Topics 1.1 and 1.5. Market and command economies land on Topic 1.2. Marginal benefit, marginal cost and marginal analysis are the opening of Topics 1.5 and 1.6. There are exactly two real gaps, and both are in Topic 1.2: the three basic questions of MKT-1.B.1 are never posed, and the mixed economy named by MKT-1.B.2 is never defined. Section 3 documents both with counts. Everything else the module owes Unit 1, it delivers.

The requirements, in the CED's own words

MKT-1.A.1 — "Economic trade-offs arise from the lack of sufficient resources (scarcity) to meet society's wants and needs." (CED p. 34)
MKT-1.A.2 — "Most factors of production (such as land, labor, and capital) are scarce, but some factors of production (such as established knowledge) may not be scarce due to their non-rival nature." (CED p. 34)
MKT-1.B.1 — "Resource allocation involves answering three basic questions: What goods and services to produce? How to produce those goods and services? And who consumes those goods and services?" (CED p. 35)
MKT-1.B.2 — "Resource allocation is significantly influenced by the economic system adopted by society, such as command economy, market economy, or mixed economy. Each system involves a particular set of institutional arrangements and a coordinating mechanism for allocating scarce resources and distributing output." (CED p. 35)
CBA-1.A.1 — "Rational agents consider opportunity costs, whether implicit or explicit, when calculating the total economic costs of any decision." (CED p. 38)
CBA-1.B.2 — "Some decisions permit rational agents to look at only marginal benefit and marginal cost. Other decisions cannot be broken down into increments in this way and must be evaluated by looking at total benefits and total costs." (CED p. 38)
CBA-2 (Enduring Understanding) — "To determine the optimal level at which to pursue an activity whose total benefits exceed total cost, rational economic agents compare marginal benefits and marginal costs." (CED p. 39)

Four things to notice in that wording.

"Most factors of production … are scarce" — not all. MKT-1.A.2 spends half its length on the exception and names it: established knowledge, which is non-rival. Krugman never makes the point; the hyphenated phrase non-rival occurs 0 times in the book, and the unhyphenated nonrival occurs 35 times — all of them 720 pages away in Module 76 on public goods. Say it here in one sentence: some resources are not scarce, because using them does not use them up.

The framework names three factors, not four. MKT-1.A.2 lists land, labor and capital. Entrepreneurship occurs 0 times in the Micro CED; the word entrepreneur occurs twice, both in Topic 3.4 on implicit cost, where CBA-2.C.2 mentions "an entrepreneur's time". Krugman teaches four and its own FRQ rubric awards a point for the fourth. Teach all four — every prep book does, and it is correct economics — but the three the framework names are the three to be certain of.

Opportunity cost is a calculation from the start. LO CBA-1.A has three verbs and (c) is calculate. CBA-1.A.1 names implicit as well as explicit cost, which is where marks are lost. Module 1 is the right place to drill this; the PPC version arrives in Module 3.

Marginal analysis is Topic 1.6, not a preamble. EU CBA-2 is one sentence and it is marginal analysis. Module 1 introduces it four hundred pages before Module 51 develops it into utility maximisation. Teach it here as the general rule — keep going while MB exceeds MC — and say that the consumer-choice machinery comes later.

3. Gaps and background

3a. Required elements this module does not contain

Two, both in Topic 1.2, both verified by full-text search.

MKT-1.B.1 — the three basic questions are never asked. Searching Krugman pp. 2–9 for "what goods", "how to produce", "who consumes" and "three basic questions" returns 0 occurrences each. Krugman defines an economy as a coordinating system and moves to the market/command contrast without ever setting out the three questions the framework requires. This is a one-slide fix, but it has to be supplied: What is produced? How is it produced? Who gets it?

MKT-1.B.2 — mixed economy is never defined. The phrase occurs 0 times in the whole 927-page book. The framework names all three systems in the same sentence; Krugman defines two. Supply the third, and supply it as the region between the two extremes rather than as a third box, because that is what makes it true of essentially every real economy.

Neither gap is Krugman's fault — the book predates the 2022 framework — but both are examinable and both are invisible if you teach straight from the text.

Three of these deserve more than a line.

Economy is background, but what sits under it is not. Krugman's definition — "a system for coordinating a society's productive activities" — is not something the CED asks for. The three questions that system has to answer are (MKT-1.B.1), and they are missing from the book. Do not skip the entry because the term is unassessed; teach the questions and let the term carry them.

Economic aggregates earn their five minutes by contrast. Aggregate occurs 0 times in the Micro CED against 177 in the Macro one. That is the cleanest statement available of what this course does not do: microeconomics is the study of the individual prices and quantities that an aggregate averages away. Teach it as the boundary, not as content.

Models are not examinable content and they are the whole method. Skill 4.A — "Draw an accurately labeled graph or visual to represent an economic model or market" — is the suggested skill for Topic 1.3 and recurs across Units 2, 3, 4 and 6. A student who expects a model to be realistic will argue with the supply-and-demand diagram for a term. Two minutes here prevents it.

5. Corrections to make explicitly in class

1. Say "most resources are scarce", not "all". MKT-1.A.2 says most, and spends the rest of the statement on the exception. Krugman does not make the point anywhere in the module. This is the half-sentence a student needs in order to answer a question about non-rival resources.

2. The framework names three factors of production; the book names four. Teach all four. Do not let a student believe the fourth earns a mark on an AP Microeconomics paper, and do not let one believe entrepreneurship is wrong — it is simply not in the list the CED wrote.

3. Money is not capital. Krugman is clear and students are not. Capital is manufactured goods used to produce other goods. The sentence "we need capital to expand" is ordinary English, not economics.

4. Opportunity cost is the single best alternative, and it includes implicit cost. Two errors, one term: summing every forgone option instead of taking the best, and counting only cash. CBA-1.A.1 names implicit cost explicitly. Both errors survive into Unit 3, where implicit cost becomes the difference between accounting and economic profit.

5. Supply the mixed economy, and supply the three basic questions. Section 3a. These are the module's only genuine holes, and both are cheap to fill.

6. "Marginal" means one more, always. It will mean exactly that in marginal utility (1.6), marginal product (3.1), marginal cost (3.2), marginal revenue (3.5), marginal revenue product (5.1) and marginal social cost (6.1). Marginal benefit occurs 16 times in the Micro CED body and marginal cost 58. Establishing the word here saves six later conversations.

If you also teach AP Macroeconomics, corrections 2, 5 and 6 all run the other way there. Mixed economy, market economy and command economy each occur 0 times in the Macro framework, and every occurrence of marginal in it is the marginal propensity to consume or save. Keep the two courses' tests apart.

Nine of the seventeen glossary terms cannot be taught in any order you like. Each row means the left term has to be in place before the right one makes sense.

Rows 1–7 all fall inside the taught time. Rows 8 and 9 begin inside it and finish in the background reading.

Teach as a pair, never singly. Each of these terms is defined by its contrast, and a student who meets one without the other will blur them in Unit 2:

  • Economics / economy — the study against the thing being studied
  • Opportunity cost / marginal cost — the whole sacrifice against the cost of one more
  • Explicit / implicit cost — cash out against value forgone; both are opportunity cost
  • Market / command / mixed economy — a triad, and the outer two are limiting cases of the middle

one. All three are named by MKT-1.B.2; only two are in the book

  • Incentive / information — changing a payoff against changing what someone knows
