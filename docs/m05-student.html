Demand

Twenty terms: the fifteen in Module 5 of Krugman’s Economics for AP, plus five your course requires that the module leaves out. They are in the order that makes them easiest to learn. This is a short topic with one hard idea in it. Almost everything you will get wrong on an exam question about demand comes from that one idea, so this guide spends most of its length on it.

Textbook pages 48–58·20 terms· Topic 2.1·1 idea that decides most of the marks

How to use this guide

Read Module 5 in your textbook first, then work through the entries here in order. They are not alphabetical — each one uses the one before it.

  • ★ Exam means the AP Microeconomics framework can test it. Every entry in this guide carries it.
  • ★ ⚑ means the framework can test it and Module 5 of your textbook does not teach it. Five entries carry the flag. Learn them from here.
  • Every entry ends with two Check yourself questions. Answer them before opening the answer, then tick Got it — the bar at the top tracks you.
  • The widgets are not decoration. The sorter in the middle is the single most useful thing on this page; do it until you get twelve out of twelve twice.

The one idea. Demand and quantity demanded are different things, and the exam is built on the difference. A change in the good’s own price moves you along the curve. A change in anything else shifts the whole curve. Three of the five multiple-choice questions at the end of your textbook’s module turn on it.

⚑ Your course asks more of this topic than Module 5 gives. AP Microeconomics wants you to explain why the demand curve slopes downward, and it opens the topic with property rights, incentives and constraints. None of that is in these eleven pages. Entries 3, 4, 9, 10 and 11 fill the gap. Two of them you have met before, in Modules 1 and 51; the substitution and income effects are new, and your textbook does not reach them until Module 46.

The 20 terms

01

Competitive market

★ Exam
Definition

A market in which there are many buyers and sellers of the same good or service, none of whom can influence the price at which it is sold.

Textbook page 48.

Everything in this module and the next three assumes this. The supply and demand model describes a competitive market; it does not describe every market, and your textbook is careful to say so.

The test is not “are there lots of firms?” but can any single participant move the price? If one seller can raise the price by holding back output, the market is not competitive and this model will give you the wrong answer.

Watch out. “Competitive” in economics does not mean “cut-throat” or “lots of advertising”. Coca-Cola and Pepsi compete ferociously and the cola market is not competitive in this sense, because those two firms are big enough to set the price.

Textbook

Coffee beans. The world market is so large that even Starbucks buys a tiny fraction of it and cannot move the price. Competitive.

Counter-example

Cola. Coca-Cola and Pepsi account for so much of total sales that they can influence the price. Not competitive.

Illustrative

Wheat against jet engines. Thousands of wheat farmers, none of whom sets the price. Two or three makers of large jet engines, each of whom does.

How it connects. This is the condition under which the whole supply and demand model (entry 2) works. Your framework’s statement of how a market reaches equilibrium, two topics from now, begins with the words “in a perfectly competitive market”. It rests on property rights (entry 3): nobody can buy what nobody owns.

Check yourself

1.1 A town has one petrol station. Is that market competitive?

Show answer

No. With one seller, that seller can set the price. The number of buyers does not rescue it — you need many on both sides.

1.2 Why does it matter whether the market is competitive before you draw a demand curve?

Show answer

Because the model predicts that price is settled by demand and supply together. If one participant can choose the price instead, the prediction is wrong, and the diagram would be telling you something false.

02

The supply and demand model

★ Exam
Definition

A model of how a competitive market works. It has five elements.

Textbook page 48.

Learn the five now, because they are the map of the next four modules and you will know exactly where you are at every point:

  1. The demand curve — this module
  2. The supply curve — Module 6
  3. The things that shift each of them — this module for demand, Module 6 for supply
  4. The market equilibrium, meaning the equilibrium price and quantity — Module 6
  5. How the equilibrium changes when a curve shifts — Module 7

You are being taught one half of one element. That is worth knowing: it stops the shift factors feeling like the point of the course, and it tells you that item 3 is a listed element in its own right — not an afterthought.

Watch out. A model is a deliberate simplification, not a claim about the real world being simple. Everything here holds all other things being equal, and the whole of this module’s difficulty comes from what happens when they are not.

Textbook

Coffee beans, 2002 to 2006. Prices rose and consumption rose. That looks like it breaks the model. It does not, and element 3 is why.

Structural

Where you are. Element 1 and half of element 3. Modules 6 and 7 finish the other three and a half.

Illustrative

Later in this course. The same structure comes back for the labor market and every other factor market in Unit 5, and for the markets that go wrong in Unit 6. It is worth the investment.

How it connects. It only applies in a competitive market (entry 1). Element 1 is the demand curve (entry 7); element 3 is entries 14 to 18. In practice the demand curve in the model is the market demand curve (entry 20).

Check yourself

2.1 Which of the five elements does this module cover?

Show answer

Element 1, the demand curve, and the demand half of element 3, the things that shift it. Supply, equilibrium and changes in equilibrium are Modules 6 and 7.

2.2 Coffee prices rose between 2002 and 2006 and people bought more coffee. Does that disprove the law of demand?

Show answer

No. The law holds all other things being equal, and other things changed — population grew and lattes became popular. The whole curve moved. Element 3 is exactly this case.

03

Property rights

★ ⚑
Definition

Property rights establish ownership and grant individuals the right to trade goods and services with each other.

Not in this module. Your textbook defines it in Module 1, page 3.

⚑ Not in Module 5 — learn it from here. Your course opens the demand topic with it: a market system only works well when property rights are well defined. Module 5 never mentions them.

A demand curve records what buyers will pay to get something. That only means anything if paying gets you the thing — if what you buy becomes yours and nobody can take it from you. Property rights are the reason a price is worth paying at all.

They are also why sellers turn up. Your textbook’s point in Module 1 is that ownership creates incentives: the right to keep or sell what you make is the reason to make it.

Watch out. “Property” does not mean only houses and land. It covers anything you can own and trade — a bag of coffee, a concert ticket, a song licence. And the word well-defined matters: where nobody owns something, like fish in the open sea, there is no price for it and too much of it gets used. You will meet that problem again in Unit 6.

Textbook

A lake. Your textbook’s own example (Module 1): owners of a lake have a reason not to pollute it when its use for recreation or sale is worth more. Ownership turns a value into an incentive.

Illustrative

Concert tickets. If a ticket is yours to resell, you will pay more for it than for one that is void the moment you cannot go. Stronger rights, higher willingness to pay.

Counter-example

Ocean fish. Nobody owns the fish until they are caught, so nobody can charge for leaving them in the sea. No well-defined property right, no market price for the stock — and overfishing.

How it connects. Without it there is no competitive market (entry 1) to describe, and it is where many of the incentives of entry 4 come from: a price only motivates you if paying it gets you something that is yours.

Check yourself

3.1 A town lets anyone use its tennis courts free, first come, first served. Why is there no price and no demand curve for court time?

Show answer

Nobody holds a right to a court slot that they could sell, so nothing is traded and no price forms. Queues ration the courts instead. Give each slot an owner who can sell it and a price — and a demand curve — appear.

3.2 Why does your framework say property rights must be well defined for a market to work well?

Show answer

Buyers will only pay for ownership they can rely on, and sellers will only produce what they are allowed to keep or sell. If goods can be taken or their ownership is unclear, willingness to pay collapses and trades that would benefit both sides never happen.

04

Incentives and constraints

★ ⚑
Definition

An incentive is a reward or punishment that motivates a particular choice. A constraint is a limit on the choices you can make — your income, your time, and the law.

Not in this module. Incentives are defined in your textbook’s Module 1, page 2; the word constraint does not appear until Module 51.

⚑ Not in Module 5 — learn it from here. Your course states two facts before it states the law of demand: people respond to incentives, and they face constraints. Module 5 never uses either word.

A price is an incentive. A higher price is a reason to buy less; a lower price is a reason to buy more. The law of demand (entry 8) is this idea, measured.

Constraints limit how far you can respond. Your framework names three: income (you cannot spend what you do not have), time (a cheap flight is no use if you cannot take the day off), and legal and regulatory frameworks (you cannot buy what the law will not let you buy). This is what “willing and able” means: the incentive decides whether you are willing; the constraints decide whether you are able.

Watch out. Income appears twice in this module in two different roles. Here it is a constraint on what you can buy. In entry 16 a change in income is a determinant that shifts the demand curve. They are one fact seen twice: loosen the constraint and more is bought at every price.

Textbook

London’s congestion charge. Your textbook, page 56: charging drivers £8 to enter central London cut traffic. A price used deliberately as an incentive — the story is told without ever using the word.

Numeric

Time as a constraint. A train ticket costs $40 and takes 5 hours; a flight costs $120 and takes 1 hour. The flight costs $80 more and saves 4 hours, so for anyone whose time is worth more than $20 an hour it is the cheaper trip.

Illustrative

The law as a constraint. A 15-year-old who would happily pay to rent a car cannot rent one. Willing, but not able — so they are not on the demand curve at any price.

How it connects. Prices as incentives are why the law of demand (entry 8) holds; the constraints are why quantity demanded (entry 6) means willing and able; and a loosened income constraint is the normal-good shift of entry 16. Many incentives come from property rights (entry 3).

Check yourself

4.1 A school canteen doubles the price of fizzy drinks and sales halve. Is this mainly an incentive or a constraint at work?

Show answer

Mainly an incentive. The higher price is a reason to buy fewer drinks — your framework’s own example of an incentive is “those presented by prices”. Nobody’s income, time or legal position changed.

4.2 Name the three constraints your framework lists, with an example of each.

Show answer

Income — you cannot buy a $900 phone with $300. Time — a free concert is no use if it clashes with an exam. Legal and regulatory frameworks — a minimum age for buying some goods keeps willing buyers out of the market.

05

Demand schedule

★ Exam
Definition

A table showing how much of a good consumers will be willing and able to buy at different prices.

Textbook page 49, the right-hand side of Figure 5.1.

The schedule comes first and the curve is drawn from it. That order matters more than it sounds, because a free-response question may hand you a table and ask for a graph, or hand you a graph and ask for numbers. They are the same information in two forms.

Your framework names it. The market demand curve (schedule), it says, is built by adding up individual demand curves (schedules) — so expect to be handed tables, not only graphs.

Watch out. A schedule is not a prediction of what will happen. It is a list of what buyers would do at each price, only one of which will turn out to be the actual price.

Textbook

Coffee beans. $2.00 → 7.1 billion pounds; $1.00 → 10.0; $0.50 → 14.2. Seven rows, one curve.

Numeric

Two rows are enough. Your textbook’s own free-response answer uses just two: $4 → 10 and $2 → 14. Any two rows showing price and quantity moving in opposite directions will earn the mark.

Illustrative

A class auction. Ask twenty classmates the most they would pay for a chocolate bar, then count how many would buy at $5, $4, $3. That count is a demand schedule.

How it connects. The demand curve (entry 7) is this table drawn. A change in demand (entry 13) is a whole new column of quantities.

Check yourself

5.1 A table shows $3 → 40 units and $5 → 55 units. Is this a demand schedule?

Show answer

No — not for demand. Quantity rises as price rises, which is the wrong direction. That is a supply schedule, which you meet in Module 6.

5.2 How many rows does a free-response answer need?

Show answer

Two, as long as they show the negative relationship. Your textbook’s printed rubric awards one mark for the labelled table and one for the values having the right direction.

06

Quantity demanded

★ Exam
Definition

The actual amount of a good or service consumers are willing and able to buy at some specific price.

Textbook page 49.

Read the last four words again. Quantity demanded is attached to one price. It is a number. It is a single point on the curve, or a single row of the schedule.

Demand, by contrast, is the whole relationship — the entire curve, every price at once. Your textbook never uses the two words loosely and neither should you. When a question says “quantity demanded rises”, it is telling you that you moved to a different point on the same curve.

Watch out. “Demand rose” and “quantity demanded rose” are not two ways of saying the same thing. They have different causes and they look completely different on a diagram. Entry 12 and entry 13 are the two cases; get those right and this topic is finished.

Textbook

One row. At $1.25 a pound, quantity demanded is 8.9 billion pounds. At $1.00 it is 10.0 billion. Two prices, two quantities demanded, one demand.

Numeric

Point A. On the diagram above, point A is price $1.50 and quantity demanded 8.1 billion pounds. The point is the quantity demanded; the curve through it is the demand.

Illustrative

Willing and able. Wanting a sports car is not demand. Wanting one and having the money at that price is.

How it connects. The law of demand (entry 8) is a statement about how this number responds to price. A movement along the curve (entry 12) is a change in it. “Willing and able” is incentives and constraints (entry 4) in three words.

Check yourself

6.1 Is “10 billion pounds” a demand or a quantity demanded?

Show answer

A quantity demanded — it is one number, and it only means anything once you say which price it goes with. The demand is the whole schedule.

6.2 Someone says “I demand a Ferrari.” Is that demand in the economic sense?

Show answer

Only if they are able to buy one at the going price. Demand requires willingness and ability together.

07

Demand curve

★ Exam
Definition

A graphical representation of the demand schedule. It shows the relationship between quantity demanded and price.

Textbook page 49, Figure 5.1.

Three drawing rules, and the examiner checks all three before looking at your economics:

  • Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal. Always. Even though price is what causes the change, it goes on the vertical axis by convention, and the convention is worth a mark.
  • Label both axes with what they measure.
  • Label the curve D, or D₁ if there will be a second one.

Four of the six marks in your textbook’s first free-response question are for exactly these conventions. Not for the economics — for the labels.

Watch out. A demand curve does not have to be straight. Your textbook’s coffee curve is not. A straight line is fine in an answer as long as it slopes downward and is labelled.

Textbook

Figure 5.1. Seven points from the schedule, joined. Downward sloping, labelled D.

Numeric

Plotting one point. The row $1.50 / 8.1 becomes the point 8.1 across, $1.50 up. That is point A in the diagram at the top of this guide.

Illustrative

Marking scheme. Axes labelled, negatively sloped curve labelled D, points matching your table, second curve labelled and to the right. Write that checklist on the inside cover of your notebook.

How it connects. It is the demand schedule (entry 5) drawn. Its downward slope is the law of demand (entry 8). The one you draw in a market diagram is the market demand curve (entry 20), and your framework also calls it a marginal benefit curve (entry 11). It is element 1 of the supply and demand model (entry 2).

Check yourself

7.1 Which variable goes on the vertical axis, and why is it worth a mark?

Show answer

Price. It is worth a mark because the rubric awards one for “Price on the vertical axis and Quantity on the horizontal axis” before it awards anything for the curve itself.

7.2 You draw a perfect curve and forget to label it. How many of the six marks are at risk?

Show answer

At least one, for “negatively sloped curve labelled Demand or D”, and a second if the shifted curve is also unlabelled. Labelling costs four seconds.

08

Law of demand

★ Exam
Definition

A higher price for a good or service, all other things being equal, leads people to demand a smaller quantity of it.

Textbook page 50.

Your framework states it this way: a change in the own-price causes a change in quantity demanded in the opposite direction and a movement along the demand curve. Those are the words to use: own-price, opposite direction, movement along.

Your course also asks why it holds, and entries 9 to 11 give the two accepted answers. Restating the law is not one of them.

The clause that does the work is all other things being equal. Without it the law is simply false, and your textbook proves it on the same page: coffee cost more in 2006 than in 2002 and the world drank more of it. Other things were not equal — there were more people, and lattes had caught on.

Watch out. Real-world counter-examples almost never break the law. They break the ceteris paribus clause. When you see price and quantity moving the same way, your first thought should be “something else moved”, not “the law failed”.

Textbook

Two cups to one. Some people who drink two cups a day at $1 a pound cut to one cup at $2. Others switch to tea. That is the law working, one household at a time.

Numeric

The schedule proves it. Price $0.50 → 14.2 billion pounds; price $2.00 → 7.1 billion. Price up fourfold, quantity demanded down by half.

Illustrative

London’s congestion charge. Charging £8 to drive into central London cut traffic about 10 per cent within three years. Raise the price of driving, get less driving.

How it connects. It is the reason the demand curve (entry 7) slopes downward, and it is what a movement along the curve (entry 12) illustrates. The substitution effect, the income effect and diminishing marginal utility (entries 9 to 11) explain it, and prices as incentives (entry 4) are what it measures. It is a statement about quantity demanded (entry 6), never about demand.

Check yourself

8.1 State the law of demand in your framework’s own words.

Show answer

A change in the own-price causes a change in quantity demanded in the opposite direction — a movement along a downward-sloping demand curve. Use “quantity demanded”, not “demand”.

8.2 Petrol got more expensive last year and people bought more of it. Has the law been broken?

Show answer

Almost certainly not. Something else changed — incomes rose, or the population grew, or more people bought cars. The curve shifted right by more than the price rise moved you up along it.

09

Substitution effect

★ ⚑
Definition

The change in the quantity of a good demanded as the consumer substitutes the good that has become relatively cheaper for the good that has become relatively more expensive.

Not in this module. Your textbook defines it in Module 46, page 458. Read pages 458–459 now — they need nothing from the modules in between.

⚑ Not in Module 5 — learn it from here. Your course requires you to explain why the demand curve slopes downward, not just that it does. Module 5 does not explain it; the explanation is 400 pages on.

When coffee’s price rises, coffee becomes expensive relative to tea. Some drinkers switch. That switch, on its own, lowers the quantity of coffee demanded. That is the substitution effect.

Your textbook has already shown you this without naming it: on page 50, some coffee drinkers “drink tea instead if the price doubles”. It works for every good, because every good has something that can partly replace it — and for most goods, your textbook says, it is the whole reason the demand curve slopes down.

Watch out. The substitution effect is not the same as substitutes (entry 14). Substitutes are a shift factor: tea’s price changes and coffee’s curve shifts. The substitution effect is a movement: coffee’s own price changes and you slide along coffee’s curve. Same switching, opposite side of the line.

Textbook

Orange juice and lemonade. Module 46: Clare buys more lemonade and less orange juice when orange juice gets dearer. It is a tiny share of her spending, so this is all substitution effect.

Numeric

Coffee doubles. On your textbook’s schedule, coffee going from $1.00 to $2.00 a pound cuts quantity demanded from 10.0 to 7.1 billion pounds — a fall of 2.9 billion. Drinkers switching to tea are part of that fall.

Illustrative

Streaming. One service raises its monthly price and some subscribers move to a rival that is now relatively cheaper. The first service slides up along its demand curve.

How it connects. It explains the law of demand (entry 8), it happens as a movement along the curve (entry 12), and it must not be confused with substitutes (entry 14). The income effect (entry 10) is the second part of the same price change, and diminishing marginal utility (entry 11) is the other accepted explanation.

Check yourself

9.1 The price of pens rises and students buy more pencils and fewer pens. On the pen diagram, is that a movement or a shift, and what is it called?

Show answer

A movement up along the pen demand curve — pens’ own price rose. The switch to pencils is the substitution effect. (On the pencil diagram it would be a shift, because a substitute got dearer.)

9.2 Why does the substitution effect make a demand curve slope down even for a good that takes a tiny share of your income?

Show answer

Because it depends only on relative prices, not on how much you spend. Any price rise makes the good dearer compared with its alternatives, so some buyers switch away, however small the purchase.

10

Income effect

★ ⚑
Definition

The change in the quantity of a good demanded that results from a change in the consumer’s purchasing power when the price of the good changes.

Not in this module. Your textbook defines it in Module 46, page 459.

⚑ Not in Module 5 — learn it from here. It is the second half of your framework’s explanation of the downward slope, and Module 5 never mentions it. Learn it together with entry 9.

A price rise makes you poorer without touching your pay. If rent takes half your income and rents rise, the same income now buys less of everything. That loss of purchasing power changes how much of the good you buy: the income effect.

It only matters for goods that take a big share of the budget — housing, food. For a pencil it is too small to notice. And its direction depends on the good. For a normal good, poorer means buy less, which reinforces the substitution effect. For an inferior good, poorer means buy more, which works against it — but the substitution effect is normally larger, so the curve still slopes down.

Watch out. The income effect is caused by a change in the good’s own price. Nobody’s income changed. If a question says incomes actually rose or fell, that is a determinant and the curve shifts (entries 16 and 17). If a price changed and people became effectively poorer, it is the income effect and you move along the curve.

Textbook

Delia’s rent. Module 46: rents rise sharply, rent is a large share of her income, and she moves to a smaller flat. Housing is a normal good, so both effects push the same way.

Numeric

What a rent rise takes away. Income $2,000 a month, rent $1,000. Rent rises to $1,200, so she has $800 a month left for everything else instead of $1,000 — a fifth less, and her pay never changed.

Illustrative

Cafeteria meals. Module 46 again: the semester meal plan gets dearer, students are left poorer, and they eat more cafeteria meals in place of restaurant meals. Cafeteria meals behave as an inferior good here, so the income effect runs backwards.

How it connects. It is the second half of the same own-price change as the substitution effect (entry 9), and together they explain the law of demand (entry 8). Its direction depends on whether the good is normal (entry 16) or inferior (entry 17). It is a movement along the curve (entry 12), never a shift. Diminishing marginal utility (entry 11) is the alternative explanation of the slope.

Check yourself

10.1 Petrol takes a large share of a delivery driver’s budget, and its price rises. Name both effects and say whether they push the quantity of petrol demanded the same way. (Petrol is a normal good.)

Show answer

The substitution effect lowers it — petrol is dearer relative to everything else. The income effect lowers it too — the driver is effectively poorer and petrol is normal. Same direction: they reinforce each other.

10.2 The price of an inferior good rises. Which way does the income effect push, and why does the demand curve still slope down?

Show answer

It pushes quantity demanded up: the buyer is poorer, and poorer people buy more of an inferior good. The curve still slopes down because the substitution effect, which pushes quantity down, is normally the larger of the two.

11

Diminishing marginal utility — the marginal benefit curve

★ ⚑
Definition

Each successive unit of a good consumed adds less to total utility than the unit before. Because of it, the demand curve is also a marginal benefit curve: its height at any quantity is what that unit is worth to the buyer.

Not in this module. You met diminishing marginal utility in Module 51, page 513; the link to demand is on page 521.

⚑ Not in Module 5 — learn it from here. Your framework gives a second explanation of the downward slope and calls the demand curve a “marginal benefit” curve. Module 5 does neither.

Your first slice of pizza is worth a lot to you; your fourth, much less. Because each extra unit is worth less, you will only buy more units if the price comes down to what the extra one is worth. That is a downward-sloping demand curve.

So the height of the demand curve at any quantity is the marginal benefit of that unit — what its buyer would pay for it. In Module 51 you met the same idea from the other side: raise a good’s price and its marginal utility per dollar falls, so you buy less of it. That is the law of demand again.

Watch out. Either explanation earns the mark: the substitution and income effects, or diminishing marginal utility, or both. What does not earn it is restating the law — “because people buy less when the price is higher” explains nothing.

Textbook

Cassie’s clams. Module 51: her marginal utility falls 15, 13, 11, 9, 7… with each clam. Each extra clam is worth less to her than the one before, so it takes a lower price to make the next one worth buying.

Numeric

Reading marginal benefit off the curve. A demand curve passes through $5 at 2 cups and $3 at 4 cups. The buyer of the 4th cup values it at $3 — its marginal benefit. The 2nd cup was worth $5.

Illustrative

An all-you-can-eat buffet. The next plate costs nothing, and you still stop. You stop where the next plate’s marginal benefit has fallen to zero.

How it connects. It explains the law of demand (entry 8) alongside the substitution and income effects (entries 9 and 10), and it is why the demand curve (entry 7) can be read as a marginal benefit curve.

Check yourself

11.1 Explain why a demand curve slopes downward using diminishing marginal utility.

Show answer

Each additional unit adds less to a buyer’s satisfaction than the one before. A buyer only buys a unit if it is worth at least the price, so to buy more units — each worth less — the price has to fall. Lower price, larger quantity demanded.

11.2 At 6 units the demand curve’s height is $4. What is the marginal benefit of the 6th unit, and is it bought at a price of $5?

Show answer

Its marginal benefit is $4. At $5 it is not bought — it is worth less than it costs — so quantity demanded at $5 is fewer than 6.

12

Movement along the demand curve

★ Exam
Definition

A change in the quantity demanded of a good that is the result of a change in that good’s own price.

Textbook page 51, Figure 5.3, the arrow from A to B.

The curve does not move. You move, to a different point on the same curve. Nothing about buyers’ tastes, incomes or numbers has changed — the only thing that changed is the price tag, and the curve already told you what would happen at that price.

The cause is always the same and there is only one of it: the good’s own price. If a question names a price, check whose good it belongs to before you decide.

Watch out. The price of a related good is not an own price. “The price of tea rises, so people buy more coffee” is a shift of the coffee curve, because the price that moved was tea’s. Always ask: whose price moved, and whose curve am I drawing?

Textbook

A to B. Coffee falls from $1.50 to $1.00 a pound and quantity demanded rises from 8.1 to 10.0 billion pounds. Same curve, lower point.

Numeric

Read it off the schedule. Both numbers are in the left column of the table above. A movement never needs a new column — that is the test.

Illustrative

A half-price offer. A supermarket cuts the price of its own bread and sells three times as much. Its own price moved: movement along.

How it connects. It is the law of demand (entry 8) in action, it changes the quantity demanded (entry 6), and it is defined against a change in demand (entry 13). The substitution and income effects (entries 9 and 10) both happen along it; a change in the price of a substitute (entry 14) never does.

Check yourself

12.1 Does the curve move during a movement along the curve?

Show answer

No. That is the whole point of the name. The curve stays exactly where it is and you slide to a different point on it.

12.2 The price of petrol rises sharply and commuters start carpooling. Movement or shift?

Show answer

Movement along the demand curve for petrol. Petrol’s own price rose, so quantity demanded fell. Carpooling is how they buy less, not a separate cause.

13

Change in demand — a shift

★ Exam
Definition

A shift of the demand curve, which changes the quantity demanded at any given price.

Textbook pages 51–52, Figures 5.2 and 5.3, the arrow from A to C.

Read the definition’s last four words carefully: at any given price. That phrase is the test. If more would be bought at $2.00 and at $1.50 and at $1.00 — at every price — the curve has moved. If more is bought only because the price came down, it has not.

Directions, and the words to use: an increase in demand is a shift to the right; a decrease in demand is a shift to the left. Never say “the curve moved up” — on a diagram, right and up look the same for a downward-sloping curve, and only one of the two descriptions is unambiguous.

Watch out. When an economist says “demand for coffee increased”, they always mean the curve shifted. They never mean people bought more because it got cheaper. Your textbook says this in so many words on page 52, and examiners write questions on the assumption that you know it.

Textbook

Coffee, 2002 to 2006. At $1.00 the quantity demanded went from 10.0 to 12.0 billion pounds; at $1.25, from 8.9 to 10.7. Every row rose, so the curve moved right.

Numeric

A to C. Hold the price at $1.50 and the quantity demanded goes from 8.1 billion on D₁ to 9.7 billion on D₂. The price never changed, so this cannot be a movement.

Illustrative

Hats. After 1945 men stopped wearing them. The demand curve for hats shifted left — not because hats got dearer, but because nobody wanted one.

How it connects. It is the opposite case to a movement along (entry 12), it is caused by the five factors in entries 14 to 18 — substitutes, complements, normal and inferior goods, and the other three — and it is what those factors do to the market demand curve (entry 20). On a table it is a whole new column of the demand schedule (entry 5).

Check yourself

13.1 What is the four-word test for a shift?

Show answer

“At any given price.” If the quantity demanded changes even though the price did not, the curve has shifted.

13.2 Why is “the demand curve shifted up” a risky thing to write?

Show answer

Because for a downward-sloping curve, moving up and moving left look similar but mean opposite things. Say right for an increase and left for a decrease and you can never be misread.

14

Substitutes

★ Exam
Definition

Two goods are substitutes if a rise in the price of one leads to an increase in the demand for the other.

Textbook page 52. This is the first of the five shift factors: prices of related goods.

Substitutes do a similar job, so buyers swap between them. Tea and coffee. Concerts and plays. Train tickets and flights. Margarine and butter.

The chain has two links and you should say both out loud: the price of tea rises, so some tea drinkers switch, so the demand for coffee increases — the coffee curve shifts right. The price of coffee never moved.

Watch out. Substitutes are not the substitution effect. Here another good’s price moves and this good’s curve shifts. In the substitution effect, this good’s own price moves and you slide along its curve. Same switching, opposite answers — so ask whose price moved.

Textbook

Tea and coffee. A rise in the price of tea induces some tea drinkers to drink coffee instead, shifting demand for coffee to the right.

Numeric

Butter and margarine. Butter gets cheaper. Some margarine buyers switch to butter, so the demand for margarine falls — a leftward shift. This is your textbook’s multiple-choice question 2.

Illustrative

Buses and cars. Cities subsidise buses to reduce the demand for driving. Cheaper substitute, leftward shift in the demand for car trips.

How it connects. It causes a change in demand (entry 13), never a movement (entry 12), and it is the mirror image of complements (entry 15). It is one of the five factors that shift demand. Do not confuse it with the substitution effect (entry 9). In Topic 2.5 a number — the cross-price elasticity — will tell you which relationship two goods are in.

Check yourself

14.1 The price of Pepsi falls. What happens to the demand for Coca-Cola?

Show answer

It decreases — the curve shifts left. Some Coke buyers switch to the now-cheaper substitute.

14.2 Does a change in the price of a substitute move you along the curve?

Show answer

No. It is not this good’s own price, so the whole curve shifts. Only the good’s own price produces a movement.

15

Complements

★ Exam
Definition

Two goods are complements if a rise in the price of one leads to a decrease in the demand for the other.

Textbook page 52.

Complements are consumed together. Computers and software. Cars and petrol. Cappuccinos and croissants. If one becomes dearer you buy less of the pair, so you want less of both.

Same mechanism as substitutes, opposite sign. Compare the two definitions word for word: they differ only in “increase” against “decrease”. Learning them apart is how students end up guessing.

Watch out. Decide which relationship you are in before you pick a direction. Ask: if this other good got more expensive, would I want more of my good or less? More → substitutes. Less → complements.

Textbook

Cappuccinos and croissants. Starbucks raised cappuccino prices in October 2006, which probably shifted the demand curve for croissants to the left.

Numeric

Multiple-choice 1. Which decrease would increase demand for a normal good? A decrease in the price of a complement. Cheaper petrol, more demand for large cars.

Illustrative

Parking taxes. Cities tax city-centre parking to discourage driving. Parking is a complement to a car trip; dearer parking, leftward shift in demand for car trips.

How it connects. The mirror of substitutes (entry 14). Both are the first of the five shift factors and both cause a change in demand (entry 13).

Check yourself

15.1 Petrol gets much more expensive. What happens to the demand curve for large cars?

Show answer

It shifts left. Petrol and cars are complements, so a rise in the price of one decreases the demand for the other.

15.2 Printers and ink cartridges: substitutes or complements? What happens to printer demand if cartridges get cheaper?

Show answer

Complements — you cannot use one without the other. Cheaper cartridges increase the demand for printers, shifting that curve right.

16

Normal goods

★ Exam
Definition

A good whose demand increases when consumer income rises — the normal case.

Textbook page 52. This is the second shift factor: changes in income.

Your framework meets income twice. Once as a constraint (entry 4): what you can buy is limited by what you have. And once as a way of sorting goods: in Topic 2.5 you will measure how demand responds to income and use the answer to decide whether a good is normal or inferior. Put the two together: when income rises the constraint loosens, and for a normal good more is demanded at every price.

Most goods are normal. Incomes rise, people buy more restaurant meals, more plane tickets, more of almost everything.

Watch out. An income change shifts the curve. It is not a price change, so it never moves you along. Students who have just learned “more money means you can afford it at that price” often reach for a movement; resist it.

Textbook

Disney World. A family’s income rises, so it is more likely to take the summer trip — and so more likely to buy plane tickets.

Numeric

Casual dining. When American incomes rise, spending at Applebee’s and Olive Garden rises. Casual dining is a normal good.

Illustrative

The framework’s own lesson. The suggested classroom activity for this topic runs an auction, then hands students fake money to raise their incomes and asks them to spot the shift. Loosen the income constraint and more is bid at every price.

How it connects. It is one case of an income change; inferior goods (entry 17) are the other. It produces a change in demand (entry 13). For a normal good, the income effect (entry 10) reinforces the substitution effect, and income itself is one of the constraints of entry 4.

Check yourself

16.1 Incomes in a region fall after its largest employer closes. What happens to the demand for air travel, a normal good?

Show answer

It decreases — the curve shifts left. For a normal good, income and demand move in the same direction.

16.2 Your framework calls income a constraint. How can it also shift the demand curve?

Show answer

A constraint limits what you are able to buy. When income rises the limit loosens, so at every price you can buy more — and for a normal good you do. More demanded at every price is a shift to the right.

17

Inferior goods

★ Exam
Definition

A good whose demand decreases when consumer income rises.

Textbook page 53.

When people can afford better, they stop buying the cheap alternative and trade up. So a rise in income shifts the demand curve for an inferior good to the left, and — the part students forget — a fall in income shifts it to the right.

That last sentence is why inferior goods are worth knowing. When incomes fall, demand for some goods goes up. Topic 2.5 gives you a number for it: a negative income elasticity is what makes a good inferior.

Watch out. “Inferior” is not a judgement about quality. It describes a relationship with income and nothing else. A bus ride is not a bad thing; it is just what people buy less of when they can afford taxis.

Textbook

Bus rides against taxi rides. The classic pair. As income rises, people switch from the bus to the taxi.

Numeric

Fast food. Your textbook’s business-press example: as incomes rise, Americans eat out more at casual-dining restaurants and somewhat less at McDonald’s and KFC. Casual dining normal, fast food inferior.

Illustrative

Own-brand groceries. Sales rise in a downturn. Falling income, rightward shift — the inferior case run backwards.

How it connects. The other case of an income change from normal goods (entry 16). Both are a change in demand (entry 13): they shift the curve, neither moves you along it. It is also why the income effect (entry 10) runs backwards for an inferior good.

Check yourself

17.1 An increase in income leads to a decrease in demand. What kind of good is it?

Show answer

Inferior. This is your textbook’s multiple-choice question 3, and the trap answer is “abnormal”, which is not a term in economics.

17.2 Incomes fall. What happens to the demand curve for instant noodles?

Show answer

It shifts right — demand increases. Instant noodles behave as an inferior good, so income and demand move in opposite directions.

18

Tastes, expectations and the number of consumers

★ Exam
Definition

The remaining three of the five factors that shift demand: changes in tastes, changes in expectations, and changes in the number of consumers.

Textbook pages 53–55, summarised in Table 5.1 on page 56.

Tastes. Fashions, beliefs, health reports. Tastes turn towards a good and the curve shifts right; away and it shifts left. Economists have nothing to say about why tastes change, only about what a change does.

Expectations. This one runs backwards from intuition, so learn it as a rule rather than by feel. If you expect the price to rise later, you buy now: demand today increases. If you expect the price to fall later, you wait: demand today decreases. Expected future income works the same way — expecting a raise makes you spend today.

Number of consumers. More buyers, more demanded at every price. This is the factor your textbook uses to explain the 2002–2006 coffee story, alongside tastes.

Watch out. Expectations are about today’s curve. A question that says “consumers expect prices to fall next month” is asking what happens to demand today, and the answer is that it falls. Reading it as a question about next month gets it backwards.

Textbook

Hats. After the Second World War, returning soldiers adopted an informal style and President Eisenhower often went hatless. Tastes changed; the curve shifted left.

Numeric

Starbucks, October 2006. Shoppers who knew a price rise was coming on 6 October bought beans before it. Expected rise → demand up today.

Illustrative

A baby boom. More infants means more buyers of nappies at every price — a rightward shift. This is your textbook’s multiple-choice question 5.

How it connects. These three, plus substitutes and complements (entries 14 and 15) and income (entries 16 and 17), are the complete list of what causes a change in demand (entry 13).

Check yourself

18.1 Consumers expect the price of a good to fall in the coming months. What happens to demand today?

Show answer

Demand decreases today — the curve shifts left, because buyers wait. Note the question asks about demand, not quantity demanded: the price has not changed yet.

18.2 A new report says apples prevent illness. Which factor is this, and which way does the curve move?

Show answer

Tastes, and the curve shifts right. This is your textbook’s second free-response question.

19

Individual demand curve

★ Exam
Definition

The relationship between quantity demanded and price for one consumer.

Textbook page 55, panels (a) and (b) of Figure 5.5.

Your textbook introduces one buyer at a time so that the market curve can be built in front of you. Darla will buy 20 pounds of coffee a year at $2; Dino will buy 10.

Your framework requires it by name: the market demand curve is derived from the summation of individual demand curves. So you need both curves and the step between them, which is entry 20.

Watch out. If a question says “the demand curve” with no qualifier, it means the market demand curve. Your textbook says the same: most of the time, when economists say demand curve, that is what they mean.

Textbook

Darla. DDarla shows how many pounds of coffee beans she will buy per year at any given price.

Numeric

At $2 a pound. Darla 20 pounds, Dino 10 pounds. Two individual curves, two numbers.

Illustrative

Your own. How many coffees would you buy in a week at $6, at $4, at $2? That is your individual demand curve, and it slopes downward for the same reason everyone’s does.

How it connects. Add all of them together horizontally and you get the market demand curve (entry 20), which is the one that is examined.

Check yourself

19.1 Darla’s income rises and she now wants more coffee at every price. Dino’s situation is unchanged. Which curves shift?

Show answer

Darla’s individual curve shifts right, and so does the market curve, because it is the sum. Dino’s does not move. A change for one buyer is enough to shift the market curve.

19.2 Does an individual demand curve obey the law of demand?

Show answer

Yes — it slopes downward for one buyer just as it does for the market. That is why adding them up produces a downward-sloping market curve.

20

Market demand curve

★ Exam
Definition

Shows how the combined quantity demanded by all consumers depends on the market price. It is the horizontal sum of the individual demand curves.

Textbook page 55, panel (c) of Figure 5.5.

“Horizontal sum” means: pick a price, add up the quantities, plot the total. Add quantities, never prices. Everyone in the market faces the same price — that is what makes it a market — so the price is the thing you hold fixed while you add.

Your framework asks for exactly this step: the market demand curve (schedule) is derived from the summation of individual demand curves (schedules). Expect a table of two or three buyers and a request for the market column — add across each row.

Watch out. More consumers entering the market shifts the market curve right without changing anybody’s individual curve at all. That is how population growth raises demand: not by making existing buyers want more, but by adding buyers to the sum.

Textbook

Darla and Dino. At $2 a pound, 20 + 10 = 30 pounds. If they are the only two buyers, that point is on the market demand curve.

Numeric

At $1 a pound. Darla 30, Dino 20, market 50. Lower price, larger sum — the market curve slopes downward because both individual curves do.

Illustrative

A third buyer. Add one more consumer who buys 5 pounds at $2 and the market quantity at $2 becomes 35. The curve shifts right, and neither Darla nor Dino changed their mind.

How it connects. It is the horizontal sum of individual demand curves (entry 19). It is what changes in demand (entry 13) shift, and it is what the whole supply and demand model (entry 2) uses. Drawn, it is a demand curve (entry 7) like any other.

Check yourself

20.1 At $3, Ana would buy 4 units and Ben 7. What is the market quantity demanded at $3?

Show answer

11 units. Hold the price fixed and add the quantities: 4 + 7. You never add the prices.

20.2 Fifty new buyers move into the town. Which curve moves, the individual or the market one?

Show answer

The market curve, and it shifts right. Every existing buyer’s individual curve is exactly where it was — there are simply more curves in the sum.

  3. Graph with Price on the vertical axis and Quantity on the horizontal axis

The six things to get right from Module 5

  1. Own price → movement. Anything else → shift. If you remember one sentence from this module, that is the sentence.
  2. The price of a related good is not an own price. Write down whose price moved and whose curve you are drawing, every time.
  3. Say “right” and “left”, never “up” and “down”, when describing a shift.
  4. Expectations run backwards. Expect a fall, wait, demand falls today.
  5. Label the axes and label every curve. Four of the six marks in the free-response question are for that alone.
  6. Explain the slope, do not restate the law. The substitution and income effects, or diminishing marginal utility. “People buy less when it costs more” is the law, not the reason for it.

One more thing, for the rest of this unit. The demand curve you built here comes straight back three times: elasticity measures how far quantity demanded moves along it; consumer surplus is the area between it and the price, which only makes sense because it is a marginal benefit curve; and every change in equilibrium starts with one of the shifts you sorted above. Learn it properly once and you will be paid for it all unit.
